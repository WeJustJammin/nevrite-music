# Feature: Voluntary Takedown

> **Number**: 12.05.01
> **Parent**: [Catalog Lifecycle After Release](./12.05-catalog-lifecycle-after-release-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Removing a live release — from one store, or from everywhere — because the artist wants it gone.
Reasons are ordinary and legitimate: a mistake shipped, a label deal supersedes the self-release,
a track is being re-recorded, a collaboration ended badly, or they simply changed their mind about
a record with their name on it.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Initiates the takedown, per store or everywhere, and sees plainly what they lose and what they cannot get back — streams, playlist positions, the release date, and (on some stores) the artist-page placement. Sees per-store removal progress, which is as asynchronous and silent as delivery was. |
| Producer | Read-only | Told that a record they made is coming down. No say — it is the rights holder's record. But being told matters: the Producer's credits (02) reference a release that will stop existing publicly, and finding that out by accident, months later, is exactly the kind of erasure the credits domain exists to prevent. |
| Operator | None | Not visible. |
| Fan | None | Not visible from the platform. The Fan experiences it as the record silently vanishing from their library — the most user-hostile moment in the domain, and one the platform currently chooses not to explain. See parent Q-03. |

## Behavior

Takedown is an ERN variant from the same generator (12.02.01), delivered per store, and it is as
asynchronous, partial and silent as the original delivery: pulled from Spotify in a day, still up
on Beatport a week later, and no partner tells you when it is really gone.

The critical property is **asymmetry**. Delivery is reversible; takedown mostly is not:

| What happens on takedown | Recoverable? |
|---|---|
| Streams and play counts | ❌ Gone — a re-delivered release starts at zero |
| Playlist positions (editorial and user) | ❌ Gone — and editorial will not re-add |
| Original release date | ❌ Redelivery is a new release with a new date |
| Save/library entries | ❌ Fans lose it silently |
| ISRC / UPC | ✅ Retained — the identifiers survive (12.07) |
| Credits and splits | ✅ Retained — provenance is ours, not the store's |

That last pair is the whole argument for D-10. On a conventional distributor, taking down a
release erases most of its existence. On WeJammin the *record of the work* survives the record's
removal from stores, because the platform owns the chain of title rather than renting it from a
DSP. **Provenance outliving distribution is the clearest demonstration of the thesis anywhere in
the domain** — the store forgets, the credits do not.

Because the loss is mostly irreversible, the confirmation must be specific about what is being
destroyed, not a generic "are you sure?".

### Happy Path

1. Musician selects a live release and chooses takedown, per store or everywhere.
2. Platform states specifically what will be lost, naming the numbers (streams, playlist count).
3. Musician confirms.
4. Takedown ERNs are generated per store and delivered.
5. Store rows move to `Taking down`, then `Taken down` as confirmations arrive — or sit in
   `Unknown` where a store never confirms.
6. The release's provenance record persists in full; only its store presence ends.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Store never confirms removal | The delivery silence problem in reverse | "Beatport hasn't confirmed in 8 days. It may still be up — we're chasing." |
| Release is taken down, then re-delivered | **A new release**: new date, zero streams, no playlists. Same ISRC | "This won't restore what you had. It's a new release with the same recordings." |
| Track is on a live compilation by someone else | Their release is unaffected — different UPC, same recording | "Track 3 stays live on {compilation}. That's a different release." |
| Co-owner objects to the takedown | A rights decision, not a distribution one | Routes to 09/24. `[PENDING — Step 5]` on whether it blocks |
| Takedown during a pre-save window | Fans authorised something that will not happen | Orphaned pre-saves (12.04.03); the grant must still be dropped |
| Fans bought it (domain 20) | Purchase and store presence are different things | `[PENDING — Step 5]` — a fan who paid should not lose what they bought |
| Takedown mid-delivery to another store | The in-flight delivery must be cancelled, not landed then removed | Transparent, but a real race |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Live | Normal | Takedown available |
| Confirming | Takedown requested | Specific loss statement, named numbers, not a generic prompt |
| Taking down | ERNs sent | Per-store progress |
| Taken down | All stores confirmed | "Gone from {N} stores" + the provenance record still intact and visible |
| Partial | Some stores confirmed | Per-store breakdown; the honest state |
| Unknown | Store never confirmed | "We can't tell whether Beatport removed it" |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Takedown is the inverse of delivery and can be modelled symmetrically | It is the same generator, the same transport, the same per-store status — the symmetry is genuinely tempting | ❌ REJECTED — the **state is symmetric; the consequences are not.** Delivery creates something recoverable; takedown destroys streams, playlist positions and the release date permanently. A redelivered release is a *new* release that happens to contain the same recordings. Treating them as inverse operations produces a UI where an artist undoes a fortnight of playlist work with a click that looks like the one that made it. The confirmation must name what dies. |
| DT-02 | Takedown erases the release | The user's mental model, and what happens on every competitor | ❌ REJECTED, and the rejection is the domain's best single demonstration of D-10. Takedown removes the release from **stores**; the credits, splits, identifiers and attestations are WeJammin's, not the DSP's, and they persist. A session player's credit on a withdrawn record is still a fact about their career — per personas.md, "the fact of the work accretes to their identity", and a commercial decision by a rights holder years later should not un-play their bass part. On a conventional distributor that credit evaporates with the store page. **Provenance outliving distribution is the thesis made visible in the least glamorous corner of the product**, and it is worth stating to the user at exactly this moment, because it is when they would expect to lose everything. |
| DT-03 | The artist can always take down their own release | It is their record; the button is obviously theirs | ⚠️ CONTESTED — a release is routinely **co-owned** (a band is an entity per personas.md; a co-writer holds a share). One member taking down the band's record is a governance question, not a distribution one, and this feature is where an unresolved org-permission model in 01/09 would first do real damage. Contrast 12.05.03: there, removal is initiated by someone else *against* the artist. Between them sits the awkward middle — removal by a co-owner — which neither feature currently owns. Routed to 09/01. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | Confirmation names the specific, irreversible losses with real numbers | Takedown is not the inverse of delivery (DT-01) |
| D-02 | Provenance survives takedown, and we say so at the moment of removal | Credits are ours, not the store's — the thesis made concrete (DT-02) |
| D-03 | Redelivery after takedown is presented as a **new release**, never a restore | It is one, and pretending otherwise sets up a worse surprise |
