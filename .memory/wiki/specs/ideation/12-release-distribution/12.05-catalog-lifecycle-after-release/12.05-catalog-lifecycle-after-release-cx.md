## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Involuntary Takedown ↔ Update & Redelivery

**Relationship**: Suspension is a state on the release that freezes editing. The intent is obvious — an artist should not be able to metadata-edit their way out of a copyright claim. The consequence is less obvious and cuts the other way: **the fix that would resolve the claim is often an update.** A missed credit that triggered an ownership dispute is resolved by adding the credit. A sample dispute is resolved by attaching the clearance. Blocking all updates during suspension blocks the resolution along with the evasion.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: sees "suspended" and "cannot edit" simultaneously, which reads as a trap. If the route out requires an edit they cannot make, the product has locked them in a room and told them the key is inside.
- **Producer**: frequently holds the resolving fact (the clearance, the true credit) and has `Config` on updates — so the person who can fix it is blocked by a state they had no part in.
- **Operator / Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The **suspension state** is owned by 12.05.03 and read by 12.05.02 as a hard gate. Single writer. The conflict is not technical but procedural — one feature's safety mechanism is another's blocked exit.
2. **Trigger chain**: Claim → suspension → updates frozen. The unresolved question is whether a **claim-resolving** update is exempt (CX-04). A blanket freeze is simple, defensible and occasionally traps an innocent artist; a carve-out is correct and requires classifying an update against a claim, which is hard and gameable.
3. **Permission intersection**: The sharpest in the sub-domain. The Musician's `Full` on updates is silently overridden to `None` by a state set by someone else. Downstream spec writers must treat suspension as a permission-layer override, not a UI disable — a disabled button that a determined user can bypass via API would be a real hole here.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Suspension must notify the artist and, per 12.05.03's Role Lens, the Producer — who may hold the answer. Notifying only the release owner discards the platform's best evidence, which is the specific mistake DT-02 in 12.05.03 says WeJammin is uniquely positioned not to make.
5. **State transition conflict**: An update **in flight** when suspension lands. The update must be cancelled, not landed — but it may already be at the partner, in which case the release is suspended here and updated there. `[PENDING — Step 5]`

### CX-02: Involuntary Takedown ↔ Voluntary Takedown

**Relationship**: Suspension removes the artist's ability to take down their own release. This looks strange — why stop someone withdrawing a record that is already being withheld? — but it is correct: a voluntary takedown during a dispute could destroy the evidence and the subject of the claim at once, and could be used to duck a resolution that would otherwise go on the record. The artist regains control when the suspension lifts.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the only place in domain 12 where they cannot act on their own release *at all* — read-only on both removal paths simultaneously. That is a severe state and it must be explained rather than merely enforced.
- **Producer**: read-only either way; no change.
- **Operator / Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Both write to the same store-presence state through the same generator (12.02.01), which is why they cannot both be live. Suspension wins by precedence.
2. **Trigger chain**: Suspension → voluntary takedown disabled → resolution → control returns. If the claim is upheld, the removal becomes permanent and voluntary takedown is moot.
3. **Permission intersection**: A third party's action strips a first party's permission over their own property. Legitimate, and severe enough that the *reason* must be on the screen — not in a help article.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Control returning after resolution is worth a notification. An artist who stopped checking should not discover months later that they could have acted.
5. **State transition conflict**: A voluntary takedown in flight when suspension lands — the removal proceeds and the dispute now concerns a release that no longer exists on stores. The provenance record persists (12.05.01 DT-02), which is what makes the dispute still resolvable. A neat, unplanned payoff of D-10: the fight can be settled about a record the stores have forgotten.

### CX-03: Voluntary Takedown ↔ Update & Redelivery

**Relationship**: On some stores, for some fields, an "update" *is* a takedown plus a redelivery — with all the irreversible loss that implies (streams, playlists, release date). The artist sees one button labelled Edit. This is the same hazard as 12.01 CX-02 (validation vs gate rendered alike) and 12.04.04 DT-02 (hard vs soft deadlines rendered alike): **the domain repeatedly presents things with radically different consequences through identical affordances.** Worth naming as a recurring pattern rather than three unrelated observations.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: bears the cost. Needs the price before the click, per field, per store (12.05.02 D-01).
- **Producer**: an asset update is the expensive kind almost by definition — a replaced master is never a quiet fix.
- **Operator / Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Both act on store presence via the same generator. An update that decomposes into takedown+redeliver must be atomic in intent — a failure between the two halves leaves the release down and not back up, which is the worst outcome available in this sub-domain.
2. **Trigger chain**: Edit → cost evaluation per store → confirm → either an in-place update or (takedown → redeliver). The second path can fail halfway. Recovery is `[PENDING — Step 5]` and matters more than its obscurity suggests.
3. **Permission intersection**: Same actors, but note the asymmetry — a Producer has `Config` on updates and `Read-only` on takedown. If their update silently decomposes into a takedown on some store, they have effected a removal they had no permission to request. That is a real hole and it follows directly from the shared machinery.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A decomposing update should notify contributors, because it is a takedown wearing an edit's clothes.
5. **State transition conflict**: Two updates racing, or an update racing a takedown. Takedown wins; the update is discarded rather than queued behind it.

### CX-04: Update & Redelivery ↔ Involuntary Takedown (the resolving-update back-edge)

**Relationship**: The most interesting unresolved question in the sub-domain. Some claims are resolved by an update — add the credit that was missed, attach the clearance that existed all along, correct the (P) line to what the rights record already says. CX-01 blocks exactly that. So the platform's safety mechanism can prevent the resolution it is waiting for.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: needs a route out that does not require the thing they are blocked from doing.
- **Producer**: often holds the resolving artifact and is the right actor for it.
- **Operator / Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Suspension gates updates; a resolving update would need to write to the release *and* to the claim's evidence set. Whether that is one act or two is unresolved and is the crux of the carve-out.
2. **Trigger chain**: Claim → suspension → resolving update (blocked?) → resolution → lift. If blocked, the artist's only route is the dispute process (24), which is slower and heavier than the fix would have been — and for a claim the artist *concedes*, that is pure waste.
3. **Permission intersection**: A carve-out would mean "updates are blocked, except updates that resolve this claim" — which requires classifying an update against a claim. Doable for narrow cases (attaching a clearance document), gameable for broad ones (editing metadata the claim rests on). Leaning: allow **additive evidence** (clearances, credits, attestations) during suspension; block **mutations** of the claimed facts. That distinction is worth carrying to Step 5.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A resolving update should notify the claimant — it may end the dispute without any process at all, which is the best available outcome for everyone.
5. **State transition conflict**: A resolving update landing at the same moment the dispute process reaches a decision. The evidence must be considered or explicitly excluded, never silently lost.

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 12.05.01 Voluntary Takedown | 12.05.02 Update (as an undo) | Considered: is takedown-then-redeliver a way to "undo" a release? Rejected — redelivery is a **new release** with a new date and zero streams (12.05.01 DT-01, D-03). Modelling it as an undo would be the single most misleading thing this sub-domain could do, because the affordance implies reversibility that does not exist. There is no undo in distribution and the product must not imply one. |
| R-02 | 12.05.02 Update | 12.05.01 Voluntary Takedown (metadata-driven removal) | Considered: should an update that removes every track amount to a takedown? Rejected — an empty release is a validation failure (12.01.02), not a removal. Modelling a removal path through the editor would give the artist a way to take a record down without ever seeing the loss statement that 12.05.01 D-01 exists to show them. |
| R-03 | 12.05.03 Involuntary Takedown | 12.05.02 Update (claim evidence) | Rejected as a *direct* edge, though the back-edge in CX-04 is real. Evidence for a claim belongs to the dispute process (24), not to the release editor. Routing claim evidence through metadata updates would conflate "correcting my record" with "defending my record" — two different acts with different audiences, different permissions and different consequences if they fail. |
