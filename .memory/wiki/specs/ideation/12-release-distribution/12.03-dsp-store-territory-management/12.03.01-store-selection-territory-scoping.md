# Feature: Store Selection & Territory Scoping

> **Number**: 12.03.01
> **Parent**: [DSP Store & Territory Management](./12.03-dsp-store-territory-management-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Choosing which stores a release goes to and in which territories — and, crucially, checking that
the artist actually holds the rights to send it there. Delivering a release to a territory covered
by someone else's exclusive licence is not a validation error; it is a breach of contract with a
counterparty who will find out.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Picks stores (defaulting to all available) and territories (defaulting to worldwide), and sees any territory they cannot deliver to greyed out **with the reason and the counterparty named** — "Japan is exclusive to {label} until Mar 2027", not "unavailable". Can deselect a store to escape a validation finding (12.01.02) and sees what that costs. |
| Producer | None | Not visible. Store and territory selection is a commercial decision belonging to the rights holder. A Producer with a points position cares about the outcome but has no say — that asymmetry is real and should not be softened by giving them a view they cannot act on. |
| Operator | None | Not visible. |
| Fan | None | Not visible. The Fan meets territory scoping as the "not available in your country" wall on the DSP — a consequence, never a control. |

## Behavior

Two coupled choices with different characters.

**Store selection** is preference. Default to every certified store (12.02.02); deselect what you
do not want. Deselecting is legitimate — some artists withhold from specific DSPs on principle,
or drop a store rather than comply with its metadata rules.

**Territory scoping** is *rights*, wearing preference's clothing. The artist may want worldwide;
whether they may *have* worldwide is a fact in 09. Sources of constraint:

| Constraint | Origin | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Exclusive licence to a label/distributor in a territory | 09 / 11 | That territory is unavailable, with a named counterparty and an expiry |
| Sample clearance limited by territory | 09 / 11 | Track-level, not release-level, restriction |
| Prior distributor still holding the release live somewhere | 12.08 / 12.05 | Cannot deliver into an existing live release without a duplicate |
| Cover/mechanical licence not held in a territory | 11 | Territory unavailable for that track |

The critical design decision: territory availability is **derived from the rights record**, not
typed by the artist. An artist self-declaring "worldwide" is exactly the self-assertion this
product exists to replace — and here it is a self-assertion with a contractual counterparty who
can sue.

Constraints can be **track-level** (a sample cleared for North America only) while selection feels
release-level. This is genuinely awkward and is the main source of complexity here.

### Happy Path

1. Musician reaches store selection; all certified stores are pre-selected, territories default to
   worldwide.
2. Platform resolves territory rights from 09 for every recording on the release.
3. Any restricted territory is shown greyed with a named reason and expiry.
4. Musician adjusts stores; each deselection shows what it costs (reach, and any finding it clears).
5. Selection is locked at delivery; changing it afterwards is a catalog-lifecycle action (12.05).

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| One track's sample is cleared for fewer territories than the release | **Track-level territory restriction.** Some DSPs support it; some do not | "Track 5's sample isn't cleared for Japan. Apple can handle that; Beatport can't — it'd need the whole release excluded from Japan." |
| Exclusive licence expires mid-rollout | Territory becomes available later — a scheduled *expansion*, not a redelivery the artist should have to remember | "Japan opens 12 Mar 2027. We'll deliver it then." |
| Artist selects a store that is suspended (12.02.02) | Store shown unavailable with a reason, not silently dropped | "Beatport is unavailable right now — not your release, their end." |
| Rights record has no territory data at all | **Cannot derive.** Falls back to the readiness gate (12.01.03) rather than defaulting to worldwide | "We don't know what territories you control. Let's fix that before delivery." |
| Artist deselects every store | Nothing to deliver | "Pick at least one store." |
| Release already live somewhere via a prior distributor | Delivering creates a duplicate — worse than not delivering | Routes to 12.08 (migration), not handled here |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Loading | Resolving rights from 09 | Skeleton store/territory picker |
| Populated | Rights resolved | All stores selected, territories with restrictions marked and explained |
| Restricted | Some territories unavailable | Greyed territories + named counterparty + expiry |
| Unknown | No territory data in 09 | Blocking; routes to the gate rather than guessing |
| Locked | Delivered | Read-only; changes route to 12.05 |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Territory scoping is a preference the artist declares — "worldwide" is a default, not a claim | Every competitor's model; it is a checkbox on DistroKid | ❌ REJECTED, and this is a **gap in the sweep's candidate list with real legal weight**. Territory is a *rights fact* (09), not a preference. A self-declared "worldwide" over a territory covered by someone else's exclusive is a breach with a named counterparty who monitors DSPs for exactly this. Competitors get away with it by pushing liability onto the artist in the ToS — which is precisely the "asking people to remember" pattern problem-statement.md names as the root cause, applied to contracts instead of credits. WeJammin holds the rights record (D-10), so it is the only platform that **can** derive this. If it does not, D-10's cost is being paid for nothing. |
| DT-02 | Territory constraints are release-level | Simplicity; it is how the picker naturally reads | ❌ REJECTED — sample and cover clearances are **per-recording**, so a single track can restrict a territory for the whole release on partners that lack track-level territory support. This is genuinely messy and cannot be modelled away: it is a real property of the rights layer meeting a real limitation of partner profiles. Both levels must exist, and the UI has to make a per-track fact legible in a per-release picker. |
| DT-03 | Deselecting a store to dodge a metadata rule should be discouraged | Instinct to protect the artist's reach | ❌ REJECTED — it is a **legitimate and informed trade**, and framing it as a mistake is paternalistic. An artist who would rather skip Beatport than ALL-CAPS-strip their title is making a real choice about their record. Show the cost (reach, revenue) and let them choose. Per `decision-classification.md` this is a product decision belonging to the user, and the same logic as 12.01.04 DT-01 (advise, never block artistic choices). |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | Territory availability is **derived from the rights record**, never self-declared | It is a rights fact with a contractual counterparty; only WeJammin can derive it (DT-01) |
| D-02 | Territory constraints exist at both release and track level | Sample/cover clearances are per-recording (DT-02) |
| D-03 | Dropping a store to avoid a rule is a supported, costed choice | It is the artist's record and their decision (DT-03) |
| D-04 | Missing territory data blocks and routes to the gate; it never defaults to worldwide | Defaulting would fabricate a rights claim (same defect as 12.02.01 DT-01) |
