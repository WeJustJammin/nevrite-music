# Feature: Claim & Whitelist Management

> **Number**: 12.06.02
> **Parent**: [Content ID & UGC Claiming](./12.06-content-id-ugc-claiming-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Deciding who does *not* get claimed. A registered fingerprint claims everything automatically —
including the artist's own channel, their label's, their collaborators', their fans', and the
video their drummer posted from the session. The whitelist is the only thing standing between an
automated rights system and the artist's own community.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Whitelists channels and accounts that should never be claimed, sees what is being claimed on their behalf, and can release a claim they did not want made. Sees claim activity as a list of **people and videos**, not as a revenue chart — because that is what it is. |
| Producer | None | Not visible. Whose videos get claimed is the rights holder's decision. Noted, though: the Producer's own session footage is a prime accidental-claim target, and they have no way to pre-empt it. |
| Operator | None | Not visible. |
| Fan | None | Not visible — **and they are the claimed party**. A fan who made a video with the artist's track gets a claim from a machine acting on the artist's behalf, with no notice from this platform and no lens into why. See DT-01. |

## Behavior

Claims arrive continuously and at scale once a fingerprint is registered (12.06.01). Each is an
automated assertion against a specific person's video. The artist's real decisions:

| Decision | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Whitelist** | Never claim this channel — pre-emptive |
| **Release** | Undo a claim already made — reactive, after the harm |
| **Monetise** | Take the revenue, video stays up (the default) |
| **Block** | Take the video down — rare, aggressive, mostly a mistake |

**Whitelisting must happen before registration**, not after. A claim against the artist's own
official video, their label's channel, or their bandmate is embarrassing and slow to undo, and by
the time they notice, it has already happened to someone.

The predictable whitelist set is largely **derivable from data WeJammin already holds**: the
artist's own channels (01), their band's (01), their collaborators on the recording (02), their
label (12.01.05). Every competitor makes the artist assemble this from memory after the claims
start landing. This is the same being-in-the-room asymmetry as MEAD (12.02.04 DT-01) and the split
sheet — the platform already knows who should not be claimed, because it knows who made the record.

### Happy Path

1. Before registration, the platform proposes a whitelist derived from the artist's channels, their
   band, the recording's contributors and their label.
2. Musician reviews, adds, confirms.
3. Registration proceeds (12.06.01) with the whitelist already active.
4. Claims begin against everyone else; the artist sees them as a list of videos and people.
5. Musician releases any claim that should not have been made; the channel is added to the
   whitelist so it does not recur.
6. Revenue accrues and is split by the ownership record (09) — the part every competitor gets wrong.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Claim against the artist's own video | Whitelist failure, and a visible one | Should be structurally prevented by the derived whitelist |
| Claim against a **fan's** video | Working as designed. Also: claiming a user of this platform | The novel case. Parent Q-02 — a values decision, not a bug |
| Claim against a **collaborator's** session footage | The drummer's own clip, claimed by a record they played on | Derived whitelist should catch it — they are in the credits (02) |
| Claim against a legitimate critic/reviewer | Fair use is not a Content ID concept | Release + whitelist; the platform cannot adjudicate fair use |
| Artist blocks instead of monetising | Takes down a fan's video. Usually a mistake, made once, remembered forever | Friction + a plain statement of what block means |
| Claim volume is enormous | Thousands of claims; a review queue nobody reads | Summarise by exception; surface the ones against **known people** first |
| Claim against another WeJammin user | Both parties are ours. Both see a version of this | Routes to 12.06.03 / 24 |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Pre-registration | Not yet registered | Derived whitelist proposal for review |
| Active | Registered, claims flowing | Claim list: people and videos, exceptions first |
| Empty | Registered, no claims | "Nothing's used your track yet" |
| Reviewing | Artist examining a claim | The video, the channel, who they are to the artist if we know |
| Released | Claim withdrawn | Channel offered for whitelisting so it does not recur |
| Conflicted | Counter-claim | Routes to 12.06.03 |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Claiming a fan's video is normal industry practice and needs no special treatment | It is exactly what every distributor's Content ID offering does, silently, for everyone | ❌ REJECTED — **no competitor has both parties as users. WeJammin does.** Per D-11, fans are first-class users, not CRM records; per personas.md the Fan's whole relationship is "I care about this artist". A platform that hosts the fan, hosts the artist, and then runs a machine that accuses the fan on the artist's behalf — with no notice to the fan from us — is doing something genuinely novel and genuinely uncomfortable. It is not obviously wrong (the claim is usually just monetisation, and the video stays up), but it is a **values decision nobody else has had to make**, and defaulting to industry practice would be making it by accident. Escalated to parent Q-02. |
| DT-02 | The artist should build their own whitelist | It is their community; only they know it. Universal industry practice | ❌ REJECTED — WeJammin can **derive most of it**: the artist's channels (01), their band (01), the recording's credited contributors (02), their label (12.01.05). Every competitor makes the artist reconstruct this from memory *after* the claims have already landed on their drummer — reconstruction again, in miniature, with a social cost attached. This is the being-in-the-room asymmetry from D-18 applied to a mundane list, and it is the kind of small, unglamorous payoff that makes the consolidation thesis feel real rather than argued. |
| DT-03 | Claim review should be presented as a revenue dashboard | It is what generates the money, and it is how every tool presents it | ❌ REJECTED — each claim is **a specific person's video**. A revenue chart abstracts away that the artist is making decisions about people, several of whom they know. Presenting claims as people-and-videos, exceptions first, with "who this person is to you" where we know (a contributor? a fan who follows you?), is both more honest and produces better decisions. It is also the only presentation that makes DT-01's tension visible to the artist rather than hiding it behind a number — an artist looking at "£340 this month" cannot see that £12 of it came from their bass player's session clip. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | The whitelist is **derived** from identity, band, credits and label, then reviewed | The platform already knows who should not be claimed (DT-02) |
| D-02 | Whitelisting happens before registration | A claim against your own drummer is not undoable socially, only technically |
| D-03 | Claims are presented as people and videos, not as revenue | Each one is a decision about a person (DT-03) |
| D-04 | Block carries friction and a plain statement of what it does | It takes down a fan's video; it is almost always a mistake |
