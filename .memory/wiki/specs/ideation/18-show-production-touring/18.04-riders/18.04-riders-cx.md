## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Templates & Versioning ↔ Technical Rider

**Relationship**: The technical rider is never consumed directly — a date consumes a *resolved
version*. The version layer is what allows one rider to serve a tour while a March gear change
doesn't rewrite what February's venue agreed to. When a new version is cut, upcoming dates re-diff
(18.03.02) and already-advanced dates hold their version unless explicitly re-opened.

**Role scoping**:
- **Producer**: cuts versions, sees which dates sit on which, and decides whether a change is safety-critical enough to re-open advanced dates.
- **Operator**: sees only their date's resolved rider — never the version history, which would expose the act's tour shape and negotiating pattern.
- **Musician**: sees what applies tonight.
- **Fan**: no visibility.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The rider document is mutable; versions are immutable snapshots. The advance owns a version reference. No party may mutate a version — only cut a new one.
2. **Trigger chain**: New version → re-diff upcoming dates → new advance items may appear → owning side notified. Advanced dates are untouched unless re-opened, which is a change-control event (18.03.05).
3. **Permission intersection**: Only the rider's owner (Producer) cuts versions. A venue's redline never produces a version — it produces a per-date overlay.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A version cut mid-tour must notify affected dates' venues, or they work from a superseded rider. Advanced dates deliberately do *not* notify — that's the point.
5. **State transition conflict**: Cutting a version while a date is mid-advance — does that date get v1 or v2? Unresolved; the safe default is that the advance holds its version at open, with an explicit "update to v2?" prompt.

### CX-02: Templates & Versioning ↔ Hospitality Rider

**Relationship**: Same version governance, with one extra edge: hospitality quantities are *derived*
from party size (18.04.02 D-01), so a rider version and a crew roster change can both alter what a
date needs. A version pins the items; it must not pin a stale head count, or the "catering for 5,
touring party of 9" failure returns through the back door.

**Role scoping**:
- **Producer**: manages both axes and sees when a frozen pack's quantities have gone stale.
- **Musician**: sees tonight's provision; contributes dietary requirements that flow from their profile.
- **Operator**: sees resolved quantities; a change after freeze reaches them as change control.
- **Fan**: no visibility.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The version owns *what* is asked for; the crew roster owns *how many*. Two sources, one resolved rider. Neither may write to the other.
2. **Trigger chain**: Party size change → quantity re-derivation → if the date is frozen, a change-control event; if not, silent update.
3. **Permission intersection**: Adding a crew member (18.08.01) silently changes what the venue must cater — a permission in the crew domain mutates a rider obligation. This needs to be visible, not silent.
4. **Notification fan-out**: The venue must be told the party grew. This is the domain's most common late-change and it originates outside the rider.
5. **State transition conflict**: A version cut and a roster change landing together must resolve deterministically — items from the version, counts from the roster at resolution time.

### CX-03: Access Rider ↔ Templates & Versioning (deliberate non-participation)

**Relationship**: The most important edge in this file, and it is a **negative** one. Access
requirements deliberately do *not* flow through the act's version layer, because they are not the
act's data. They belong to the person (18.04.03 D-01), live on their profile in domain 01, and
attach to whichever dates that person is on. If the person leaves the tour, their requirements
leave with them — mid-version, mid-tour, without a rider amendment.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: their requirements travel with them across bands, tours and employers. No re-disclosure per gig.
- **Producer**: cannot version, template, fork, or amend them. Can only see and provide for them.
- **Operator**: sees resolved requirements for their date, need-to-know only.
- **Fan**: no visibility.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: There is none, deliberately. The act's rider references the person's requirements; it never copies them. A copy would fork ownership and re-create the problem — a stale copy of someone's access needs, editable by a TM.
2. **Trigger chain**: Person joins/leaves a date → their requirements attach/detach → affected dates re-diff. This fires from the crew/lineup domain, not from the rider.
3. **Permission intersection**: **The sharpest intersection in the sub-domain.** The Producer has full authority over the act's riders and *zero* authority here. Enforcement must be at the data layer — a UI-only rule will be defeated by the first bulk edit.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A new access requirement mid-tour re-diffs all remaining dates and notifies the Producer of what to fix — not of why it exists.
5. **State transition conflict**: A frozen advance references the act's rider version *and* the person's live requirements. These have different immutability rules by design: the act's terms are settled, but a person's access need arising after freeze (an injury) must reach the venue anyway. Freeze cannot trap it.

### CX-04: Hospitality Rider ↔ Access Rider

**Relationship**: They sit next to each other in every real-world rider and are constantly
conflated — that conflation is the failure 18.04.03 exists to correct. A dietary requirement and an
access requirement look alike (both are personal, both are "needs"), but hospitality items are the
act's and tradeable, while access items are the person's and are not. The boundary is not
taxonomic tidiness; it decides who can trade the item away.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: authors both, in different places — dietary on their profile as hospitality input, access as their own rider.
- **Producer**: can buy out catering; cannot buy out access.
- **Operator**: sees a count of dietary needs and a set of access requirements; may not infer one from the other.
- **Fan**: no visibility.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Both reference person-level data in domain 01. A severe allergy is arguably both — it's a dietary requirement *and* a medical need that can't be bought out (18.04.02 edge case 2). The overlap is real and needs a resolution rule.
2. **Trigger chain**: A buyout redline on catering must check whether any dietary item is medical-severity before it can be accepted — that check crosses this boundary.
3. **Permission intersection**: The Producer's buyout authority stops at the medical/access line. Where exactly that line sits is Q-01 below.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A buyout affecting someone with a severe requirement must notify that person, not just the negotiating parties.
5. **State transition conflict**: Both aggregate to the venue, but under different disclosure rules — aggregate counts for dietary, need-to-know requirements for access. A single "special requirements" export would collapse both models and leak.

### CX-05: Technical Rider ↔ Hospitality Rider

**Relationship**: Medium confidence. They share an authoring surface, a party-size dependency and an
advance, and the venue perceives them as one document. But they diff against different venue data
(gear vs amenities), carry different privacy exposure, and are answered by different people at the
venue (production vs hospitality/front of house). The pairing is presentational more than
structural.

**Role scoping**:
- **Producer**: authors both; sees them as one rider.
- **Operator**: often routes them to two different people internally — a fact the advance's item assignment should respect.
- **Musician**: contributes to both.
- **Fan**: no visibility.

**Synthesis questions answered** _(Medium confidence — remainder deferred to Step 5)_:
1. **Shared state conflict**: None — disjoint item sets, shared only by the party-size input.
3. **Permission intersection**: None distinct; both follow the act's rider authority.

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 18.04.01 Technical Rider | 18.04.03 Access Rider | No shared state and no trigger dependency. Both are diffed against the venue, but against different data by different mechanisms, and neither constrains the other. A stage ramp is an access requirement, not a technical one, even though both are about the stage — the distinction is *whose need it is*, and that's exactly what CX-04 covers. Drawing this edge would invite treating access as a technical spec item, which is the failure 18.04.03 DT-01 rejects. |
| R-02 | 18.04.01 Technical Rider | 18.04.03 Access Rider (via gear) | Considered separately: does a performer's IEM/hearing requirement bridge them? Rejected — a hearing-protection access need is the person's; the IEM system is the act's technical spec. They may reference the same equipment while remaining different objects with different owners. |

> **Notes for agents:**
> - CX-03 is a **non-participation** cross-cut. It is recorded because the absence is a designed constraint, not an oversight — a later agent who "fixes" access riders to version with the act's rider would be reintroducing 18.04.03 DT-02's failure.
> - The rejected pairs both concern the same temptation: treating access requirements as technical spec items because they involve physical infrastructure. The discipline is ownership, not subject matter.
