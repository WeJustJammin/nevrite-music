## Overview

What the buyer actually acquires when they buy a digital good — which is never the file. They
acquire an **entitlement**: a right, of a stated scope, to run or use something, on some number
of machines, under terms, possibly revocably. This sub-domain owns the entitlement record, its
enforcement (activation, seats, blacklisting), its bridges to third-party DRM the industry
already runs on, and — the part no store models — the machine-readable statement of what the
buyer is permitted to *do with the output*.

**Why this is a sub-domain**: issuance creates the entitlement; activation enforces it against
physical machines; seats bound it; blacklisting revokes it; terms scope it; trials are a
time-limited instance of it. Six capabilities over one entity, each meaningless alone.

## Open Questions

|---|----------|-------|-------------|
| Q-01 | **Does WeJammin run an activation server at all?** Online activation requires the platform to be a runtime dependency of software it did not write — an availability obligation (a down activation server stops paid work in studios) and an architecture commitment far beyond a storefront. The alternative is unenforced licences plus watermarking and social pressure, which is what most sample/preset vendors already accept. | User | `/ideate-validate` |
| Q-02 | GDPR erasure vs perpetual entitlement: a buyer exercises the right to be forgotten, but their perpetual licences are contractual obligations to *vendors* and must arguably survive. Deleting the account deletes the entitlement record; keeping it defeats the erasure. Which wins? | User | `/create-prd-security` |
| Q-03 | Does an entitlement belong to a person or an org? An Operator's studio buys plugins that must outlive the engineer who clicked buy; a Band (entity, domain 01) may own shared tools. Personal-only entitlements make studio purchases unmanageable. | Agent | `/create-prd-architecture` |
