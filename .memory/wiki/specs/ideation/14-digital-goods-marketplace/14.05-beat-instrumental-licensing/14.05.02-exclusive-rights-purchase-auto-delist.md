# Feature: Exclusive Rights Purchase & Auto-Delist

> **Number**: 14.05.02
> **Parent**: [14.05 Beat & Instrumental Licensing](./14.05-beat-instrumental-licensing-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Buying a beat outright — the artist pays materially more, the beat leaves the catalog forever, and
what changes hands is not a file but **ownership**. This is the only transaction on the platform
where a purchase destroys its own product, and the only one in domain 14 that writes to the
copyright record (domain 09) rather than to an entitlement.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | The exclusive buyer. Pays a lump sum believing they now own the beat — and needs to know, before paying, exactly what they are getting: whether existing leases survive (CX-02), what share of the copyright transfers, and whether the producer retains publishing. Almost nobody asks these questions and all of them matter. |
| Producer | Full | The beatmaker selling. Permanently loses a catalog item that might have earned for years, for one payment — a genuine business decision, not a checkout. Also the party who must actually *hold* the rights they are selling (DT-02). |
| Operator | None | No column. Explicit absence — no venue or studio role in a beat copyright transfer. |
| Fan | None | Not on the fan surface. A fan may notice a public beat page disappear; that is all. |

## Behavior

An exclusive purchase atomically: takes payment, delists the beat at every tier (CX-01), resolves
existing leases per policy (CX-02), and records a rights transfer into domain 09. The rights
transfer is the part that makes this unlike every other sale in the domain — an entitlement says
"you may use this"; this says "you own this".

The transaction must therefore state, before payment, what is actually transferred: the master,
the composition, publishing share, and the producer's retained interests. Beat-market practice
varies wildly and the defaults are frequently invisible to the buyer.

### Happy Path

1. Producer enables an exclusive tier with a price and a defined rights scope.
2. Artist opens the beat; the exclusive tier shows the scope in plain language *and* the count of live leases (index Q-02).
3. Artist purchases; the transaction locks the listing (CX-01 — the race is the danger).
4. Payment captures; the beat delists at every tier atomically.
5. Existing leases are resolved per policy; every lessee is notified (CX-02).
6. A rights transfer is written to domain 09; the artist's ownership record now reflects it.
7. The public preview comes down (CX-04) — the beat leaves the internet.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Two exclusive purchases race (CX-01) | **The worst failure in the sub-domain**: two artists each believing they own one copyright. Requires a hard lock, not read-then-write. | Loser: "This beat was sold moments ago" — and their payment must never capture |
| Existing leases survive the exclusive sale (CX-02) | The "exclusive" buyer shares the beat with N artists for months or years | Live-lease count disclosed *before* payment — index Q-02 |
| Lease checkout completes microseconds before the exclusive | The exclusive is encumbered by a lease sold after the buyer decided to buy | `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]` |
| Payment captures, rights transfer to 09 fails | Artist paid for an exclusivity that doesn't exist in the ownership record — the platform's own thesis broken at the moment it mattered | Must be atomic or compensated; never silently reconciled later |
| Producer sells an exclusive on a beat they co-produced | They are selling a copyright they hold only part of. Extremely common — beats routinely have co-producers. | See DT-02 and Q-02 |
| Producer sells an exclusive, then re-uploads the same beat under a new title | Fraud, and trivially easy | Audio fingerprinting against sold exclusives — see Q-03 |
| Artist refunds an exclusive (14.09) | Does the copyright transfer reverse? Do the terminated leases come back? | `[PENDING]` — index Q-03; probably the sharpest refund case on the platform |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | An exclusive purchase is a premium tier — the top row of the tier table | The candidate's framing (exclusivity listed alongside "tiered leases"), and how it renders in every beat store | ❌ REJECTED — **it is a copyright assignment with a buy button, and the tier table is a costume.** Every other row in that table mints an entitlement; this one transfers ownership, writes to domain 09, destroys the product, and terminates or encumbers other people's live contracts. Its failure mode is not "buyer disappointed" but "two artists own one copyright" — an unwindable state that no refund fixes. It is also the *only* transaction in domain 14 that changes who owns something, which is precisely the object the entire project exists to keep an accurate record of (D-18). Modelling it as a checkout tier is how the beat industry ended up with its litigation profile: a life-altering rights transfer presented with the ceremony of a shipping upgrade. It deserves confirmation friction, disclosure of what actually transfers, and atomicity with the rights record — none of which a "premium tier" implies. |
| DT-02 | The producer selling the exclusive owns the beat | The unexamined assumption underneath the entire feature — and underneath the candidate list, which never raises it | ❌ REJECTED — **beats routinely have co-producers, and this feature lets one of them sell the whole thing.** A beat made by two producers over a shared session, or built on a sample pack whose terms forbid exclusive resale (14.02.05), or containing an uncleared chop — in each case the seller does not hold what they are selling, and the platform brokers a copyright transfer that is void. This is 14.02.05 DT-03 ("vendors granting rights they do not hold") at its most consequential: not an exposed sample in a track, but a **copyright sold twice**. And here the project's own machinery is the answer rather than an afterthought — WeJammin holds credits (02), splits (09), and provenance for work made on the platform. If the beat was made here, the platform *already knows* it has two producers and can require both to consent. That is a genuine, non-obvious advantage no beat store has, and it points at the same conclusion as DT-02 in 14.05.01: the rights model belongs with the rights domains, and this storefront should be reading it rather than inventing it. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | The exclusive sale, delist, lease resolution and rights transfer to 09 are atomic — or compensated, never silently reconciled | Per DT-01/CX-01. A partial failure leaves an artist paying for an exclusivity the ownership record denies. |
| D-02 | The rights scope (master, composition, publishing share, producer's retained interests) is stated in plain language before payment | Beat-market defaults are invisible and vary wildly; the buyer is transferring a copyright and usually does not know what they are getting. |
| D-03 | The live-lease count is disclosed before an exclusive purchase | Per CX-02 — "exclusive" that shares the beat with 40 live leases is a word the buyer understands differently from the seller. |
