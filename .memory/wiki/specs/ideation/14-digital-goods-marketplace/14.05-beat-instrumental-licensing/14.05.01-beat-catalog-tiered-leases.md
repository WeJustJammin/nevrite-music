# Feature: Beat Catalog & Tiered Lease Options

> **Number**: 14.05.01
> **Parent**: [14.05 Beat & Instrumental Licensing](./14.05-beat-instrumental-licensing-index.md)
> **Status**: [PARTIAL]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-17

## Overview

A beatmaker's storefront: instrumentals listed with a player, each offered at tiered prices that
are not really prices — they are **rights bundles**. The £20 MP3 lease, the £50 WAV lease, the
£100 trackout and the £300 unlimited differ in file format, yes, but principally in stream caps,
term length, and what the artist may do with the record they make.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | **The artist buying a beat.** Picks a tier — almost always by price, rarely by understanding what the tier obligates them to (DT-01). Their purchase is the foundation of a record they will release, so the tier's caps become their problem years later, at scale, when the track works. |
| Producer | Full | **The beatmaker selling.** Uploads instrumentals, sets tiers and prices, sets their tag (14.05.04). For many working producers this is the primary income stream in the portfolio personas.md describes — small, frequent, non-exclusive sales. |
| Operator | None | No column. A venue or studio operator neither buys nor sells beats. Explicit absence. |
| Fan | None | Not on the fan surface as a buyer. A fan may *hear* a public beat page (14.05.04) but has no reason to license an instrumental. |

## Behavior

A beat is a listing (14.01.01) of product type `beat` with an always-available tagged preview
(14.05.04) and a tier table. Each tier is expressed through the terms registry (index D-03) — a
tier IS a terms bundle with a price — so a track cleared against a beat lease uses the same
clearance chain as one cleared against a sample pack (14.02.05).

Tier axes that actually vary in this market: delivered format (MP3 / WAV / trackout stems), stream
and sales caps, term length, whether radio/TV/sync is permitted, whether the producer must be
credited, and whether the beat remains available to others (non-exclusive) or not (14.05.02).

### Happy Path

1. Producer uploads an instrumental; a tagged preview is generated (14.05.04).
2. Producer sets tiers, choosing from terms templates rather than writing licence text.
3. Beat publishes with the preview publicly streamable and the tier table visible.
4. Artist finds the beat (search, or the producer's page), streams the tagged preview.
5. Artist picks a tier; the caps and term are stated in plain language at the buy button — not in a PDF.
6. Purchase completes; untagged files unlock at the tier (CX-04); a lease is minted with caps and term (CX-03); terms bind (14.02 CX-02).
7. The lease's obligations flow into the artist's release record (12) and clearance chain (09).

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Artist buys a 10,000-stream lease; the track does 2 million | The model's defining event. The artist is in breach of a contract they didn't read, on a record that finally worked. | Caps stated at purchase; proactive notification on approach (14.05.03) — not a lawyer's letter at 2M |
| Beat sells exclusively while in someone's cart (CX-01) | The product no longer exists | "This beat was sold exclusively and is no longer available" — cart item removed |
| Producer sells a beat containing an uncleared sample | Every artist who leased it has an exposed record. 14.02.05 DT-03, fanned out to a customer base. | Attestation at submission; the platform cannot verify the chain |
| Producer raises tier prices | Applies to new leases only; existing leases are bound (14.02 CX-02) | Existing lessee: unaffected |
| Two artists lease the same beat and both release | Normal and expected for non-exclusive — but frequently surprises the artists | Non-exclusivity must be stated plainly, not implied by the tier name |
| Producer deletes a beat with live leases | Listing withdrawal is a soft-delist, not a lease termination — the catalog page goes, but every live lease and its bound terms (14.02 CX-02) persist unchanged, and lessees keep their library row and untagged files. A producer cannot un-sell a licence they already granted. | Producer: "Withdrawn from sale — X active leases remain in force and are unaffected." Existing lessee: no change, beat stays in their library. New buyers: not found. |
| Artist buys a tier, then wants to upgrade after the track takes off | Common and commercially sensible — the upgrade path is real revenue | See Q-02 |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Tiers are pricing — good/better/best, a packaging decision | The candidate's phrasing ("tiered leases") and how the tier tables *look* on BeatStars | ❌ REJECTED — **tiers are contract terms with a price attached, and treating them as packaging is exactly why the beat market generates the disputes it does.** The difference between a £20 and a £50 lease is not file quality; it is a stream cap, a term length, and whether the artist may put the record on the radio. An artist picks a tier the way they pick a shipping option — by price, in ten seconds — and thereby signs an obligation that binds a record for years. Then the track works, blows past 10,000 streams, and they are in breach of a document they never opened. Personas.md names this precisely as the Musician's worst accidental behaviour: "signs a split sheet they don't understand and discovers years later they gave away their publishing." The design consequence is not a nicer tier table — it is that **caps must be stated as plain-language obligations at the buy button** and enforced as proactive notifications (14.05.03), not as a PDF nobody reads and a lawyer's letter at 2M streams. |
| DT-02 | This is a store transaction — it belongs in domain 14 | The ratified map's placement, which the domain index defends on the "player and a cart" argument | ⏳ DEFERRED — escalated to index Q-01, because classification made the objection stronger rather than weaker. Look at what is actually transacted: not a file, but a **licence over a future record** — stream caps, term limits, radio permissions, credit obligations, and at the exclusive tier an outright copyright transfer (domain 09). The MP3 the artist downloads is almost incidental; they could get the same audio from the tagged preview with a screen recorder. What they pay for is the *right*. Domain 11 (Music Licensing) exists for precisely this object, and domain 09 owns the transfer. The counter-argument — that it's bought in a store with a player and a cart — is real but shallow: the cart is a platform cross-cut, and the player is a cross-cut too. **A cross-cut and a cross-cut do not make a domain.** I am not overturning D-16 unilaterally; but the domain index's own hedge ("it is genuinely both") deserves resolution rather than inheritance, and the honest recommendation is that 11 owns the rights model while 14 owns at most the storefront. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | Tier caps and obligations are stated in plain language at the buy button, not in an attached licence document | Per DT-01. The artist picks by price in ten seconds; if the obligation isn't in that moment, it doesn't exist for them until it's a dispute. |
| D-02 | Tiers are expressed through the terms registry (14.02.05), not a parallel beat-licensing vocabulary | Per index D-03 — a second terms system would split the clearance chain in half for no gain. |
| D-03 | Non-exclusivity is stated plainly rather than implied | Artists routinely believe a lease is exclusive-ish. It is not, and the surprise arrives after release. |
