## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Exclusivity ↔ Catalog

**Relationship**: An exclusive purchase removes the beat from sale entirely — all tiers, at once,
atomically with payment. This is unique on the platform: every other transaction leaves the
product available, and this one destroys it. A race here (two buyers, one exclusive) is not a
double-sold widget; it is two artists who each believe they own a copyright.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the exclusive buyer, paying materially more precisely for the removal. Their entire purchase *is* the delisting.
- **Producer**: the beatmaker, who loses a catalog item permanently and gains a lump sum — a real business decision, not a checkout.
- **Operator**: not affected.
- **Fan**: sees a public beat page vanish, if beat pages are public (14.05.04 Q-02).

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The listing is the shared entity; the exclusive sale mutates it terminally. Single writer, but the race matters enormously.
2. **Trigger chain**: Payment → delist → lease resolution (CX-02) → rights transfer to domain 09. If the rights transfer fails after payment, the artist has paid for an exclusivity that does not exist in the ownership record. Must be atomic or compensated.
3. **Permission intersection**: Only the beat's owner can sell an exclusive — and per 14.05.02, only if they still hold the copyright to sell.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Existing lessees must be told (CX-02). So must anyone with the beat in a cart — their cart item no longer exists.
5. **State transition conflict**: **Yes, and it is the sharp one.** Two exclusive purchases racing, or an exclusive racing a lease checkout. Needs a hard lock; the failure mode is two people owning one copyright.

### CX-02: Exclusivity ↔ Lease Lifecycle

**Relationship**: When an exclusive sells, existing leases are in a defined state that nobody
agrees on. Industry practice: leases survive to expiry, so the "exclusive" buyer shares the beat
with N existing artists for months or years. Buyer expectation: exclusivity means exclusive, now.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician (exclusive buyer)**: paid for exclusivity, may receive a beat still licensed to others. Must be told the count *before* paying.
- **Musician (existing lessee)**: has a released track; a terminated lease would strand it. Their record is already out.
- **Producer**: sold both, and is the one both parties will blame.
- **Operator / Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Leases and the exclusive are competing rights over one asset — a genuine conflict resolved by policy, not by merge.
2. **Trigger chain**: Exclusive sale → enumerate live leases → apply policy (survive/terminate) → notify all parties. Async notification, sync policy application.
3. **Permission intersection**: The exclusive buyer's rights are bounded by leases they did not sign and cannot see before purchase — unless we show the count, which index Q-02 requires.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Every live lessee, plus the exclusive buyer receiving an honest encumbrance disclosure.
5. **State transition conflict**: A lease checkout completing microseconds before an exclusive — now the exclusive is encumbered by a lease sold after the buyer decided to buy.

### CX-03: Catalog ↔ Lease Lifecycle

**Relationship**: The tier the buyer picks in the catalog defines the caps and term the lifecycle
enforces. Tiers are expressed through the terms registry (index D-03), so a beat lease and a
sample pack licence speak the same vocabulary and reach the same clearance chain (09).

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: picks a tier by price and discovers its caps later, which is the model's core defect (14.05.01 DT-01).
- **Producer**: defines tiers; their choices become other people's obligations.
- **Operator / Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The tier is bound at purchase (14.02 CX-02) and frozen; the vendor cannot retroactively tighten caps.
2. **Trigger chain**: Purchase → lease minted with caps and term → lifecycle begins tracking.
3. **Permission intersection**: The tier *is* the permission — over a released record, not a file.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Cap approach and term expiry notify the artist (14.05.03).
5. **State transition conflict**: None at purchase.

### CX-04: Tagged Preview ↔ Catalog

**Relationship**: The full instrumental is publicly streamable with a producer tag over it; the
untagged files unlock only at the purchased tier. The tag is the reason a beatmaker can put their
whole catalog on the open internet.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: hears the tag while shopping; gets untagged files on purchase.
- **Producer**: sets their tag; it is simultaneously security and marketing (a recognisable tag is free advertising).
- **Operator**: not affected.
- **Fan**: hears tagged beats if beat pages are public — the only Fan touchpoint in this sub-domain.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The tagged preview and untagged masters are separate artifacts of one product.
2. **Trigger chain**: Publish → tagged preview generated. Purchase → untagged files unlock at tier (MP3 / WAV / trackout stems).
3. **Permission intersection**: Tier determines which untagged artifacts unlock. A trackout tier gets stems; an MP3 lease does not.
4. **Notification fan-out**: None.
5. **State transition conflict**: Exclusive sale (CX-01) removes the public preview too — the beat leaves the internet.

A producer tag is **audible, overt, promotional, and identifies the seller**; a forensic watermark is **inaudible, covert, defensive, and identifies the buyer**. They have inverse goals, inverse audiences, and inverse success criteria — a tag that can't be heard has failed; a watermark that can be heard has failed. Recording this because a spec writer would otherwise reasonably try to build them with one mechanism. |
