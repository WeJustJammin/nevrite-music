# Feature: Bundled Software Licences with Used Hardware

> **Number**: 14.06.03
> **Parent**: [14.06 Used Licence Transfer & Resale](./14.06-used-licence-transfer-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

When someone sells a used audio interface, a controller, or a studio's retired rack, the software
that came with it is part of what they are selling — and today, on every platform, it silently
isn't. The buyer receives a box; the licences stay in the seller's account forever. This feature
makes the bundled licence transfer *with* the hardware, which requires a domain 13 sale and a
domain 14 licence transfer to settle as one transaction across two very different physics.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | The common case. Sells an interface that came with a DAW-lite and a plugin bundle; the buyer reasonably expects them. Today the seller usually doesn't even know the licences are separable, and the buyer discovers the gap after the box arrives. |
| Producer | Full | Same, with more valuable bundles — a used UAD interface or Apollo carries real licence value that can exceed the hardware's. |
| Operator | Full | **Studio clear-outs, at volume.** Retiring a room means hardware and its licences together, and the Operator is the one persona for whom this is a recurring business event rather than a one-off. Their one substantial workflow in domain 14. |
| Fan | None | Not on the fan surface. Fans do not sell studio hardware. |

## Behavior

A domain 13 hardware listing may declare bundled licences held by the seller in their WeJammin
library (14.03.03). The transfer policy (14.06.01) is checked per licence — bundled/OEM grants
often carry stricter terms than retail ones (CX-03), and some transfer *only* with the unit, which
is the one case where bundling helps.

Settlement is the hard part (CX-02): one transaction, two settlements, different physics. The
licence moves instantly and irrevocably; the hardware ships, is inspected, and may come back. The
two cannot settle at the same moment, and every ordering has a victim.

### Happy Path

1. Seller lists hardware in domain 13; the flow surfaces licences in their library plausibly associated with that product and asks whether they are included.
2. Seller confirms; the registry checks each licence's transfer policy (CX-03).
3. Listing shows the hardware **and** the transferable licences — which is a materially better listing than any competitor's, and is most of this feature's value.
4. Buyer purchases; funds go to escrow (14.06.02).
5. Hardware ships and is delivered; the inspection window runs.
6. On window close, both settle: funds release and the licence transfers atomically.
7. Buyer activates the licence on their machine (14.02.02).

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Licence settles on payment; buyer returns the hardware | Buyer keeps a licence they didn't pay for; clawback (14.09.03) is needed after they may have activated it | The reason for settling on delivery instead — but see the next row |
| Licence settles on delivery; hardware is in transit for 3 weeks | Seller's licence is frozen in escrow while a box crosses a continent; if the sale fails they've lost use of it for nothing | Escrow state visible to both; the cost of the safer ordering |
| Hardware arrives dead; buyer returns it | Licence must claw back (14.09.03) — and the seller may have already deactivated their machines | `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]` — CX-02's core unresolved case |
| Buyer has no vendor account to receive an OEM licence | 14.02.03's iLok problem arriving in a used sale, after the money moved | Eligibility checked *before* purchase, not at settlement |
| OEM licence is non-transferable (CX-03) | Common. The bundle is hardware-only; the buyer must be told before buying, not after. | Per-licence transfer state on the listing |
| Licence transfers only *with* the unit | The one case bundling helps — policy and transaction agree | Clean path |
| Seller doesn't know they have bundled licences | The status quo everywhere: value silently evaporates on every used sale | The surfacing step (Happy Path 1) is the feature's real contribution — see DT-02 |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Loading | Listing flow surfacing licences | "Checking your licences for this product…" |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | The bundle settles when the hardware is delivered — mirror the gear escrow's existing conditional | The obvious reuse of domain 13's settlement model, which already conditions on delivery | ❌ REJECTED as a clean answer, because **the two legs have incompatible reversibility and every ordering has a victim.** Settle the licence at payment and the buyer can return the hardware and keep a licence they've already activated — clawback (14.09.03) then has to reach a machine that may never come online again (14.02.02 D-01's fail-open posture). Settle at delivery and the seller's licence sits frozen for weeks while a box crosses a continent, unusable, and if the sale collapses they have lost its use for nothing. Settle at inspection-window close and you have both problems, smaller. There is no ordering that is safe for both parties, because a shipment is reversible and a licence transfer is not — and no amount of escrow design fixes an asymmetry in physics. The honest resolutions are all product decisions with a named loser (Q-01), and the feature must not pretend a technical answer exists. |
| DT-02 | The value here is completing the transaction — moving the licence with the box | The candidate's framing ("Bundled Software Licences with Used Hardware Sales"), which is entirely about the transfer | ❌ REJECTED — **the value is the discovery, not the transfer, and the transfer is the part that might not be worth building.** Today a seller lists an interface and does not know it carries £600 of licences; the buyer does not think to ask; the value evaporates silently on every used sale in the market. The step that captures it is Happy Path 1 — the platform *knows* what is in the seller's library (14.03.03) and can surface "this product usually ships with these three licences; do you still hold them, and are they included?" That question has never been asked at the point of listing anywhere, and it improves the listing whether or not the licences can move: "includes 2 of 4 bundled licences (2 non-transferable)" is a better, more honest listing than any competitor's, and it prices the hardware correctly. Which lands on the same conclusion as 14.06.01 DT-01 from a different direction — **the registry-plus-disclosure half of this sub-domain is worth building even if the escrow half never is** (index Q-01). |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | The listing flow proactively surfaces plausibly-bundled licences from the seller's library and asks whether they are included | Per DT-02 — this is the feature's real value and it works even if transfer never settles. Nobody in the market does it. |
| D-02 | Per-licence transfer state is shown on the listing, including the non-transferable ones | "Includes 2 of 4 bundled licences" is honest and prices the hardware correctly. Silence lets the buyer assume all four. |
| D-03 | Buyer eligibility to *receive* a licence (vendor account, DRM system) is checked before purchase | Discovering at settlement that the buyer cannot hold the licence is a failure after the money moved. |
