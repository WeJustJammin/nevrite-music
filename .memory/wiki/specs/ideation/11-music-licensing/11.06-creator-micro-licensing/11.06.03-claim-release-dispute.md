# Feature: Claim Release & Dispute Escalation

> **Number**: 11.06.03
> **Parent**: [11.06 Creator Micro-Licensing](./11.06-creator-micro-licensing-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

The remedy for the thing the product promised would not happen. A creator with a valid licence gets claimed
anyway — by a stale aggregator catalogue, a PRO sweep, a false fingerprint match, or their own impatience —
and needs it gone, fast, without becoming a lawyer. It was not in the sweep's candidate list, and it handles
the single most common complaint in this market.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Read-only | Sees claims raised against their catalogue and how they resolved. **Not the adjudicator** — a claim against a licence their work is subject to is not theirs to arbitrate; they already consented by opting in. |
| Producer | Read-only | Same, master side. |
| Operator | None | No stake in this capacity. |
| Fan | Full | **As creator buyer.** Raises "I have a licence, release this" in one action; tracks it; escalates. Must never be asked to understand *why* they were claimed — that is the platform's problem, not theirs. |

## Behavior

The creator's action is one button: **"I have a licence."** Everything after that is the platform's work, and
that boundary is the feature. A creator who has to explain the difference between a master claim and a
publishing claim has been failed.

The platform holds what nobody else does: a verifiable licence record with a resolvable owner set (11.08.03).
That is exactly what a claim dispute needs and exactly what creators currently cannot produce — which is why
they lose disputes they should win.

**Claim sources are heterogeneous, and the release path differs by source:**

- **Our own race** (whitelist propagated after publication) — the platform's fault; release without ceremony
- **A licensed work's co-owner via another distributor** — a real conflict inside the arrangement; the platform must fix its own house
- **A third party with no standing** — a bogus claim; the platform disputes on the creator's behalf
- **A genuine mismatch** (the creator used a different track than they licensed) — the claim is correct, and saying so plainly is the honest answer

### Happy Path

1. Creator sees a claim on a video; taps "I have a licence" from the claim or from their licence library
2. Platform matches the claimed asset against the creator's licences automatically — the creator does not identify anything
3. Platform classifies the claim source and picks the release path
4. Platform executes: whitelist repair, dispute filing, or a direct approach to the claimant
5. Claim released; monetisation restored; creator told it is done
6. If the claim was correct, the platform says so plainly rather than fighting a battle the creator will lose

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| The race: published before whitelist landed | Platform's fault. Release immediately, no questions. | "That was on us. Released." |
| Co-owner's other distributor claims it | A conflict inside the arrangement — the opted-in work is being claimed by its own owner's other agent | Platform fixes its own house; creator is not told to negotiate |
| Claimant has no standing at all | Platform disputes on the creator's behalf, using the licence record as evidence | Creator does nothing |
| Creator used a different track than licensed | **The claim is correct.** Say so. | "This is a different track from the one you licensed." Honest, unwelcome, right. |
| Claim released, then re-lands | Recurrence means the root cause was not fixed — a whitelist that will not stick, a claimant who will not stop | Must escalate, not loop. A release that re-claims is worse than no release. |
| YouTube's dispute process just says no | The platform's leverage is limited to what YouTube grants it — the same dependency as 11.06.02 DT-02 | Honest about limits; no promises the platform cannot keep |
| Creator's subscription lapsed since | **Irrelevant.** Per `#R-02` — a persisted licence is released like any other. | Subscription state is not an input here, by deliberate invariant |
| Claim on a video with 3 licensed tracks and 1 unlicensed | Partial: release what is covered, say what is not | Per-asset resolution, never all-or-nothing |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Loading | Matching the claim to a licence | Fast — the creator's revenue is stopped while this runs |
| Empty | No claims | The desired state. This feature should be invisible. |
| Populated | Claims present | Each with status and who is acting — always the platform, never "action required from you" |
| Error | Release failed | Escalated, visibly. **Never silently pending** — a stuck claim is a creator losing money in silence. |
| Partial | Some assets released | Per-asset status |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | This is customer support, not product — a queue and a human, not a feature | The sweep's implicit position: it lists micro-licensing and whitelisting and stops, which only makes sense if release is a support function | ❌ REJECTED — it is **the enforcement of the product's core promise**, and the promise is the product (`#CX-01`). Modelling it as support means the promise has no mechanism and degrades into "email us and we'll see". It also throws away the platform's structural advantage: **it holds a verifiable licence with a resolvable owner set**, which is precisely the evidence a dispute needs and precisely what creators cannot produce today. That is not a support ticket; it is the reason to buy here rather than from Epidemic. Designed as product, with support as the escalation, not the mechanism. |
| DT-02 | The creator should specify which licence covers the claim — they know what they used | Reasonable-sounding, and how every incumbent's dispute form works | ❌ REJECTED — the creator does not know, and requiring them to know is the failure. personas.md's Fan "will not tolerate professional-tool complexity" and this population is one step past that: they are running a business under a deadline with their revenue switched off. **The platform must match the claimed asset to the licence automatically** — it has the fingerprint, the purchase record, and the channel. Asking the creator to do that join is asking them to do the platform's job while panicking. One button: "I have a licence." |
| DT-03 | The most common claim source will be **the platform's own arrangement**, not outside bad actors | Reasoning from `#CX-02` and 11.06.02 DT-03: a work is opted in by owners who may *also* be distributing it through DistroKid, a label, or an aggregator whose catalogue asserts a claim on every use — and none of those know about WeJammin's licence | ✅ CONFIRMED — and it is uncomfortable, because it means **the platform will most often be disputing against its own users' other agents.** A Musician opts into the creator catalogue while their distributor claims every use of the same track. The creator is caught between two arrangements the same rights holder made. The platform cannot fix this at claim time; it has to be prevented at **opt-in** — "is this work registered for claiming anywhere else?" — which is a question nobody currently asks and which 11.06.01's opt-in flow must. **A real design consequence found by asking who actually claims.** |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | The creator's entire action is one button: "I have a licence" | Per DT-02 — the platform holds the fingerprint, the purchase and the channel; asking the creator to join them is asking them to do the platform's job |
| D-02 | Release is product, not support | Per DT-01 — the promise needs a mechanism, and the platform's licence record is the evidence nobody else has |
| D-03 | A correct claim is acknowledged plainly | Fighting a battle the creator will lose costs them time and the platform its credibility |
| D-04 | Subscription state is never an input to a release decision | Per `#R-02` — otherwise the cliff returns through the support queue after the product designed it out |
| D-05 | A re-landing claim escalates rather than loops | A release that re-claims means the root cause is unfixed; looping hides it |
