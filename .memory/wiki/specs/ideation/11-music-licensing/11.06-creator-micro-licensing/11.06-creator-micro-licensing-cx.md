## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Catalogue ↔ Whitelisting (the product's actual promise)

**Relationship**: The creator is not buying a licence. They are buying **the absence of a claim**. A track
purchased, paid for, and then flagged by Content ID has delivered the harm it was bought to prevent — and to
the creator it is indistinguishable from having stolen the music. So the purchase must not merely *permit*
use; it must reach into an external matching system and register that this asset, on this channel, is fine.

That external reach is the sub-domain's structural weakness: **it depends on a relationship with YouTube and
Meta that WeJammin does not have** (index Q-03). Every incumbent in this market has one, and it is much of
what a creator is paying for.

**Role scoping**:
- **Fan** (as creator buyer): experiences the whole sub-domain as one thing — "will this get me claimed?" They do not know or care that whitelisting is a separate mechanism.
- **Musician / Producer**: opted their work into the catalogue and must not be able to un-opt in a way that retroactively claims a licensed video
- **Operator**: not affected

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The licence is WeJammin's record; the whitelist entry lives in **YouTube's** system. Two sources of truth in two companies, and the platform controls one. Reconciliation is a permanent background obligation, not a one-time write.
2. **Trigger chain**: Purchase → whitelist write → external confirmation. **If the external write fails, the purchase must not silently complete** — the creator would be holding a licence and a live claim risk with no idea. Either the purchase fails, or it completes with a prominent, honest "whitelisting pending" state. Never silence.
3. **Permission intersection**: The rights holder's 11.04 policy governs catalogue inclusion. A work whose co-owner blocks derivative/creator use never reaches this catalogue in the first place.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Whitelist failures must reach the creator immediately — this is the one notification in the sub-domain that cannot be batched into a digest.
5. **State transition conflict**: The creator uploads their video *before* the whitelist propagates (they will; the video is done and they are impatient). The claim lands, then the whitelist arrives. **Claim release (11.06.03) exists partly for this self-inflicted race**, which is entirely predictable and not the creator's fault.

### CX-03: Persistence ↔ Whitelisting (the cliff)

**Relationship**: The defining trauma of this market. A creator subscribes, licenses 200 videos over two
years, cancels, and discovers their back catalogue is now claimable — every video they ever made, retroactively
at risk. Whether that happens is decided by whether cancellation revokes the whitelist entries.

The honest position: **a licence granted while the subscription was live must survive its cancellation**, and
the whitelist must survive with it. Anything else means the creator never owned anything — they rented the
absence of a claim, and the rent stops when the payment stops.

**Role scoping**:
- **Fan** (as creator buyer): the party who gets hurt, and who will tell everyone. This is a reputational event, not a support ticket.
- **Musician / Producer**: their catalogue is committed to videos that exist forever, for a subscription that ended. They should know that at opt-in — see 11.06.04.
- **Operator**: not affected

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The whitelist entry outlives the subscription. It is therefore **not** a property of the subscription — it is a property of the licence, which was granted permanently. Modelling the whitelist as subscription state is the bug that creates the cliff.
2. **Trigger chain**: Cancellation → **must not** cascade to whitelist revocation for already-licensed assets. It must cascade to *new* licensing capability only. Getting this cascade wrong once destroys the product's reputation permanently.
3. **Permission intersection**: A rights holder leaving the catalogue faces the same question in reverse — see 11.06.04. Their exit must not claim videos licensed while they were in.
4. **Notification fan-out**: At cancellation, the creator must be told exactly what survives and what does not, in plain terms. This is where trust is either earned or destroyed.
5. **State transition conflict**: Cancellation racing an in-flight purchase. The purchase either completed (and persists forever) or did not (and does not). No middle state can be allowed to exist.

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 11.06.01 Catalogue | 11.06.03 Claim Release | No direct edge. A claim is not raised against a *purchase*; it is raised against a **use of an asset on a channel**, which is whitelisting's domain (CX-02). Routing claim release back to the catalogue would imply the remedy is a refund — and it is not. The creator does not want their money back; they want the claim gone and their video monetised again. Conflating the two would build the wrong product: a returns desk instead of a fix. |
| R-02 | 11.06.03 Claim Release | 11.06.04 Persistence | Considered: does a lapsed subscription weaken a claim release ("your licence is over, we can't help")? Rejected as an edge — **if persistence is correct (CX-03), a claim against a persisted licence is released exactly like any other**, and subscription state is not an input to the release decision. Making it one would smuggle the cliff back in through the support queue after CX-03 designed it out of the product. The absence of this edge is a deliberate invariant, not an oversight. |
