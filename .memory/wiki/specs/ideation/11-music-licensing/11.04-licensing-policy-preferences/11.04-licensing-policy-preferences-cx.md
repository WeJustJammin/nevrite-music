## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-02 + CX-03: Individual policies ↔ the effective aggregate

**Relationship**: The three children form a lattice, not a chain. Each owner authors a policy over their own
share. The work's *effective* policy — the thing 11.02.01 actually evaluates — is a fold over all of them,
and the fold is **not symmetric**: permissions combine restrictively (all must permit) while refusals combine
absolutely (any may refuse). This asymmetry is not a design choice; it is how music rights work. Any co-owner
can refuse a licence, and no majority can overrule them. The consequence is that a work's licensability is
governed by its **most restrictive owner**, and the aggregate is a document nobody wrote.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: typically holds small shares in many works, so is more often the *subject* of someone else's aggregate than the author of their own. Must be able to see why their own policy is inert.
- **Producer**: typically holds larger master shares and is the party who discovers at deal time that a co-writer who never came to the session holds an absolute veto. Per personas.md they are the one a buyer's deadline lands on.
- **Operator**: not affected
- **Fan**: not affected directly — **but silently governed** when buying a micro-licence (11.06). The population most affected by the aggregate has no visibility into it, which is correct and worth stating.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Nobody owns the effective policy — it is derived, and must never be editable. An editable aggregate is an override, and an override lets a majority strip a co-owner's veto, which is the one thing music rights do not permit.
2. **Trigger chain**: Any owner's policy change → aggregate recompute → clearance recompute (11.02.01) → catalogue availability changes. **If the work is under a hold or in a live negotiation when this fires, both must be notified** — the same fail-open leak as `11.02-clearance-one-stop-status-cx.md#CX-01` synthesis Q2, arriving from a different direction. It recurs across the domain and is worth naming at domain level.
3. **Permission intersection**: This *is* the permission intersection. Refusals are absolute and unweighted; permissions are conjunctive. A 90% owner and a 1% owner have identical veto power, which will surprise every user who thinks in percentages and is nonetheless correct.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A policy change that kills other owners' earning capacity should tell them (CX-04). Not to enable pressure — to prevent the silent-inert failure where three people believe their catalogue is earning and it has been dead for a year.
5. **State transition conflict**: Concurrent policy edits by different owners. The fold is order-independent for refusals (absolute) but **not** for thresholds — two owners setting different auto-approve ceilings need a deterministic rule (lowest wins, per 11.04.03). Order-independence must be a property of the fold, not an accident of write order.

### CX-01: Veto ↔ Per-Work Policy

**Relationship**: The simplest and most important edge in the sub-domain: a category block beats an
auto-approve. If three of four co-writers auto-approve everything and the fourth refuses tobacco, the work
does not license to tobacco. Not at a higher price, not with three votes, not ever. Evaluation order is the
correctness property — **refusal is checked first, and if it fires, nothing else runs**.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the refusing party is most often the one with the least commercial leverage and the strongest feelings — which is exactly whose refusal this must protect
- **Producer**: must see the block early, not at deal time, or they will build a pitch on a work that cannot take it
- **Operator**: not affected
- **Fan**: not affected

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The veto and the policy are separate records with separate authors. A veto is not a value in someone's policy — it is a constraint over everyone's.
2. **Trigger chain**: Veto set → aggregate recompute → any in-flight quote or consent round for the blocked scope is **withdrawn**, not merely stopped for future requests. A veto that only applies to new requests lets an in-flight deal complete against it.
3. **Permission intersection**: An auto-approve cannot pre-authorise past a veto that did not exist when the auto-approve was set. The aggregate is evaluated at request time, not at policy-authoring time — otherwise stale permission survives a new refusal.
4. **Notification fan-out**: The vetoing party's identity is disclosed to co-owners (they need to negotiate with them) but `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]` whether to the buyer. Naming the refuser to a buyer invites off-platform pressure on the person least able to resist it.
5. **State transition conflict**: Veto set concurrently with a licence executing. **The gate's fresh re-check at issuance (11.02.01 D-04) is the serialization point** — this is exactly what that decision exists for.

| R-01 | 11.04.02 Veto | 11.04.02 Veto (veto-of-veto) | Considered: can a majority of co-owners overrule an outlier's veto — a "bad-faith veto" escape hatch for the case where one holdout kills every deal forever? Rejected as a node and as a mechanism. It contradicts how music rights actually work (co-ownership is unanimous by construction), and a platform that lets a majority strip a co-owner's refusal is building the exact instrument the Producer anti-persona would use to "push it through while contributors aren't paying attention". The commercial problem it addresses is real and is recorded as 11.02.04 Q-01 (escalation paths), where the honest answers are buyout or nothing — not override. |
