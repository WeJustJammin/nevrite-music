# Feature: Catalogue Stake Sale & Rights Finance

> **Number**: 23.06.05
> **Parent**: [Advances, Commission & Recoupment](./23.06-advances-commission-recoupment-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Sell a share of your future royalties for cash now — a permanent transfer, not a loan. The sweep flagged this candidate as speculative with securities exposure, and that flag is correct: a marketplace where investors buy income streams from named creators is a securities venue with a music skin, whatever it is called. It is retained as a node rather than deleted so the owner cuts it knowingly (parent D-04), and because the mechanics genuinely belong here — it is the same netting problem as recoupment, without an end date.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Offers a stake in a catalogue's future royalties. The population who can do this meaningfully is small — you need a catalogue with predictable royalty income, which the session-player centre of the Musician persona does not have. |
| Producer | Full | Producer points on released records are exactly the kind of predictable royalty stream a fund would buy, arguably more so than a working musician's. |
| Operator | None | Not visible. A venue holds no rights in works and has no catalogue. Explicit absence (parent Role Matrix). |
| Fan | None | Not visible **as a seller**. And the tempting inverse — letting fans *buy* stakes in artists they love — is precisely what turns this from a niche B2B instrument into a retail securities offering to consumers. See DT-03; it is the most dangerous idea in domain 23. |

## Behavior

`[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]` — deliberately not specified further at breadth. Specifying the mechanics of a securities venue before the owner has decided whether it exists would be inventing detail that is likely to be cut, and the regulatory shape determines the product shape rather than the reverse. What is known: a sold stake permanently diverts a share of future income (`#CX-04`), the ownership transfer touches domain 09's chain of title, and the instrument competes with advances for the same user need (`#CX-05`).

### Happy Path

1. Musician with a catalogue of released works wants cash and does not want debt.
2. They offer a percentage of future royalties from a defined set of works.
3. A funder assesses using royalty history (10) and the platform's ownership record (09).
4. The stake sells; ownership of that income share transfers and is recorded in 09.
5. Every future royalty line for those works diverts the sold share, forever.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| The user does not grasp that this **never ends** | An advance recoups and stops; a stake sale is permanent. A musician in a cash crisis is the worst-placed person to weigh that, and the stake sale's headline number is usually bigger | `#CX-05` — the comparison must be explicit, and the permanence stated in the loudest terms the design allows |
| Ownership transfer vs the rights record (09) | A sold stake changes the chain of title — the thing D-10 says WeJammin holds end-to-end. This is not a finance record, it is a rights record | The rights half arguably belongs to 09 entirely; see Q-02 |
| The stake is resold by the buyer | A secondary market — which is unambiguously a securities market | Out of scope at breadth; and the point at which any doubt about the classification disappears |
| Splits are contested later (24) | Selling a share of income from a work whose ownership is disputed sells something that may not be yours | The provenance thesis cuts both ways: the platform's own records are what a buyer relies on |
| A fan buys a stake | Retail investors buying income streams from named artists — see DT-03 | The line the product must not cross casually |
| Valuation is wrong | The user sells a catalogue for a fraction of its worth, permanently, because they needed rent | The failure has no remedy, and the platform hosted it |
| Tax treatment of the proceeds | A capital transaction, not income — misclassifying it corrupts 23.01 and 23.02 (the same trap as an advance) | Ties to 23.02; treatment is jurisdiction-specific and materially complex |
| The musician later wants it back | They cannot. There is no unwind | Permanence, again — the feature's defining property |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | This is a marketplace feature, and WeJammin already builds marketplaces (D-05, D-06, D-14) | The consolidation instinct — a third marketplace alongside gear, digital goods and services | ❌ REJECTED: the other marketplaces trade **goods and labour**. This one trades a **share of future income from an asset**, which is the textbook definition of a security in most jurisdictions. The resemblance to D-14's marketplace family is superficial and actively misleading — it makes a regulated financial venue look like a fourth listing type. The sweep's own "securities exposure" flag was right, and the domain map's marketplace pattern should not be allowed to smuggle it in. |
| DT-02 | A domain expert would expect the **provenance record to be what makes this possible** — and that is the trap | Any catalogue buyer: the reason catalogue deals take months and cost fortunes in due diligence is that nobody can prove who owns what. WeJammin's whole thesis (D-10, D-18) is holding the ownership record end-to-end — which would collapse that due diligence and make small catalogues tradeable for the first time. This is, genuinely, the most commercially valuable thing the provenance data could ever enable. | ✅ CONFIRMED — **and it is the strongest argument for extreme caution, not for building it.** The platform's provenance record becomes the thing investors rely on to price an asset. If a split is later found wrong (which 23.04.04 exists precisely to detect), WeJammin's record caused a mispriced securities transaction. The thesis asset becomes a liability the moment it underwrites a trade. The most valuable use of the provenance data is also the one that puts the platform's core asset at risk — and that tension deserves to be on the record before anyone finds it attractive. |
| DT-03 | Fans buying stakes in artists they love would be a beautiful fit — the platform has both populations (D-11) | The synergy that this domain map makes almost irresistible: fans are first-class users, artists need money, connect them | ❌ **REJECTED, emphatically.** This is retail investors buying unregistered securities in named individuals, sold to them by a platform that also runs the artist's fan relationship. It stacks: securities registration, retail suitability, promotion rules, and a conflict where the platform profits from both the artist's fanbase and the sale of their income. Add D-13's point that fan-scale reach brings statutory duties, and this is the single most dangerous idea in domain 23. Recording it explicitly **because** it is the obvious next thought once D-11 and this feature sit in the same product — someone will propose it, and it needs to already have been rejected in writing with reasons. |
