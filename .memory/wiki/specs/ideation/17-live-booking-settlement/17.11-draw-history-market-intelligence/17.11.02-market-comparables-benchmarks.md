# Feature: Market Comparables & Benchmarks

> **Number**: 17.11.02
> **Parent**: [Draw History & Market Intelligence](./17.11-draw-history-market-intelligence-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

What acts like this draw in markets like this, and what rooms like this pay — aggregated across many
artists' settled shows. It is the most commercially valuable thing the settlement corpus could
produce and the most dangerous: **it is built entirely from other people's confidential data**, and
none of them agreed to inform a rival's decision.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Reports | Aggregate only — "acts in your genre draw 250-400 at 400-cap rooms in Manchester". **Never another artist's identifiable numbers.** Uses it to know whether an offer is fair, which is the first time that has been possible without an agent's private knowledge. |
| Producer | None | No role. |
| Operator | Reports | Aggregate only — never a competing promoter's deal terms. Uses it to size an offer for an act with no local history. |
| Fan | None | Never visible. |

## Behavior

Comparables aggregate draw records (17.11.01) across artists, markets, room sizes, genres and slots —
crossing a **confidentiality gate** (CX-01) that is the feature's defining constraint rather than a
filter on it.

Aggregation thresholds (never render a cell below N shows, never attribute, never allow
differencing) address *identifiability*. They do not address **consent**: the promoter whose
settlement is in the denominator did not agree to size a rival's offer, and no threshold changes that.

And in a thin market the thresholds barely work anyway: "average guarantee, this genre, 400-cap rooms,
Manchester" is realistically three promoters. An Operator who knows the market — and they all do —
can often name them.

**This feature cannot be built until index Q-01 is answered by the owner.** It is not an
implementation detail.

### Happy Path

1. A corpus of settled shows accumulates across many artists and rooms.
2. An Operator sizing an offer for an act with no local history requests a comparable.
3. System aggregates matching records above the threshold, honouring the consent policy.
4. Operator sees an aggregate range with its sample size and confidence — never a source.
5. That range seeds guidance (17.11.03) and the offer's modelling (17.03.02).

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Thin market — the cell is three promoters | Threshold satisfied, anonymity not. An insider can name them | `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]` — thresholds are not a privacy model (DT-02) |
| Differencing attack — query twice, subtract | Aggregates leak individuals when the population moves by one | Requires real disclosure control, not a minimum count |
| Cold start — four settled shows in the corpus | A benchmark from four shows is worse than none; it will be believed | Must refuse rather than render (D-03); this is the launch state (index Q-02) |
| A promoter learns their deal informed a rival's offer | The reputational failure mode; entirely foreseeable | The policy must be decided *before* launch, not after the complaint |
| Reciprocity model: see benchmarks only if you contribute | The obvious design — and coercive | `[PENDING — Step 5]` — see DT-03 |
| Source settlement amended after feeding a benchmark | The benchmark is already spent; someone made an offer on it | `[PENDING — Step 5]` (CX-01) |
| Aggregate written back onto an artist's record | Would manufacture a number with no event behind it | Structurally forbidden (sub-domain R-02) |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Comparables are the payoff — the settlement corpus's most valuable output | The domain index's claim that settlement is "the source dataset for draw intelligence" | ⚠️ **PARTIALLY CONFIRMED — and it is the payoff the platform may not be entitled to take.** The data is real, uniquely held, and commercially precious. It also belongs to parties who handed it over to settle a show, not to inform a competitor. **The platform's ability to build this is not the same as its right to**, and monetising other people's confidential deal terms is exactly the behaviour that would make professionals distrust a platform they are otherwise happy to work in. `meta/problem-statement.md` Q-02 asks whether the lock-in is "earned or hostile"; this feature is where that question becomes concrete about *other people's* data. |
| DT-02 | Aggregation thresholds make it safe | Standard privacy engineering — never show a cell below N | ❌ REJECTED: **thresholds address identifiability, not consent, and in this market they barely address identifiability.** "Average guarantee, this genre, 400-cap rooms, Manchester" is three promoters; an insider names them. Differencing attacks work on aggregates when the population is small and moves. Live music markets are thin by nature — and treating a threshold as the answer would ship a privacy claim the product cannot honour. Real disclosure control is a floor, not a solution, and the consent question survives it intact. |
| DT-03 | A reciprocity model solves consent — contribute to consume | The obvious design, used by real benchmarking products | ❌ REJECTED as a *solution*, though it may be the eventual mechanism. Reciprocity is **coercive** when the platform is where the work happens: an Operator does not meaningfully consent to sharing their deal terms if refusing means losing the intelligence their competitors have. That is consent extracted by market position, which is precisely the "hostile lock-in" `meta/problem-statement.md` flags for the owner. It may still be the right answer — but it must be chosen with that named, not adopted because it is the pattern everyone uses. |
