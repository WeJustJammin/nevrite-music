# Feature: Shared Clock, Click & Count-In

> **Number**: 08.01.03
> **Parent**: [Latency Budget & Playability](./08.01-latency-budget-playability-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Answers the question every network jam room silently gets wrong: *whose time is real?* When latency
between endpoints is asymmetric, a single centrally-generated click arrives at different wall-clock
moments for different players, so "everyone plays to the click" quietly means "everyone plays to a
different click." This feature owns the timing reference — who generates it, who is compensated, and
what a count-in means when there is no shared now.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Producer | Full | Owns the room's timing. Sets tempo and time signature, selects or accepts the timing model (symmetric vs conductor), designates which player is the timing reference under the conductor model, and starts the count-in. This is the persona who runs the room, so the clock is theirs. |
| Musician | Read-only | Hears the click and the count-in; sees the tempo, the time signature and — importantly — an honest statement of *whose* time they are hearing and what delay they are being asked to accept. Can adjust their own click level and sound locally. Cannot change tempo or the timing model; that would be two people steering. |
| Operator | None | Not visible. A studio's physical click/cue infrastructure is their asset, but the network room's timing reference is the Producer's instrument. If an Operator is running the session, they are acting as Producer — per `meta/personas.md` these are lenses on behaviour, not account types. |
| Fan | None | Not visible. No fan path into a jam room. |

## Behavior

`[PARTIAL]` — the timing model is an owner decision (parent index Q-03), and the honest state of this
feature is that the *product* choice determines almost everything about the behaviour.

The two candidate models and their real trade-offs:

| Model | How it works | What it costs the player |
|-------|-------------|--------------------------|
| **Symmetric** | Everyone hears everyone else late by their pairwise latency. No player is privileged. | Every player hears a slightly different ensemble. Works only when latency is genuinely low; degrades into mush as it rises. Nobody has to accept an unnatural self-monitoring delay. |
| **Conductor** | One endpoint's time is authoritative. Other players accept a deliberate local delay so that what the conductor hears is coherent. | The non-conductor players hear themselves late — which is musically unpleasant and takes real acclimatisation, but is a known technique. It buys a much wider workable radius. |

The count-in is where the model becomes visible to the player, which makes it the right place to be
honest about it rather than hiding the compromise behind a "3, 2, 1".

### Happy Path

1. Producer sets tempo and time signature for the room.
2. System reads the playability verdict (08.01.02) and proposes a timing model, showing the trade-off in plain language rather than as a setting name.
3. Producer accepts or overrides; under the conductor model, designates the reference player.
4. System tells every player, before anything starts, whose time they are on and what delay they are accepting.
5. Producer starts the count-in; system places the click per endpoint using each one's directional delay so that the *reference* hears a coherent downbeat.
6. Players play.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Directional delay unknown (only RTT available) | The click cannot be honestly aligned. System must say so rather than align on a halved RTT and pretend. | "We can't align the click precisely on this route — expect drift." |
| The designated conductor drops out mid-take | The room loses its timing reference. Must NOT silently promote another player mid-take — that would shift everyone's time under their hands. | `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]` — likely: stop, preserve the take, re-designate. |
| Timing model changed mid-take | Blocked. Changing the timing reference while people are playing is destructive. It is a prompt for the next take, never a live action. See parent CX-02. | "This will change after the current take." |
| Latency drifts during a take, breaking alignment | `[PENDING — Step 5]` — whether the click re-aligns live (which itself is an audible timing change) or holds and lets it drift. Both are bad; this is a genuine product choice. | `[PENDING]` |
| A player mutes their click and relies on the ensemble | Permitted. Under the conductor model this is how experienced players actually work. Not an error state. | No warning. |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Empty | No tempo set | "Set a tempo to start." |
| Configuring | Producer choosing model | Model choice with the trade-off stated, not just named. |
| Counting in | Count-in started | Visual + audible count with the beat number. |
| Running | Take underway | Tempo, bar/beat position, timing model, whose time you are on. |
| Reference lost | Conductor endpoint dropped | `[PENDING — Step 5]` |
| Error | Alignment not possible on this route | Explicit "click is not aligned" warning — never a silent misalignment. |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | A domain expert expects the shared-clock problem to be **named** — a centrally-generated click on an asymmetric-latency network does not arrive at a shared moment, so "play to the click" is incoherent without a stated timing model. The candidate list omitted this entirely. | Domain reasoning: this is the central unsolved problem in network jamming and the reason JackTrip-family tools expose "delayed feedback"/conductor techniques rather than pretending a click is universal. | ✅ CONFIRMED — added as a Deep Think node. A jam-room product that ships a click without deciding whose time is real has shipped a bug wearing a metronome. |
| DT-02 | The count-in is the right place to expose the timing compromise to players. | Reasoning: it is the one moment every player is already looking at the same thing, waiting, and receptive to a one-line statement. Burying "conductor mode: you hear yourself 22 ms late" in a settings panel guarantees nobody reads it and everybody blames the platform when it feels wrong. | ✅ CONFIRMED — honesty at the count-in is cheap; confusion mid-take is expensive. |
| DT-03 | This feature could be delivered by simply syncing everyone to NTP and generating a click locally on each endpoint. | Naive engineering framing. | ❌ REJECTED — synchronised *clocks* do not solve the problem. Even with perfect wall-clock agreement, each player still hears the others late by the transit delay, so a locally-generated in-phase click means each player hears the click aligned with their own playing and misaligned with everyone else's arriving audio. The problem is not clock agreement; it is that there is no shared *acoustic* now. This rejection is worth keeping because it is the mistake an implementer will otherwise make. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | The timing model is a stated product commitment, not a hidden default | Both models cost the player something real. Hiding the cost produces users who think the product is broken. |
| D-02 | The timing model cannot change mid-take | Shifting the timing reference while people play is destructive to the take and to the players. |
| D-03 | If directional delay is unknown, the system declares the click unaligned rather than aligning on RTT/2 | RTT/2 is wrong on asymmetric routes, and a confidently-wrong click is worse than an admittedly-imprecise one. |
