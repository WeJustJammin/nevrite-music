# Feature: Gear Collection & Visibility

> **Number**: 15.04
> **Parent**: [Gear Registry & Ownership](./gear-registry-ownership-index.md)
> **Status**: [PARTIAL]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-17

## Overview

The owner-facing view of everything they have registered — currently owned, formerly owned, out on
loan — and the controls governing who can see any of it. Reverb's My Collection and Discogs'
Collection prove the retention behaviour the domain rationale cites: people maintain these lists
for years with no transaction attached, which makes this the domain's daily-use hook. It also
carries the domain's sharpest hazard, because a public list of what you own and what it's worth is
a shopping list for someone who wants to steal it.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Their gear list — the thing they actually open the domain for. Owned, formerly owned (a CV of gear, and the part that accrues to *them* rather than to the objects), out on loan (15.08). Sets visibility per item, not per account. Sees an aggregate value from 15.05, which is the number they'd rather not think about and definitely don't want public. |
| Producer | Full | Same, with a bigger and more valuable list, and a stronger reason to publish a slice of it: a Producer's gear list is marketing — clients choose rooms by what's in them. The tension between publishing for work and hiding for safety is sharpest here. |
| Operator | Full | Their personal collection, distinct from the org's asset register (15.07). The distinction matters because an engineer's own mics living in a studio are neither the studio's asset nor safely public. |
| Fan | Read-only | Sees a public collection if the owner published one — an artist's rig is genuinely interesting to fans, and this is one of the few places domain 15 touches domain 20. Sees exactly what was published: no serials, no values, no addresses. |

## Behavior

The collection is a list view over gear records with ownership states, filters, and visibility
controls. Nothing here is novel — the novelty is that **visibility must be evaluated across the
union of what WeJammin knows**, not per-surface.

**The exposure hazard.** A public collection says what you own and roughly what it's worth. Domain
17/18 says where you'll be next Tuesday and, by implication, that you won't be home. Domain 01 says
what city you live in. Domain 20 says which fans follow you. Each is individually reasonable and
each is a decision the user made in a different part of the product on a different day. Composed,
they are a targeting package: *this person owns £40k of vintage guitars, lives in Bristol, and is
in Berlin until the 14th.* Van and venue theft is endemic — the domain rationale says so — and this
feature could make it materially easier. That is not a hypothetical privacy concern; it is a
physical-safety one, and no single feature's privacy settings can see it.

### Happy Path

1. Musician opens their collection: 34 items, grouped by type, with an aggregate value shown privately.
2. They publish three signature instruments to their public profile because it's part of their identity as a player.
3. System evaluates the publication against what else is public about them and warns: their tour dates are public, so publishing high-value gear tied to their name and city creates an exposure they may not have intended.
4. They publish the three anyway — informed, which is the point — and the values and serials stay hidden.
5. Fans see the rig on their profile (domain 20). Nobody sees the other 31 items or any serial.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Public collection + public tour dates + public city | The exposure composition | An explicit warning at publication that names the composition — not a generic privacy notice |
| Formerly-owned items | Retained; they're the owner's gear CV | "Previously owned" section; also feeds gear discography (15.09), since the record they played on doesn't stop being true when they sell the guitar |
| Gear out on loan | Owned but not held | Shown with custody state (15.08); a theft report needs to know it's meant to be elsewhere |
| Aggregate value on a public collection | Never published | Values are private by default and not publishable as an aggregate at all — see D-02 |
| Item published, then flagged stolen | Flag state visible; publication becomes a recovery asset rather than a liability | Flag renders on the public item |
| Owner publishes everything without thinking | Foreseeable — the default matters more than the control | Default is private; publication is per-item and deliberate |
| Serial visible in a user-uploaded photo | The control is bypassed by the photo itself | `[PENDING — /create-prd-security, Q-02]` — a real hole, since photographing the serial is what 15.01.01 asks for |
| Fan opens a direct link to an item the owner never published (or later un-published) | Access denied at the item level, not the profile level — visibility is per-item (D-01), so a valid link to one public item grants nothing about a private one | "This item isn't public" — never a value, serial, or "exists but hidden" leak that would confirm ownership |
| Owner flips an item public → private while a Fan is viewing the cached public profile | Server-evaluated visibility wins; the next fetch/refresh resolves to denied. Stale render is tolerated only until the client re-reads | Item disappears on refresh; no partial data (value/serial) is ever served from the private state |
| Automated scraping of many public collections to assemble a cross-user target list | The individual-item abuse the composition warning cannot stop; rate limiting and no-aggregate-value (D-02) blunt it, but a determined scraper still reads what was deliberately published | `[PENDING — /create-prd-security]` — public-collection scraping defence (rate limits, per-item exposure only, no sortable value) is a platform T&S concern (domain 24), not settable here |
| Two of the owner's roles (personal + org via 15.07) claim the same physical unit | Personal collection and org asset register are distinct records (D-01 scope); the same serial surfacing in both is a custody/ownership conflict, not a visibility one | Item shown once in the personal collection with its own visibility; org-register duplication resolved in 15.07, referenced not merged |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | A public gear collection is a low-risk social feature | It's how Reverb and Discogs treat it, and it's what an artist wants for their profile | ❌ REJECTED — **this is the domain's most serious cross-domain hazard.** Individually, a public collection, public tour dates (17/18), and a public home city (01) are all reasonable and all user-chosen. Composed, they say: *this person owns £40k of guitars, lives here, and is demonstrably not home until the 14th.* The domain rationale establishes that van and venue theft is endemic — this feature could make it targeted. Critically, **no single feature's privacy settings can detect this**, because each decision was made in a different domain on a different day; the composition has no owner. This forces a platform-level exposure cross-cut, and it is why this feature owns visibility rather than deferring it to a global privacy setting. |
| DT-02 | Aggregate collection value is a natural thing to show | It's the number every collector wants, and Reverb shows it | ✅ CONFIRMED privately, ❌ REJECTED publicly — and it must not be publishable even optionally. A public "£47,000 of gear" attached to a named person with a tour schedule is a *ranked* target list, and it lets an attacker sort the whole user base by what's worth stealing. The number is real and useful to the owner (it drives insurance, per 15.05.03); it just has no safe public form. Offering it as a toggle would mean some users turn it on, which is enough. |
| DT-03 | "Formerly owned" is clutter that should be archived away | It's gear they don't have — why keep it in the collection? | ❌ REJECTED — it is one of the more valuable things here. It is the owner's gear CV ("I owned a '65 Jazzmaster for a decade"), and it is what makes provenance accrue to the **person** rather than only to the object: a musician's history of stewardship is itself a credential, and it's the closest gear analogue to the credits graph the platform is built on. It also underpins gear discography (15.09) — the record their old Tele played on doesn't stop being true when they sell it. Selling gear does not erase having owned it, and treating it as deletion would throw away the domain's only person-shaped asset. |
| DT-04 | A wishlist / "wanted" list belongs in the collection | Reverb bundles it; it feels like part of the same feature | ❌ REJECTED as a child of this domain — wanting gear is a **marketplace** behaviour (saved searches, want ads, price alerts) with a transaction intent, and it belongs to domain 13. It has no registry semantics: there is no object, no identity, no chain, no owner. Bundling it here would drag marketplace discovery into a provenance domain and blur a boundary D-14 already drew deliberately. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | Visibility is per-item and private by default | Per DT-01. Account-level visibility is too blunt: the same person reasonably wants their signature Tele public and their entire pedalboard invisible. |
| D-02 | Aggregate value has no public form, not even opt-in | Per DT-02. A toggle means some users enable it, which is enough to produce a sortable target list. |
| D-03 | Publication warns on the *composition*, naming the specific exposure | Per DT-01. A generic "this will be public" notice is useless — the hazard is invisible precisely because it spans domains, so the warning must name what else is public about them. |
