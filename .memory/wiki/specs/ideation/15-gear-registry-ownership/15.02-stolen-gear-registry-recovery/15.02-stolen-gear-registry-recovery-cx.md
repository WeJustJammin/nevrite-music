## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Theft Report ↔ Point-of-Sale Screening

**Relationship**: Reports write flags; screening reads them. The dependency is trivial; the
interesting property is what happens when the set is empty or sparse — which is its state for the
platform's entire first phase. A "no match" result carries information only in proportion to
registry coverage, and the render must carry that proportion with it. This is what forces D-01's
wording and what makes screening's MoSCoW placement dependent on flag-registry population rather
than on the screening feature's own complexity.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: files reports (van theft) and reads screening results (buying used). Both sides.
- **Producer**: files on studio break-ins; reads when buying outboard.
- **Operator**: files on venue theft, which is endemic and often insider; reads when acquiring backline.
- **Fan**: read-only on the screening result at a listing. They will over-read a green result — which is exactly why the wording matters.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The flag set is owned by 15.02.01. Screening is a pure reader. No conflict.
2. **Trigger chain**: Screening fires at listing creation and again at purchase (state can change in between — a listing may sit for weeks). If the flag store is unreachable, screening must fail **loud**, not silently pass. A silent pass is indistinguishable from a clean result and is the domain's worst failure.
3. **Permission intersection**: Screening does not reveal the reporter's identity to the seller. A flag hit tells the seller their listing is blocked, not who accused them.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A hit notifies the reporter (their gear surfaced — the emotional peak of the entire domain), the seller, and moderation (domain 24).
5. **State transition conflict**: A flag filed while a listing is live must retro-screen open listings, not only new ones.

### CX-02: Theft Report ↔ Flag Lifecycle

**Relationship**: A flag is filed at a moment of crisis and then abandoned. The gear is recovered,
or the insurance pays out and the owner mentally closes it, and the flag stays lit forever. Every
stale flag is a future false positive on an innocent seller. The lifecycle must therefore be driven
by the system (periodic reconfirmation, insurance-payout linkage) rather than by the reporter's
goodwill, because the reporter has no remaining incentive.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician / Producer / Operator**: file, then never return. This is not negligence — it is the predictable shape of the incentive.
- **Fan**: no visibility.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The flag's state machine is owned by 15.02.04; 15.02.01 only creates the flag.
2. **Trigger chain**: An insurance payout (15.05.03) transfers ownership to the insurer in most policies — which should *change* the flag rather than clear it, since the gear is still stolen but the claimant is now different. `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]`
3. **Permission intersection**: Only the reporter (or moderation) can withdraw a flag; a possessor cannot clear a flag on gear they hold, or theft would self-launder.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Reconfirmation prompts must be rare enough not to be noise and frequent enough to prevent rot.
5. **State transition conflict**: Gear recovered *and* independently sold on: the flag's terminal state and the chain's transfer record can disagree.

### CX-03: Sighting Reports ↔ Flag Lifecycle

**Relationship**: The most delicate interaction in the domain. Stolen gear reaches good-faith
buyers — that is how fencing works. When such a buyer sees "this is reported stolen" and comes
forward, they are simultaneously the platform's best evidence source and the party who stands to
lose the instrument they paid for. If the flow treats them as a suspect, they will quietly relist
it elsewhere and the gear vanishes. If it treats them as a victim (which they are), it gets
recovery cooperation and a witness to where they bought it — which is the lead that actually
matters.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the archetype on both ends — the person robbed, and the person who unknowingly bought it.
- **Producer / Operator**: same, usually at higher value.
- **Fan**: may report a sighting; must never be drawn into the dispute.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: A sighting does not mutate the flag; it attaches evidence to it.
2. **Trigger chain**: Sighting → the possessor is invited into the dispute flow, not accused in it.
3. **Permission intersection**: The reporter and possessor must never get each other's contact details directly. Brokered only — see 15.02.03 DT-01.
4. **Notification fan-out**: The owner is notified their gear surfaced. This is a high-emotion moment and the single most likely point of vigilante behaviour in the product.
5. **State transition conflict**: Multiple sightings of one item, some false; sightings on gear whose flag was already withdrawn.

### CX-04: Screening ↔ Flag Lifecycle

**Relationship**: A flag has states, and only some of them justify blocking a sale. `Reported` is
an allegation by one party. `Contested` means someone with a claim disputes it. Rendering either
as "stolen" at point of sale states as fact what the platform does not know, and does so on a page
that names a seller — which is defamation-shaped. The screening render must be a function of flag
*state*, not flag existence.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician / Producer / Operator**: as sellers, they can be flagged by a hostile party and need the contested state to be legible, not fatal.
- **Fan**: as a buyer, needs "someone disputes ownership of this" — which is enough to walk away, and is true.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: None — screening reads state it does not own.
2. **Trigger chain**: A state change must re-render live listings, not only new screens.
3. **Permission intersection**: The buyer sees flag *state*; they do not see the parties or the evidence.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A state change notifies both parties and any live listing's watchers.
5. **State transition conflict**: A flag contested mid-checkout. The purchase and the state change can race. `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]`

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 15.02.02 Point-of-Sale Screening | 15.02.03 Sighting Reports | Both read the flag set, but neither triggers or mutates the other. Screening is system-initiated at listing time and blocks a transaction; a sighting is an out-of-band human observation with no transaction attached. They are independent readers of shared state. Considered because a screening *hit* looks like a sighting — but it isn't: the platform already knows about that listing, so no observation has been added. |
| R-02 | 15.02.01 Theft Report | 15.02.03 Sighting Reports | Considered and rejected as a two-way cross-cut. A sighting presupposes a flag and attaches to it; the relationship is a one-way read through state 15.02.01 owns, with no feedback into report filing. The genuinely interesting coupling is sighting → dispute (CX-03), which is recorded. |
