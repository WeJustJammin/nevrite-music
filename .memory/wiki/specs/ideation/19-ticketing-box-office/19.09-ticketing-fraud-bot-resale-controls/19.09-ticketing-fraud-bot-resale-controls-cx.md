## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Purchase Limits ↔ Transfer Controls

**Relationship**: A per-buyer limit of 4 means nothing on its own. Buy 4, transfer them to a second account, buy 4 more, repeat. The limit constrains a *purchase*, not an accumulation, and transfer is what converts one into the other.

This means the two features are one control wearing two names: a limit without transfer discipline is theatre, and transfer discipline without a limit has nothing to protect.

**Role scoping**:
- **Operator**: sets both, and must understand they are one decision.
- **Fan**: experiences the limit at purchase and the transfer rule when they try to give a ticket to a friend — which is the overwhelming majority of transfers and the reason this cannot simply be locked down.
- **Musician**: configures the policy for their show; it is their fans on both sides.
- **Producer**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The buyer's accumulated holding for a show, which is only computable if identity is stable — and identity is the whole difficulty (19.09.01 DT-01).
2. **Trigger chain**: Purchase → limit check against holdings → transfer out → holdings drop → limit re-opens. If transfer decrements the sender's holdings, the limit is trivially defeated; if it does not, a fan who genuinely gave a ticket to a friend cannot buy a replacement.
3. **Permission intersection**: Yes — a locked-transfer show makes the limit real without any detection at all. Structural over detective (parent D-03).
4. **Notification fan-out**: No.
5. **State transition conflict**: Concurrent purchases across accounts racing one limit. `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]`

### CX-02: Transfer Controls ↔ Resale & Price Caps

**Relationship**: The pair that decides whether this sub-domain has any power at all. **Resale is transfer with money attached** — and the money moves somewhere WeJammin cannot see. A fan sells on StubHub or Twickets or Facebook; the buyer pays £200 for an £18 ticket; the execution is a WeJammin transfer, which the platform sees as a friend giving a friend a ticket.

Therefore: transfer policy *is* resale policy. A platform that permits free transfer has permitted uncapped resale, regardless of whether it operates a marketplace, and regardless of what its resale price cap says.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the party with the strongest interest — their fans, their price, their name on the £200 listing.
- **Fan**: both the seller who genuinely cannot attend and the scalper, and the platform cannot tell them apart from the transfer alone.
- **Operator**: executes the policy.
- **Producer**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The ticket's holder. Transfer changes it; resale changes it and moves money elsewhere.
2. **Trigger chain**: Off-platform resale → on-platform transfer → the platform sees only the transfer. **The money is invisible**, which is why a price cap on a marketplace WeJammin operates does not constrain a market it does not.
3. **Permission intersection**: Locking transfer forecloses resale entirely — and also forecloses the fan who genuinely cannot go (19.09.02 DT-02).
4. **Notification fan-out**: A transfer notifies the recipient; nothing reveals whether money changed hands.
5. **State transition conflict**: A refund on a transferred ticket — payer and holder are different people (19.06.01 Q-03).

### CX-03: Purchase Limits ↔ Resale & Price Caps

**Relationship**: `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]`. Confirmed at breadth: the two attack the scalper's economics from opposite ends. Limits raise the cost of acquiring inventory; a capped face-value exchange removes the margin that made acquiring it worthwhile. Neither works well alone — a limit without a cap just makes scarce inventory more valuable, and a cap without a limit lets one actor hoover up the allocation and sit on it.

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 19.09.01 Bot Mitigation | Generic fraud detection engine | Not a rejected pair but a rejected *node*: a generic fraud engine was considered as a child here and routed out as a cross-cut (parent D-02). A stolen card, a fake account and a chargeback behave identically whether the purchase is a ticket, a guitar (13) or a plugin (14). What is ticketing-specific is the scarcity regime — bots exist here because tickets are dated, finite and resellable at a markup, which is not true of a plugin licence. |
| R-02 | 19.09.02 Transfer Controls | 19.03 Guest List & Comps | Considered because a comp is arguably a transfer — the artist gives a ticket to a person. Rejected: a comp is *issued*, not transferred. It originates from an allocation, has no payer, and is created for a named person rather than moving between two. The mechanics, the money and the accountability are all different — 19.03.03's whole subject is who authorised the issuance, a question a transfer never asks. |
| R-03 | 19.09.03 Resale | 19.06.01 Individual Refunds | Genuinely tempting, and worth stating why it is rejected as a *cross-cut* while remaining an important adjacency. A face-value exchange and a refund solve the same fan problem — "I can't go" — and 19.06.01 Q-02 explicitly asks whether resale is the better answer than a refund right. But they are not coupled: the exchange finds a new buyer and the seat never returns to the venue's pool; the refund returns the seat and the money. Different money, different inventory path, different party bearing the cost. The relationship is a product *choice* between them, not a runtime interaction — which makes it a MoSCoW question rather than a CX entry. |
