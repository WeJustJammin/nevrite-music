# Feature: Sales Pacing & On-Sale Health

> **Number**: 19.05.03
> **Parent**: [Box Office Counts, Drops & Day-of-Show](./19.05-box-office-counts-drops-index.md)
> **Status**: [PARTIAL]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-17

## Overview

Answers one question: **is this show going to be fine, and if not, is there still time to do something about it?** Pacing reads the count over time against a trajectory and turns "we're at 180" into "we're at 180, which is 40% behind where this room usually is at four weeks out".

The value is entirely in the timing. A show that is 40% behind at four weeks out is recoverable — release the holds, open a discount window, spend on promo, add a support act with a local following. The same fact at four days out is a description of a bad night that everyone already suspected.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Reports | Sees pacing for their own show, in the drop (19.05.02). Their available actions are different from the Operator's and mostly promotional — post about it, activate their fanbase (domain 20), pull in a local support. Cannot release holds or change price. |
| Producer | None | Not visible. No box-office surface. |
| Operator | Full | Sees pacing across their shows, configures the trajectory/target, receives the alerts, and holds the actions — hold release (19.01.02), discount window (19.02.02), promo spend (domain 21). |
| Fan | None | Never sees this. A fan learning that a show is pacing badly is a fan who decides not to go. |

## Behavior

Pacing compares the count's trajectory against a reference. The reference can be:
- **The show's own target** — the number the deal was built on (domain 17 guarantee + costs = the break-even count).
- **The room's history** — how this venue's shows on this day of week typically pace.
- **Comparables** — how similar artists in similar rooms have paced. This is **domain 22's data**, consumed here, not owned here (parent D-05).

**The reference is a curve, not a line.** Real ticket sales are heavily back-loaded — a large share of a show's tickets sell in the final week, with a spike on announce and a spike at the deadline and a long quiet middle. A linear "you should be at 50% by the halfway point" model alarms on nearly every healthy show and destroys the feature's credibility in its first month (Q-01, DT-03). The reference therefore models the expected *cumulative* curve to a given days-out mark, and pacing is the gap between the actual count and where the curve says a healthy show of this type sits at this many days out — not a straight-line division of the target by the calendar. The exact curve family and its parameters are `[PENDING — /write-be-spec: the specific curve shape, per-genre/per-room fitting, and cold-start default before any room history exists]`; the behavioral commitment at this level is that the model is back-loaded and time-shaped, never linear.

**Thresholds are anchored to the deal, refined by history.** The primary alert threshold is a percentage gap against the break-even trajectory (D-02) — the deal is per-show, so the threshold is per-deal by default, not a single global number that a stadium and a 200-cap room would both fail (Q-03). Where room history exists it tightens the reference curve; it does not replace the break-even anchor. Exact percentages and how many bands of alert exist (e.g. "watch" vs "act now") are `[PENDING — /write-be-spec + Operator configuration]`; the commitment here is per-deal anchoring, Operator-adjustable, never a hardcoded global.

Alerts fire on thresholds, and every alert must name an action. An alert that says "your show is behind" and nothing else is a notification that trains dismissal.

### Happy Path

1. Show goes on sale; system establishes the trajectory reference — break-even count from the deal, plus the room's history where available.
2. Count accumulates; pacing is computed against the reference.
3. At four weeks out, the show is 40% behind. Threshold fires.
4. Alert reaches the Operator **with the actions attached**: 30 tickets are still held (19.01.02) — release them; the fan-club window never opened — open it; promo spend is at zero.
5. Operator releases the holds from inside the alert, without going to find another screen (`#CX-02` Q2).
6. Musician sees the same pacing in their next drop, scoped to their view, with their own action: activate the fanbase.
7. Pacing recovers, or it does not — but nobody found out on the night.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| New room, no history | The most common case for a growing platform, and the one where pacing is least able to help | Fall back to the deal's break-even count. Honest: "no history for this room yet" beats a confident fabricated benchmark |
| New artist, no comparables | Same | Break-even only; state the limitation |
| Alert with no action attached | Trains the Operator to dismiss alerts | Structurally excluded — an alert names its actions or it does not fire. See DT-01 |
| Show paces badly and the alert is correct but nothing can help | Real — some shows just do not sell | The honest output is "this is where you are"; the product does not owe a fix |
| Pacing computed on an ingested count with a delivery lag (19.07) | The trajectory is measured against stale data and may alert late or wrongly | Must state freshness (19.05.01 D-02); a lagging count makes pacing less useful exactly where WeJammin does not own the rail |
| Late surge — most shows sell heavily in the final week | A trajectory that assumes linearity will alarm on every healthy show | The reference must model the real curve, which is heavily back-loaded. A naive linear model produces alerts that are always wrong. See Q-01 |
| Pacing used to justify a price increase on a hot show | Technically available, hostile to the Fan persona | Not offered; same posture as 19.02.05 D-04 |
| Alert fires after the show | Meaningless | Pacing ends at close; post-hoc analysis is domain 22's |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| No reference | On sale, no target and no history | Count only, no pacing claim. Honest absence beats a fabricated benchmark |
| Pacing | Reference established, on sale | Trajectory vs reference, with time-to-show |
| Healthy | Within threshold | Quiet. A healthy show should generate no noise at all |
| Behind — actionable | Threshold breached with time remaining | Alert naming the gap **and the available actions**, actionable from the alert |
| Behind — late | Threshold breached with no time left | Stated, without pretending an action helps |
| Ahead | Selling faster than reference | Informational; may prompt a second-night conversation with 19.02.05's waitlist depth |
| Ended | Show closed | Pacing stops; 22 takes over |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | **A pacing alert must carry its actions, or it must not fire** | Reasoning about why every operational alerting system decays into noise; the Operator persona is "on a phone at a loading dock", not at a desk with time to investigate | ✅ CONFIRMED — this is the feature's design centre and it is what separates it from a chart. An alert that says "your show is 40% behind" hands the Operator a problem and a homework assignment: go and work out what to do, on a phone, between load-in and soundcheck. They will dismiss it, and after three dismissals they will dismiss all of them. An alert that says "40% behind — **you have 30 tickets still held, release them?**" is a decision with a button, and the connection is already established because the pacing feature can see the manifest (19.01.02) and the ladder (19.02.02). The alert is not the product; the closed loop from signal to action is. Note this composes with 19.01.02 DT-02 (the held-inventory alert fires on remaining inventory, not on a date) — two features independently concluding that the alert must arrive when action is still possible. |
| DT-02 | Pacing needs comparables to be useful, so this feature depends on domain 22 having market data | Reasonable-sounding dependency | ❌ REJECTED as a blocking dependency, and the rejection changes the MoSCoW answer. The most useful reference is not a market comparable at all — it is **the deal's own break-even count**, which is already known from domain 17 the moment the show is booked: the guarantee plus the costs divided by the net per ticket. "You need 210 to break even; you're at 180 with four weeks to go" is more actionable than any market benchmark, requires no data platform, no history, and no other domain, and it works on day one for a brand-new room with a brand-new artist. Comparables (22) make it *better*; they are not what makes it *work*. Treating 22 as a prerequisite would defer a feature that is buildable immediately and would blind exactly the small rooms and new artists who have no history and need the warning most. |
| DT-03 | **The reference must model a back-loaded curve; a linear pace model is worse than none** | Real on-sale curves are front-spiked (announce), back-spiked (final week / deadline), and quiet in the middle; every promoter knows a show that is "half sold at the halfway point" is doing *extremely* well, not merely on track | ✅ CONFIRMED — this is the difference between a credible feature and one that gets muted in month one. A linear model says a show 30% sold at the calendar midpoint is 20 points behind; the real curve says that show is comfortably ahead, because most of its sales are still to come. A pacing alert that fires on healthy shows is worse than no pacing at all: it is D-04 (a healthy show makes no noise) violated by construction, and it burns the exact alert-credibility that DT-01 says is the whole product. The curve does not need to be perfect or per-genre on day one — even a single generic back-loaded curve beats linear — but linear is a defect, not a v1 simplification. Ties to the late-surge edge case: the surge is not an exception to handle, it is the shape the reference must already assume. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | An alert names its available actions and is actionable from the alert, or it does not fire | An alert that hands over a problem and a homework assignment gets dismissed, and then they all do (DT-01) |
| D-02 | The primary reference is the deal's break-even count, not a market comparable | Break-even is known on day one from domain 17, works for new rooms and new artists, and is more actionable (DT-02) |

| D-04 | A healthy show generates no noise | Quiet is a feature; alert fatigue is how the whole mechanism dies |
| D-05 | No pacing claim is made without a reference | "No history for this room yet" beats a fabricated benchmark |
| D-06 | Pacing is not exposed as a price-increase trigger | Same posture as 19.02.05 D-04 — hostile to the Fan persona |
| D-07 | The reference models a back-loaded curve, never a linear split of target ÷ calendar | A linear model alarms on healthy shows and violates D-04 by construction; the late surge is the assumed shape, not an exception (DT-03) |
| D-08 | Alert thresholds are anchored per-deal (gap vs break-even trajectory), Operator-adjustable, not a hardcoded global | A stadium and a 200-cap room cannot share one absolute threshold; the deal is the natural unit and is known day one (Q-03) |
