## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Reschedule ↔ Individual Refunds

**Relationship**: When a date moves, the default is that tickets remain valid — the show is still happening, just later. But the buyer bought a specific night, and the new one may be impossible for them. So a reschedule opens an **opt-out refund window**: a time-boxed period in which any holder may refund, no reason required, regardless of what the ordinary refund policy says.

This is the interaction that makes reschedule distinct from an edit. Moving a date does not merely change a field — it hands a new right to every person who already bought.

**Role scoping**:
- **Operator**: executes the reschedule, funds the refunds that result, and does not know how many until the window closes.
- **Fan**: gains a right they did not have. Must be told clearly: your ticket still works, and if the new date does not suit you, here is your money back, by [date].
- **Musician**: usually the cause; sees the resulting attrition, which is often severe.
- **Producer**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The ticket. Reschedule rewrites its event date; the refund window gives its holder a right against it. Same object, two writers, no conflict — but the *count* moves as refunds land, and pacing (19.05.03) is now measuring against a trajectory for a show that no longer exists on that date.
2. **Trigger chain**: Reschedule → notify every holder → open the window → refunds process individually → window closes → remaining inventory returns to sale, and should route to the waitlist first (19.02.05 D-01). If the notification fails, the window is a right nobody knew they had — which is worse than not offering it.
3. **Permission intersection**: The opt-out right overrides the ordinary refund policy for its duration. A "no refunds" policy does not survive the seller moving the date.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Every holder, and it must actually arrive. Also: wallet passes must update to the new date (19.12) or the fan turns up on the wrong night holding a pass that says so.
5. **State transition conflict**: A refund landing while the window is closing; a second reschedule during the first window's opt-out period (real, and grim).

### CX-02: Reschedule ↔ Cancellation

**Relationship**: `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]`. Confirmed at breadth: "postponed, new date TBC" is a state that can persist indefinitely, and it is commercially attractive to the seller precisely because it defers the refund obligation while holding the buyer's money. Several jurisdictions responded to the 2020 postponement wave by legislating that an indefinite postponement converts to a refund right after a defined period.

The product consequence: an indefinite postponement cannot be a resting state. It must carry a deadline by which it becomes a real date or becomes a cancellation.

**Role scoping**: **Operator** holds the money and the obligation; **Fan** holds a ticket for a show with no date; **Musician** is usually the cause.

### CX-03: Cancellation ↔ Individual Refunds

**Relationship**: Both end in money going back, and they share the rail — but they are not the same operation. An individual refund is a discretionary decision on one ticket, which can fail and be retried without consequence to anyone else. A cancellation is a single obligation discharged across every holder at once: it is not optional, it has no policy question attached, and a partial completion is an incident rather than a retry queue.

The distinction matters because modelling cancellation as a loop over the individual refund path inherits the individual path's semantics — discretion, per-case policy, per-case failure — none of which apply.

**Role scoping**:
- **Operator**: one decision, N executions, and a duty to complete all of them.
- **Fan**: does not apply for anything; the money comes back.
- **Musician**: sees the settlement consequence.
- **Producer**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The tickets and the count. Cancellation voids every ticket; individual refunds may already be in flight against some of them when it fires.
2. **Trigger chain**: Cancel → void all → refund all → settlement reversal (17) → notify all. Partial failure is the hard part: 400 refunds where 6 fail leaves six people who are owed money and do not know the system knows. This must be a tracked incident with a completion guarantee, not a queue that drains silently.
3. **Permission intersection**: Cancellation needs no policy check — the policy is irrelevant when the seller cancels.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Every holder, plus every party to the settlement, plus the waitlist (19.02.05), who were never buyers but were waiting.
5. **State transition conflict**: An individual refund in flight when the cancellation fires — must not double-refund. Genuinely a money-safety race.
