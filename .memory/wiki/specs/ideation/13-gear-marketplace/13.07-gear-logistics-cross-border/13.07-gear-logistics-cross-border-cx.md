## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: CITES ↔ Landed Cost

**Relationship**: Strict ordering. A prohibited movement has no landed cost — the question doesn't arise. Quoting duty on an instrument that cannot legally cross the border is worse than useless: it tells the buyer the sale is possible and prices it, which is an implicit assurance the platform cannot honour. CITES runs first and can terminate the whole cross-border path.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: doesn't know their guitar has Brazilian rosewood (13.07.03 Role Lens) and doesn't know duty exists (13.07.02). Both determinations are made *for* them.
- **Producer**: heaviest international buyer; hits landed cost constantly, CITES rarely (outboard has no fingerboards).
- **Operator**: may have a broker and a deferment account, changing the landed-cost arithmetic but not the CITES answer.
- **Fan**: sees "ships to you" or "doesn't", and a total. Never sees either mechanism — by design (13.07.03 D-04).

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Both read the catalog's model attributes — materials for CITES, category for tariff classification. Both are overridable at unit level (13.02.03). No write conflict; a shared read dependency on catalog correctness, which 13.01.01 DT-02 already flags as high blast radius.
2. **Trigger chain**: Destination known → CITES determination → if prohibited, stop → else landed cost. **CITES fails closed** (13.07.03 D-02); landed cost must not fall through to an unquoted sale (13.07.02 States). Both refuse rather than guess, which is unusual for this domain and correct for both.
3. **Permission intersection**: Both are seller-Config / buyer-Read. Neither can be influenced by the buyer, which matters — a buyer who *wants* the instrument badly cannot talk their way past either.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A permit-required determination means a multi-week wait the order state must tolerate (13.07.03 Edge Cases).
5. **State transition conflict**: Rules change (13.07.03 Q-02). A determination made at listing may be stale at checkout — which is why both gate at **checkout**, not at listing. A stale "permitted" is the destroy-the-instrument case.

### CX-02: Packing Standards ↔ Damage Claims

**Relationship**: The standard exists to be the claim's pivot. 13.07.01 DT-02 rejects treating packing guidance as a help article precisely because a claim needs an assessable term; 13.07.04 D-03 adjudicates on evidence rather than argument. Together they make "was it packed properly?" answerable instead of arguable — which is the only reason either is worth building.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: has never shipped a guitar; the standard is education *and* liability, delivered at the moment they're holding the tape.
- **Producer**: knows the physics, needs the evidence step not to be friction.
- **Operator**: packs at volume; the standard must be fast to comply with and fast to evidence.
- **Fan**: buyer-side only. Benefits from a mechanism they never see, and claims against a standard they never read.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The evidence pack (13.02.04) is the shared entity — packing evidence is a pack artifact, sealed at dispatch, immutable after. Neither feature owns the pack; both write to it.
2. **Trigger chain**: Label print → standard surfaced → seller packs → packing evidence into the pack → seal → transit → damage → claim reads the pack. **The chain breaks if the seller skips the evidence step**, which is permitted (13.02.04 D-02 — prompted, not blocking). That is a deliberate trade: a blocked dispatch is an angry seller; a defended seller who skipped once and lost a claim photographs forever.
3. **Permission intersection**: The seller produces the evidence that may later be used against them, which is 13.02.02 DT-02's reframe — it protects the honest seller far more often than it convicts the careless one.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Claim opened → seller, carrier, insurer, and possibly domain 24.
5. **State transition conflict**: Escrow must hold while a claim runs (13.07.04 Cross-Cut Notes), which collides with the order's auto-settle timer (13.06.02 D-02). A claim opened on the last day of the inspection window must suspend settlement — and therefore suspend ownership transfer (13.06.05). **Flagged for Step 5**: the interaction between claim-opening and auto-settle is unspecified and would silently transfer ownership of a destroyed object.

### CX-03: Freight ↔ Damage Claims

**Relationship**: The risk peaks coincide. Freight items are the heaviest, most fragile, most awkward to pack, most expensive to insure, and least replaceable — a Hammond organ or a Leslie cabinet is exactly what 13.07.04 DT-02's "money doesn't fix this" is about. Medium confidence: the correlation is obvious from both files' subject matter but no evidence yet establishes the claim *rate* on freight versus parcel.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the persona shipping their first freight item, with the least idea what could go wrong.
- **Operator**: ships freight routinely; may hold their own transit insurance, making platform insurance a duplicate cost (13.07.04 Role Lens).
- **Producer**: heavy racks; knows the risk.
- **Fan**: unlikely to buy freight items; would be devastated by the outcome.

**Synthesis questions deferred** — Medium confidence. Step 5 should establish whether freight needs a *distinct* insurance regime rather than a higher declared value: freight carriers' liability terms, claim processes and inspection requirements differ structurally from parcel, and 13.07.04 currently treats declared value as the only dial. Also open: whether the domain should actively steer irreplaceable freight items toward local pickup (13.11), which 13.07.04 DT-02 hints at and nothing currently implements.

### CX-04: CITES ↔ Freight

**Relationship**: A hypothesis. A restricted instrument moving as palletised freight combines the two worst failure modes — seizure (irreversible destruction) of a shipment that is harder to intercept, recall or inspect mid-route than a parcel. Low confidence: recorded because the *combination* is severe, not because there is evidence it is common. Most CITES-relevant items (guitars) are parcel-sized; most freight items (organs, cabinets) are less likely to contain restricted materials — the two sets may barely intersect.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician/Producer**: would be the ones affected, in the rare case (a vintage grand piano with ivory keys is the archetype and it is a real object).

**Synthesis questions deferred** — Low confidence. Step 5 should test whether the intersection is real enough to design for, starting with the ivory-keyed piano case. If it is only that one archetype, this is an edge case of CX-01, not a pair.

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 13.07.02 Landed Cost | 13.07.04 Damage Claims | Considered: a damaged import's duty should be reclaimable as part of the claim. Rejected as a *pair* — duty reclaim is a returns problem (13.07.02 Q-03, owned with 13.08.01), and a damage claim's remedy is the item's value, not its import cost. The two touch only through the order, which is composition rather than interaction. |
| R-02 | 13.07.01 Packing Standards | 13.07.03 CITES | Considered: restricted materials might need specific packing or documentation attached to the box. Rejected — the permit accompanies the shipment as paperwork, which is a carrier/broker concern (CX-M14), not a packing-standard concern. Nothing about rosewood changes how you pack a guitar. |
| R-03 | 13.07.02 Landed Cost | 13.07.01 Freight | Considered: freight cost is part of landed cost, so they interact. Rejected — landed cost *includes* freight as an input, which is composition, not interaction. Neither constrains the other's behaviour; there is no trigger, no shared state, no race. Contrast CX-01, where CITES genuinely terminates landed cost's execution. |
