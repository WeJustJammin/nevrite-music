# Feature: Waitlist & Backfill

> **Number**: 16.06.04
> **Parent**: [Space Booking & Reservations](./16.06-space-booking-reservations-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16
> **Split from**: sweep candidate 14 — backfill is a demand-side capability, not a reservation state

## Overview

Capturing the demand that arrives when a room is full, and converting it into a sale when a slot
frees up. Someone wanted Tuesday and Tuesday was taken; a week later the Tuesday booking cancels and
the Operator has a perishable slot and no idea who to call.

The Operator's inventory is time: **an unsold Tuesday is gone forever**. A cancellation is not
neutral — it is a slot that was sold and is now decaying, with the clock running. Backfill is the
only mechanism in the domain that recovers that value, and the platform is uniquely positioned to
run it because it already knows who asked.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Operator | ⚙️ Config | Enables waitlisting per room, sets how a freed slot is offered (first-come broadcast vs ranked). Sees latent demand for slots they cannot sell — which is genuinely new information about their own business. |
| Musician | Full | Joins a waitlist for a date they wanted; gets told when it frees. Also the party a released hold (16.06.01) affects most — that Tuesday was pencilled and is now real. |
| Producer | Full | Same. Studio backfill is higher-value per slot and lower-frequency; a freed studio week is worth chasing. |
| Fan | ❌ None | No visibility. Fans waitlist **tickets** (19), never rooms. |

## Behavior

A waitlist entry is an expression of demand against a **room + date/window**. When a slot frees — a
cancellation (16.06.03), an expired hold, or a released challenge (16.06.01) — waitlisted parties are
notified.

The offer mechanic is the design question. **First-come broadcast** is simple and fair-feeling, and it
means everyone gets an alert and one person wins — the rest learn the platform's alerts are usually a
waste of time. **Ranked sequential offers** are fairer and slower, and slow is fatal for a slot that
expires. The right answer probably varies with lead time: a slot freeing in 3 months can afford a
sequential ladder; a slot freeing tomorrow cannot.

**The highest-value trigger is an expired hold**, not a cancellation. Holds expire constantly and
silently (16.06.01 D-02), and each expiry is a slot the Operator had mentally sold and just got back —
usually without noticing. The platform notices.

Three things stay open and are carried as Open Questions rather than left pending here: the **offer
mechanic** (Q-01, `/create-prd` — owner), the **notification budget** (Q-02, `/create-prd-compile`),
and whether **waitlist demand data** is surfaced to Operators as an analytics product (Q-03,
`/create-prd` — owner, cross-check 22). Everything else in this feature is decided (D-01–D-04).

### Happy Path

1. Band wants a rehearsal room Tuesday 7pm; it is booked
2. Instead of a dead end, they join the waitlist — the search result becomes a captured intent
3. Tuesday's booking cancels 4 days out (16.06.03)
4. Waitlisted band is notified within seconds; books it
5. The Operator's perishable slot is resold without them lifting a finger, and they never knew it was at risk

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Slot frees 2 hours before it starts | Almost unsellable; broadcast is the only viable mechanic at that lead time | Instant broadcast |
| 40 people waitlisted, one slot | 39 disappointments. Repeatedly, and they learn to ignore the notification — the mechanic degrades itself | Depends on the offer mechanic — `[OPEN — Q-01 → /create-prd, owner]` |
| Waitlisted party no longer wants it | Stale demand is the norm — a tour date moved, a project died. **Resolved**: waitlist entries expire, like holds (D-03) | "Removed quietly" — the `Stale` state in the table below (D-03) |
| Operator sees strong latent demand for Saturdays | New, actionable information about their own business — and arguably an analytics product (22) rather than a booking feature. The depth data exists either way (D-04); whether it is shown to the Operator is the open half | `[OPEN — Q-03 → /create-prd, owner values decision]` |
| Waitlist is used to snipe a slot the moment a rival's hold lapses | Adversarial and probably fine — the hold expired, the slot is fair game. Worth naming rather than discovering later | — |
| Nobody is on the waitlist | The common case for most rooms; the feature is dormant until liquidity exists | Nothing happens |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Waitlisted | Joined | "You'll be told if this frees" |
| Offered | Slot freed, offer sent | Time-boxed offer |
| Converted | Waitlisted party booked | Normal reservation (16.06.03) |
| Expired | Offer window passed | Offer lapses; next party or broadcast |
| Stale | Entry aged out | Removed quietly |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Waitlist is a reservation state | The sweep bundled it into candidate 14 with deposits and buffers | ❌ REJECTED — hence the split. A reservation is **supply committed**; a waitlist entry is **demand captured with no supply attached**. They have different lifecycles (a waitlist entry has no room, no money, and no obligation), different failure modes, and different owners. Bundling them hid a demand-side capability inside a supply-side state machine. |
| DT-02 | Cancellations are the main backfill trigger | It is the obvious source of a freed slot | ❌ REJECTED: **expired holds are the bigger source**. Holds are how live music books (16.06.01 DT-01), most holds evaporate, and every expiry is a slot the Operator had mentally sold and silently got back. Today nobody notices — the Operator forgot the hold existed (their named anti-persona). The platform notices automatically, which makes this feature a direct beneficiary of hold TTLs. |
| DT-03 | Waitlist is a marketplace nice-to-have | Standard e-commerce back-in-stock | ❌ REJECTED on the Operator's inventory physics. Their stock is **perishable and non-fungible** — an unsold Tuesday is not "still in stock", it is destroyed. Back-in-stock alerts recover a sale that would have happened later; backfill recovers value that would have been **annihilated**. Higher stakes, and it only works if the recovery is fast. |
| DT-04 | Broadcast-to-all is the fair mechanic | Everyone gets a shot | ⚠️ PARTIALLY REJECTED: it is fast, which matters for a perishable slot, and it manufactures 39 disappointments per slot. Repeated, it trains people to ignore the notification — the mechanic destroys itself. Ranked sequential is fairer and too slow for short lead times. The likely answer is **lead-time-dependent**: sequential when there is time, broadcast when there is not. Q-01. |
| DT-05 | Waitlist data is just a queue | It is a list of people waiting | ❌ REJECTED: it is **latent demand data** — the only signal in the domain about the bookings that did *not* happen. An Operator learning that 12 bands wanted Saturdays they could not sell is being told something about their pricing (16.06.08) and their capacity that no other source can tell them. That is plausibly a 22 Analytics product, and it means this feature generates value even when it converts nothing. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | Split from candidate 14 — waitlist is demand captured, not supply committed | Different lifecycle, no room, no money, no obligation (DT-01) |
| D-02 | **Expired holds** are a first-class backfill trigger, alongside cancellations | Holds evaporate constantly and silently; the Operator forgot they existed (DT-02) |
| D-03 | Waitlist entries expire, like holds | Stale demand (a moved tour date, a dead project) is the norm, not the exception |
| D-04 | Waitlist depth is **demand data**, valuable even when it converts nothing | It is the only signal about bookings that did not happen (DT-05) |
