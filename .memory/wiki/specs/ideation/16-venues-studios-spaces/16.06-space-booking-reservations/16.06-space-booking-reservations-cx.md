## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: External Calendar Sync ↔ Availability & Holds — *the adoption pair*

**Relationship**: The most consequential pair in this sub-domain, and the one the sweep missed
entirely. 16.06.01 builds a venue-shaped calendar with ranked holds and challenges — genuinely better
than anything the Operator has. And it is **worthless, or worse than worthless, without CX-01**,
because the Operator's calendar already exists in Google Calendar and a paper diary. A better calendar
that is not their calendar is the fourth incompatible place, and the double-booking that is their
stated switching trigger becomes something the platform causes.

The direction of authority is the whole point: **we do not become the source of truth by being good.
We become useful by syncing with the source of truth that already exists.**

**Role scoping**:
- **Operator**: the only persona who touches sync, and the one whose adoption depends on it entirely.
- **Musician / Producer**: never see it; every slot they book is real because of it.
- **Fan**: none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Two calendars, both writable, both authoritative to their owner. There is no clean answer — only a stated policy (16.06.02 Q-03).
2. **Trigger chain**: Booking here → outbound event there. Event there → inbound block here. Both async, both with a race window that cannot be closed, only narrowed.
3. **Permission intersection**: The Operator's external calendar may be shared with staff who have no WeJammin account and can delete a WeJammin booking without knowing what it was.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A sync failure must be **loud** (16.06.02 D-04). A silently frozen calendar sells slots that do not exist behind a green tick.
5. **State transition conflict**: The core risk. A slot booked here and double-booked there between syncs. An inbound event cannot cancel a paid booking; it also cannot be discarded. Unresolved.

### CX-03: Compound Booking ↔ Availability & Holds

**Relationship**: A compound holds several resources with several calendars, atomically. The failure
mode is specific and severe: a compound that holds the room, fails on the engineer, and **leaves the
room held** has automated the Operator's named accidental anti-persona — "forgets to release a hold,
blocking a slot they'd happily have sold". The platform would be doing it systematically, at machine
speed, to a persona it is selling a fix to.

**Role scoping**:
- **Producer**: composes compounds constantly; hits the partial-availability case regularly.
- **Operator**: their rooms are the resource that gets orphaned by a bad compound.
- **Musician**: simpler compounds (room + backline), same mechanics.
- **Fan**: none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Each resource has its own calendar; the compound owns the atomicity across them.
2. **Trigger chain**: Hold all → confirm all, or release all. Failure anywhere releases everywhere. A failure *during release* is the nasty case — a partial release leaves exactly the orphan the design forbids.
3. **Permission intersection**: If a member is a 05-listed engineer, whose permission grants the hold? Unresolved (16.03.03 Q-03, 16.06.05 Q-04).
4. **Notification fan-out**: A compound touches the Operator and the engineer, who may be different parties with different expectations.
5. **State transition conflict**: Two compounds racing for one shared member (the same engineer, two studios). Atomic holds are the only defence.

### CX-04: Compound Booking ↔ Rate Cards & Extras

**Relationship**: The quote is computed over the compound, not read off a list. This is what makes
16.06.07 a product rather than a price page: the client selects a room, and the system adds the
mandatory assistant they did not choose (16.03.03 D-04), the control room the exclusion rules imply,
and the hire item they did pick — and shows the real total **before payment**. The industry's default
is a headline rate and an invoice with four surprises on it.

**Role scoping**:
- **Producer**: quotes their own client off this number; a wrong total costs them personally.
- **Musician**: rehearsal and small-studio booking is almost entirely a price decision.
- **Operator**: sets the parts; the total is derived, and they may not have ever seen it computed themselves.
- **Fan**: none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: None — rates are Operator-only (16.06.07 D-03), compounds are client-composed.
2. **Trigger chain**: Compose compound → resolve mandatory/implied members → price each → total. A missing price on a mandatory member is the silent failure: the quote is wrong and looks right.
3. **Permission intersection**: The community can never touch rates (16.05.03 D-03) — the domain's one absolute write rule.
4. **Notification fan-out**: None.
5. **State transition conflict**: The rate changing between quote and payment. A quote must be locked for its window (16.06.08 D-04), regardless of any pricing rule.

### CX-07 / CX-08: Recurring & Lockout ↔ Availability and Reservation — *the inversion pair*

**Relationship**: Recorded together because they say the same thing from two angles: **the entire
16.06 model does not apply to a tenancy.**

Against **16.06.01** (CX-07): a lockout room has no availability. It is not "booked" — it is off the
market. Nothing to hold, nothing to challenge, nothing to release. And a recurring series is not N
bookings on a calendar; it is a standing soft claim, closer to a permanent hold than to a set of
reservations.

Against **16.06.03** (CX-08): every reservation mechanic is wrong. Deposits → tenancy deposit. Ladder
→ notice period. Buffers → meaningless (the room is theirs). Completion → nothing completes; the
tenancy just continues. Non-payment → eviction, not a refund dispute.

**Role scoping**:
- **Operator**: lockout is their best revenue — predictable, monthly, no per-session admin, no empty-Tuesday risk. The domain's other 8 features serve their *worst* revenue.
- **Musician**: the lockout room is effectively the band's premises.
- **Producer**: a lockout that becomes a project studio inverts into supply (16.06.09 Q-02).
- **Fan**: none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: A lockout removes a room from the availability system entirely. If any other feature assumes every room has a calendar, it breaks here.
2. **Trigger chain**: None of 16.06.03's chain fires. No deposit ladder, no completion, therefore — importantly — **no harvest trigger** (16.05.06). A lockout band uses a room weekly for two years and the platform never asks them a single question about it. The domain's highest-frequency supply generates zero freshness data.
3. **Permission intersection**: A tenant is not a client; they hold keys. There is no per-session permission to grant.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Rent and notice, not bookings.
5. **State transition conflict**: The tenancy/booking boundary itself — a room cannot be both. Converting a bookable room to a lockout must handle existing bookings, and nothing currently says how.

> **Finding worth escalating**: CX-08's point 2 is a real hole. Lockout is a large share of rehearsal
> revenue (16.06.09 DT-05), rehearsal is the domain's frequency play (16.04 DT-04), and lockout
> generates **no completion events** — so the harvest engine (16.05.06) never fires on the supply that
> is used most. The freshest data comes from the least-used rooms.

### CX-02: Availability & Holds ↔ Waitlist & Backfill

**Relationship**: Holds expire constantly and silently (16.06.01 D-02) — that is the design, and it is
the counter to the Operator forgetting. Each expiry is a slot that was mentally sold and just came
back, usually without the Operator noticing. Backfill is what notices. This makes hold TTLs
*generative*: they do not only prevent a loss, they create a stream of sellable inventory the Operator
never knew they had.

**Role scoping**:
- **Operator**: gets slots resold without touching anything.
- **Musician / Producer**: get told the date they wanted just opened.
- **Fan**: none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: None — the hold releases, the slot is available, the waitlist reacts.
2. **Trigger chain**: TTL expiry → slot available → waitlist notified → offered. Fast is essential; the slot is decaying.
3. **Permission intersection**: None.
4. **Notification fan-out**: The mechanic's weak point — broadcast creates 39 disappointments per slot and trains people to ignore alerts (16.06.04 DT-04).
5. **State transition conflict**: A waitlist offer racing the original holder re-requesting the slot they just lost.

### CX-05, CX-06, CX-09, CX-10 — summary detail

**CX-05 (Posture → Availability)**: posture decides whether the calendar is visible at all. An
enquiry-only venue may show no availability whatsoever, which means 16.06.01's holds exist but are
invisible until a booker grants one. Posture is upstream of everything transactional.

**CX-06 (Reservation → Waitlist)**: a cancellation releases perishable inventory with a clock running.
Straightforward; the interesting variant is CX-02's expired hold, which is higher-volume.

**CX-09 (Sync → Reservation)** — *Medium*: an inbound external event landing on a paid booking. It
cannot cancel it (money and a counterparty are involved) and it cannot be ignored (the Operator
believes their calendar). The only honest resolution is to surface it as a conflict to a human.
`[PENDING — Step 5]` — 16.06.02 Q-03.

**CX-10 (Pricing → Waitlist)** — *Medium*: waitlist depth is the only demand signal the domain could
ever generate, and therefore the only possible input to dynamic pricing. It requires liquidity that
does not exist, which is one of the reasons dynamic pricing is proposed `wont` (16.06.08 D-02). Also
inverted: if that data ever exists, its first use is probably Operator analytics (22), not automated
pricing.
