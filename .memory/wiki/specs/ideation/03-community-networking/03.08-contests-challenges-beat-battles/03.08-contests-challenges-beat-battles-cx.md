## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Prizes & Submission Rights ↔ Contest Creation & Briefs

**Relationship**: This is the pair that makes contests a sub-domain rather than a feature, and its direction
is counter-intuitive. One expects the brief to define the prize; in fact the **rights position constrains
what the brief may ask for**. A remix competition asks entrants to create derivative works from a master —
which the organiser must have the right to license for that purpose (domain 09/11). A "flip this sample"
challenge distributes a sample that must be clearable. Per personas.md the Musician's worst accidental
behaviour is "uploads a stem containing an uncleared sample and exposes every collaborator downstream" — a
contest does that *at scale*, to strangers, with the platform's brief as the invitation.

Per D-02 both halves are settled before a single submission exists. This is D-18 (capture at source) applied
to a contest: the moment to agree who owns what is when nobody yet knows whose entry will be brilliant.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: as organiser they may not understand that their remix brief requires rights they do not hold; as entrant they are the one whose work is at risk. Per personas.md they are the persona who "signs a split sheet they don't understand" — a contest brief is a split sheet with a leaderboard.
- **Producer**: the most likely organiser of a craft contest, and per personas.md the trust broker whose name on the brief is what makes people enter.
- **Operator**: organises scene-facing contests around their room (a battle night). Their rights exposure is smaller — they are supplying a venue, not a master.
- **Fan**: no access to either (D-04) — they watch.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The brief owns the terms; the rights position is part of the brief, not a separate object. There is deliberately no way to publish a brief with unresolved rights — that is the whole design.
2. **Trigger chain**: Publishing a brief requires the rights check to pass. If it fails, the contest cannot open. This should block, not warn: a warned organiser publishes anyway.
3. **Permission intersection**: Yes — the organiser's rights in the source material (domain 09/11) determine whether the brief is publishable at all.
4. **Notification fan-out**: None at this pair.
5. **State transition conflict**: Rights terms must be **immutable once a submission exists**. An organiser changing the terms after entries arrive is retroactively rewriting a deal people already accepted — the exact wrong the platform exists to prevent, committed inside its own feature.

### CX-02: Contest Creation & Briefs ↔ Submissions & Eligibility

**Relationship**: The brief defines who may enter and what counts. The critical property is that eligibility
must be checkable **at intake**, not at judging: a submission rejected after the work is done is the
free-work harvest scam (03.03.04 DT-03) with a deadline attached, and it is worse here because the entrant
did the work believing the rules admitted them. Anything the brief requires that cannot be machine- or
cheaply-checked at intake is a dispute the organiser is scheduling for later.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician / Producer**: entrants who need to know before working, not after.
- **Operator**: config-level — scene-scoped eligibility ("Bristol only") is the case they care about, and per 03.06.01 Q-01 the scene boundary is itself unresolved, which makes "Bristol only" harder to adjudicate than it sounds.
- **Fan**: no access.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Brief owns the rules; submissions reference them. The rules are frozen at first submission (see CX-01 (5)).
2. **Trigger chain**: Submission triggers eligibility evaluation at intake. Failure must be immediate and legible.
3. **Permission intersection**: Scene-scoped contests (03.06.01) inherit that scene's membership as the eligibility set, and therefore inherit its boundary ambiguity.
4. **Notification fan-out**: An open contest notifying a whole scene is a broadcast; volume limits apply as they do for calls (03.03.04).
5. **State transition conflict**: An entrant's eligibility can change mid-contest — they leave the scene, or their claimed status lapses. Eligibility should be evaluated at submission and frozen.

### CX-03: Submissions & Eligibility ↔ Judging, Voting & Anti-Gaming

**Relationship**: The submission set is the judged set, and per D-03 the judging model determines how hard
eligibility has to work. A jury can exercise judgement about a borderline entry; a public vote cannot —
it has already happened by the time anyone notices. This is why the judging model is not a preference but
a constraint on the rest of the sub-domain.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician / Producer**: entrants and, per D-03's preference for expert judging, judges.
- **Operator**: config — may appoint judges for a scene contest.
- **Fan**: read-only — they watch results, and per D-04 they do not vote, because a fan vote measures the entrants' follower counts (DT-01 on 03.08.03).

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Submissions are owned by 03.08.02; judging reads them and writes verdicts.
2. **Trigger chain**: Close of submissions triggers judging. A judge who does not judge stalls the contest indefinitely — a real and common failure that needs a deadline and a fallback.
3. **Permission intersection**: A judge who is also an entrant, or who has a credit with an entrant, is conflicted — and per 03.04.01 the platform **knows this from the graph**, which is a genuine advantage no other contest platform has.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Results notify all entrants. Losing entrants are the majority and their experience determines whether the contest format survives in the scene.
5. **State transition conflict**: A submission withdrawn during judging; a submission disqualified after votes are cast.

### CX-04: Judging ↔ Prizes & Submission Rights

**Relationship**: Judging decides who is paid, which converts a taste dispute into a financial dispute. This
is where a community ritual acquires the machinery of a marketplace — payouts (domain 23), possibly KYC
(constraints.md flags KYC/AML as pending for vendor payouts), and a dispute path (24) that a scene argument
does not need. Per parent Q-04, an entry fee would move it further still into regulated territory.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician / Producer**: winner (paid) or entrant (not). Per personas.md the Musician makes a living from "a portfolio of small income streams" — a contest prize is one of those streams, so it is not play money to them.
- **Operator**: may fund a prize.
- **Fan**: no access.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The verdict determines the payout; the payout is domain 23's mechanism.
2. **Trigger chain**: Verdict → payout → rights position resolves per the brief. If the payout fails (KYC, banking), the winner is announced but unpaid — the worst possible state for the organiser's credibility and the platform's.
3. **Permission intersection**: Payout may require identity/tax status the entrant never provided at submission — per constraints.md, tax reporting and KYC are open compliance questions. A contest can therefore announce a winner it cannot legally pay.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Winner and entrants notified; the result is a feed event (03.02.01).
5. **State transition conflict**: A result overturned after payout is unrecoverable in practice. Disputes must resolve *before* money moves.
