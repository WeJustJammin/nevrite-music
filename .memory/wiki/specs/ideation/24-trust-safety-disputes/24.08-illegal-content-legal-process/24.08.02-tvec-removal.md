# Feature: Terrorist & Violent Extremist Content Removal

> **Number**: 24.08.02
> **Parent**: [Illegal Content & Legal Process](./24.08-illegal-content-legal-process-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Removal of terrorist and violent extremist content, and the policy that decides what counts. Most
platforms treat this as a video-and-social problem imported from elsewhere. **For a music platform it is
native**: extremist music is an established, organised, commercially distributed category — NSBM (national
socialist black metal), white-power rock, and the label and mail-order networks built around them have
existed for four decades. A multi-vendor marketplace selling physical media (D-05) and merch (domain 20)
is a distribution channel for exactly that inventory, and every major music platform has had a public
incident over it.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | None | No visibility as machinery — but the persona with the most at stake in the policy, from the other direction. Extreme, political, violent and transgressive art is a legitimate and enormous part of music, and a heavy-handed policy removes their work. Their exposure is the false positive. |
| Producer | None | No visibility. Same false-positive exposure on the material they host and mix. |
| Operator | None | No visibility. **Their real-world version is not content at all** — it is a promoter trying to book a band with a known affiliation into their room, which is a decision no software makes for them. |
| Fan | None | No visibility. Buys the records. The demand side of a distribution channel is where the volume is. |

## Behavior

Two distinct mechanisms, frequently confused:

- **Hash matching** against shared industry sets of designated-organisation propaganda. High confidence,
  automatic, and narrow — it catches beheading videos and attack manifestos.
- **Policy judgment** on everything else, which on a music platform is nearly everything. There is no hash
  for a black-metal label's back catalogue.

The second is a **written policy** applied by a human (24.08 CX-03), and the register (24.08.04) is where
its reasoning lives. The line is art vs advocacy, and it is genuinely hard: a historical recording, a
band's early material, a genre with adjacency, a lyric about war, an iconography borrowed for shock.

**Scoring users for extremism is rejected** (24.08 CX R-01) — the available signals are genre proxies, and
a model built on them flags black metal, industrial, punk, drill and war-themed folk indiscriminately.

Removal runs through the ordinary ladder (24.02.01) with a statement of reasons and an appeal — unlike
CSAM, this is a judgment, and judgments must be contestable.

### Happy Path

1. Content or a listing is reported, or matched against a shared hash set
2. Hash match on designated-organisation propaganda → removed
3. Everything else → human review against the written policy
4. Decision cites a rule (24.02.04); removal carries an SoR (24.02.03) and an appeal (24.02.02)
5. Repeat patterns escalate through the ordinary ladder

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| An NSBM label lists its back catalogue as physical media | **The music-native case.** No hash set covers it; a written policy must, and it must be specific enough to apply | Blocked or removed, citing a specific rule |
| A black metal band with no ideology, corpse paint and a Norse aesthetic | **Not extremist.** The imagery overlaps and means nothing | Nothing |
| A historically significant recording by a band with an ugly past | Art vs advocacy, and reasonable people disagree | `[PENDING]` — 24.08 Q-02 |
| A drill track, a murder ballad, a war song, a death-metal lyric sheet | **Not extremism.** 24.01.02 DT-01 in the highest-stakes context | Nothing |
| Merch with extremist iconography (domain 20) | The commerce half, and the easier call — a symbol on a shirt is not a lyric | Blocked; prohibited-items overlap (24.02.05) |
| A promoter books a band with a known affiliation into a venue | **Not a content problem.** No software decides this for an Operator | Out of scope, stated |
| Hash match false positive | Rare; appealable via the ordinary ladder | SoR + appeal |
| Coded language, dog whistles, numeric symbolism | Requires domain literacy the platform will not have | `[PENDING]` — 24.01.03 DT-02's scarce reviewer, again |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Clear | Default | Nothing |
| Hash-matched | Designated propaganda | Removed; SoR issued |
| Under policy review | Reported, no hash match | Content stays live pending review |
| Removed | Policy decision | SoR citing the specific rule + appeal route |
| Retained | Reviewed, not a violation | Nothing |
| Escalated | Novel policy question | The register (24.08.04) is where the answer gets written down |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | TVEC is a video and social-media problem; a music marketplace has minimal exposure | The default scoping, and it is backwards | ❌ REJECTED — **a music platform's TVEC exposure is higher than a generic marketplace's, not lower.** Extremist music is not a hypothetical category: NSBM and white-power rock are established genres with organised label and mail-order distribution networks going back to the 1980s, and a multi-vendor marketplace for physical media (D-05) plus merch (domain 20) is precisely the distribution channel they use. Spotify, Bandcamp, Apple and Amazon have all had public removal incidents over exactly this. **The exposure is commercial, not just editorial** — this platform does not merely host the content, it would *sell and ship* it, take a fee, and pay out a vendor. That is a materially worse position than hosting a video, and no candidate in the sweep noticed. |
| DT-02 | Hash-sharing (GIFCT and equivalents) covers the requirement | The compliance answer | ❌ REJECTED — those sets are built for **designated-organisation propaganda**: attack footage, manifestos, branded terrorist media. They do not, and cannot, cover a black-metal label's back catalogue, because the material is not propaganda from a designated organisation — it is *music*, made by a band, sold as a record. The judgment (is this art with ugly aesthetics, a historically significant document, or actual advocacy?) is exactly the thing a hash set exists to avoid having to make. **A music platform has to make it anyway, in writing**, and the register (24.08 CX-03) is where the reasoning has to live so that decisions are consistent and defensible rather than reinvented per case. |
| DT-03 | The policy line can be drawn once and applied mechanically | The hope | ❌ REJECTED — the line sits in the middle of music's most contested territory, and the false-positive cost is **the platform's own users' art.** Black metal's aesthetics overlap with the genre's extremist fringe almost completely — corpse paint, Norse imagery, anti-Christian symbolism — and the overwhelming majority of it is not political at all. Punk, industrial, drill and folk all carry violent, historical or transgressive content that is unambiguously art. Meanwhile the actual advocacy often hides in coded language a generalist reviewer cannot read. **So the false positives are loud, sympathetic and damaging; the true positives are quiet and hard.** That is the worst possible signal-to-noise profile for a mechanical rule, and it is why this is a policy with a reviewer rather than a filter. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | Two mechanisms, kept separate: hash matching (narrow, automatic) and written policy (broad, human) | DT-02. Conflating them means either the hash set is asked to make a judgment it cannot, or the judgment is treated as if it had a hash's certainty. |
| D-02 | No extremism scoring of users | 24.08 CX R-01. Genre proxies produce a defamatory artifact. |
| D-03 | Removals run through the ordinary ladder with an SoR and an appeal | Unlike CSAM, this is a judgment — and judgments the platform's own users will contest must be contestable. |
