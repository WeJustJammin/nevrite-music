# Feature: DMCA Notice, Counter-Notice & Repeat Infringer Policy

> **Number**: 24.05.01
> **Parent**: [Copyright & Authenticity Enforcement](./24.05-copyright-authenticity-enforcement-index.md)
> **Status**: [DEEP]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-17

## Overview

The statutory notice-and-takedown cycle and the strike counter that safe harbour is conditioned on:
512(c) notices, expeditious removal, 512(g) counter-notices with the put-back window, and — the part that
actually decides whether the platform survives — a 512(i) repeat-infringer policy that is *reasonably
implemented* and actually executed. Sony Music v. Cox (2019) (~$1B) and UMG v. Grande ($46.7M) were not
lost for lacking a policy. They were lost for having one and exercising discretion around it.

For a platform hosting user-uploaded audio at the centre of its product (D-10), this is the single
highest-stakes feature in domain 24, and it is a Must from user one.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Both sides, and both are painful. As a **claimant**: files notices against someone using their recording — and discovers that independents get no priority lane (24.01.04 Q-01). As a **target**: receives a claim on their own work from a label bot, and must counter-notice — which means giving the claimant their home address and consenting to federal jurisdiction. To reclaim their own song. |
| Producer | Full | The highest-exposure party. Their working uploads *are* the masters labels control: a rough mix of a track they produced, uploaded to their own session, matches the label's master exactly. They are structurally the most likely to be claimed for possessing the thing they made. |
| Operator | Full | Low volume — a promo video with a soundtrack, a photo of the room with a poster in it. Their claims usually run the other way: someone using their venue's photos in a fake listing. |
| Fan | Full | Rare but **statutorily identical**. A fan whose gig video is claimed has exactly the same 512(g) right as a label. The platform cannot make it a professional-only feature, and negligible volume does not reduce the obligation. **None-access is not an option here** — 512(g) attaches to whoever uploaded the material, and a Fan uploads (gig video, cover clip) exactly as a professional does. |

> All four personas are **Full** — deliberately. This is the one feature in the sub-domain where the access
> level is fixed by statute rather than by product judgement (see the index Role Matrix note). Withholding
> the counter-notice path from any persona would forfeit safe harbour for that persona's uploads.

## Behavior

Notices require the 512(c)(3) elements — signature; identification of the copyrighted work; identification
of the material claimed to infringe **with a specific location (URL/asset ID)**; claimant contact
information; a good-faith-belief statement; and a statement under penalty of perjury that the information
is accurate and that the sender is authorised. **An incomplete notice is not a notice** (17 U.S.C.
512(c)(3)(B)) and must not trip the strike counter. Removal is *expeditious* on a complete notice — the
product target is **removal actioned within 24 hours of a complete notice being accepted**, not "fast".

Counter-notice requires the 512(g)(3) elements — signature; identification of the removed material and its
prior location; a statement under penalty of perjury of good-faith belief that removal was a mistake or
misidentification; and the counter-noticer's name, physical address, phone, plus **consent to the
jurisdiction of the federal district court** and acceptance of service from the complainant. A valid
counter-notice opens the statutory put-back window: material is restored **not less than 10 nor more than
14 business days** after the claimant receives the counter-notice (512(g)(2)(C)) **unless** the platform
first receives notice that the claimant has filed a court action seeking to restrain the activity.

**The strike counter is rigid, automatic and auditable** (24.05 D-02). It runs alongside the general
proportionate ladder (24.02.01), and it does not accept discretion — that is the entire lesson of Cox.
A reversed strike must decrement (24.02.02), or the ratchet punishes people for the platform's errors.

**The platform's own provenance record is weighed before a claim is actioned** (24.05 CX-01). This is the
capability no other intermediary has, and it is the direct application of D-18 to enforcement.

### Claim Lifecycle

| Stage | Trigger | Effect |
|-------|---------|--------|
| No claim | Default | Nothing |
| Notice received | Complete 512(c)(3) notice accepted | Uploader notified: removal notice + counter-notice rights + strike consequence stated in advance |
| Removed | Expeditious action (≤24h) | Content down; the record of it (ownership, provenance, audit trail) is not removed |
| Counter-noticed | Uploader submits valid 512(g)(3) counter-notice | Claimant notified; put-back clock starts on the claimant's receipt |
| Restored | Business day 10–14 elapses, no suit-filed notice received | Content back; strike decremented if the reversal so provides |
| Stayed down | Suit-filed notice received before window elapses | Content stays down pending the court |
| Strike N | Counter incremented on a valid notice not reversed | Subject sees the count and what the next rung is — stated plainly, in advance |
| Terminated | Repeat-infringer threshold reached (Q-02) | Access closed; property preserved and exportable (24.02.01 DT-01) |

### Happy Path

1. A rights-holder files a notice with the 512(c)(3) elements (via 24.01.01, or 24.01.04 in bulk)
2. Completeness is checked; incomplete → rejected without a strike, with the specific missing element named
3. The claim is weighed against the platform's provenance record for that asset
4. Complete and not contradicted → removal actioned within 24 hours + notice to the uploader with counter-notice rights
5. Uploader may counter-notice; on a valid counter-notice the put-back window opens on the claimant's receipt
6. No suit-filed notice by business day 14 → material restored. Suit-filed notice received → material stays down
7. The strike counter increments on the schedule the policy states, automatically and without human discretion

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Category | Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Provenance contradiction | Label bot claims a Producer's own rough mix of a record they produced | The platform's provenance graph contradicts the claim — it holds the session record showing the upload predates the release (24.05 CX-01). The removal obligation still runs (the platform is not the adjudicator), but the competing record is surfaced to the uploader and attached to the case | Producer: the claim, with the platform's own contemporaneous record cited alongside it and a one-tap path to counter-notice |
| Empty / partial data | Incomplete notice — no sworn statement, or no specific URL/asset ID | Rejected with code `INCOMPLETE_NOTICE`. **No strike, no removal** — an invalid notice is not a notice (512(c)(3)(B)) | Claimant: the specific missing element(s) named field-by-field, with a resubmit path |
| Permission | A user who is **not** the uploader tries to counter-notice content they did not upload | Rejected with code `NOT_UPLOADER` — 512(g) standing belongs to the party whose material was removed. For co-owned masters, any recorded co-owner of the removed asset has standing; a non-owner does not | Non-owner: "Only the uploader or a recorded owner can counter-notice this material." |
| Permission / property | Strike would terminate a user whose account holds co-owned masters | Access closes; the record does not (24.02.01 DT-01). The rigid ladder and the property constraint coexist — co-owners retain independent access to their own copies | Subject: account closed, ownership records preserved and exportable; co-owners: unaffected on their own accounts |
| Malicious input — bogus notice | Fraudulent notice (perjured, or a serial claimant weaponising takedown to suppress a rival's own work) | The notice is still processed (the platform cannot pre-judge good faith), but the claimant's accuracy is logged to the 24.01.04 flagger ledger; a pattern routes to trusted-flagger review, and the target is told 512(f) misrepresentation liability exists | Target: the claim proceeds, plus a plain-language note that a knowingly false notice carries 512(f) liability and how to report abuse (routes to 24.01.04 / 24.06.01) |
| Malicious input — bogus counter-notice | A counter-notice sworn in bad faith to force put-back of infringing material | Processed per statute (the platform is not the adjudicator); the sworn perjury statement and the disclosed identity are what expose the counter-noticer. Logged to the case evidence locker (24.09) | Counter-noticer: the perjury warning shown before submission; claimant: the counter-notice with the restoration clock |
| The address trap | Uploader wants to counter-notice but does not want to disclose their home address | The 512(g)(3)(D) requirement is non-waivable by the platform. **Default behaviour (D-05)**: before submission the form states in plain language exactly what will be disclosed (name, physical address, phone) and to whom (the claimant), and that they are consenting to federal-court jurisdiction. The uploader may proceed or abandon. Whether WeJammin builds *more* than transparent disclosure — address shielding, a designated-agent intermediary, counsel referral, indemnity — is the values decision in Q-01 | Uploader: an explicit pre-submission disclosure panel naming every field the claimant will receive and the jurisdiction consequence, with "proceed" / "cancel" — never a silent submit |
| Multi-claimant | Same work claimed by two different claimants (e.g. both hold rights, or one is mistaken) | Neither is adjudicated by the platform; **each complete notice is an independent notice** (D-04). Removal happens **once** — a second complete notice against already-removed content is a no-op for takedown. The strike counter attaches to a `(claimant, asset, infringement-event)` tuple, so the same claimant re-filing cannot inflate the count, but two genuinely distinct claimants each register their own potential strike. Each claim carries its own counter-notice path | Target: two claims listed distinctly, each with its own counter-notice path and its own strike consequence; the content, already down from the first, is not removed twice |
| Multi-hyphenate in two roles | The **same human** is both claimant and target — e.g. as a label/master rights-holder (entity A) they auto-claim a stem their own producer-self (entity B) uploaded | Detected when the claimant identity and the uploader identity resolve to the same underlying person/graph node. The claim is flagged as a **self-claim** and held for the user rather than actioned — a person does not need the statutory machinery against themselves. Does not increment the counter | Subject: "This claim targets your own upload — resolve it in your project rather than through a takedown," with a link to the session record (D-18) |
| Concurrency / race | Put-back window elapses at the **same moment** the claimant files suit and sends the suit-filed notice | Tie-break rule: if a valid suit-filed notice is received **at or before** the moment the restoration takes effect, the content **stays down**. A suit-filed notice that coincides exactly with restoration is treated as arriving first (fail-safe toward the claimant, because an erroneous restoration during active litigation is the costlier error) | Uploader: "A court action was filed; your material remains down pending the court" instead of the expected restoration |
| Concurrency | Bulk submission lands N notices against one uploader within the same second (24.01.04) | Each is completeness-checked and provenance-weighed independently, and a duplicate of the same notice within the batch does not count again; the counter increments per distinct valid `(claimant, asset, event)` tuple, deduped, never once-per-batch-arbitrarily | Subject: each valid notice reflected individually in the strike history, with timestamps |
| Network / delivery failure | The takedown / counter-notice-rights notification cannot be delivered (bounced email, stale contact) | Removal is still expeditious (the statute does not wait on delivery), but the **strike does not finalise into a termination** on a notice the user demonstrably never received: the platform escalates delivery across every channel on file (D-07), logs each attempt, and the counter-notice window's clock for the uploader starts on confirmed delivery, not on send | Subject (once reached): the removal notice, the counter-notice window still open, and a record that earlier delivery attempts failed |
| Reversal | Reversed strike — appeal upheld (24.02.02) or counter-notice unopposed through the window | The counter **must decrement** (24.05 D-02); a strike the platform itself got wrong cannot ratchet the user toward termination | Subject: strike count corrected, with the reason logged |
| Flagger reliability | Claimant is a trusted flagger with a degraded accuracy record | Status review is triggered in 24.01.04 — **but the notice is still a notice today**. Accuracy history informs future trust tier, not the validity of a statutorily complete notice already filed | Target: the claim proceeds; the claimant's tier is separately under review |
| Jurisdiction | Non-US user, non-US claimant, or non-US-hosted posture | The DMCA notice/counter-notice flow is **the platform's single implemented regime at launch** (D-06) — US safe harbour is what protects the hosting infrastructure. A non-US party is routed through the same DMCA flow, and the interface names which regime it is operating rather than silently implying local law. EU Art 17 CDSM / UK CDPA divergence is Q-03 | User: an explicit label that this is the DMCA (US) process, with a note that regional regimes may differ (Q-03) |

|-------|---------|---------------|

| Error | Submission failed on validation or transport | The specific failure — `INCOMPLETE_NOTICE` with the missing element named, `NOT_UPLOADER`, or a transient transport error with a **safe retry** where retrying the same submission never double-files or double-counts a strike |
| Partial | A notice was received but is missing one or more 512(c)(3) elements | Held in an **incomplete** state — not actioned, not counted, not removed. Claimant prompted for the exact missing element; uploader sees nothing until (and unless) the notice becomes complete |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Safe harbour is automatic if you take things down when asked | The near-universal misunderstanding | ❌ REJECTED — 512(i) conditions safe harbour on a repeat-infringer policy that is **reasonably implemented**, and "implemented" means executed, not published. Cox had a policy and worked around it; the verdict in Sony Music v. Cox (2019) was ~$1B. Grande had one and did not terminate; UMG v. Grande, $46.7M. **A written policy you do not execute is worse than no policy** — it is affirmative evidence that you knew and chose not to act. The product consequence is concrete and unusual: the strike counter must be **automatic and auditable from launch**, precisely so that no human can be shown to have exercised discretion around it. That is a design constraint derived from a litigation posture, and it is the opposite of how every other enforcement surface in this domain is built. |
| DT-02 | The platform can be neutral machinery between claimant and uploader | The intermediary's comfortable self-image | ❌ REJECTED — **neutral machinery produces an asymmetric outcome, and on this platform it points the wrong way.** To reclaim their own work, a musician must file a 512(g) counter-notice, which requires giving the claimant their **physical address** and **consenting to federal court jurisdiction**. A label risks a filing fee. A session musician risks being sued in a foreign court and having their home address handed to a corporation's lawyers. The statute is symmetric; the consequences are not, and the asymmetry runs precisely against the persona `personas.md` calls the structural centre. WeJammin cannot rewrite 512(g) — but a platform whose thesis is that musicians should hold the proof of their own work (D-18) has to decide *deliberately* whether it stands neutrally beside that trap or does something about it (a designated agent, address shielding, counsel referral, an indemnity). Inheriting the default is itself a choice, and it is a choice against the product's own promise. The default behaviour (D-05) at least refuses to let the disclosure happen *silently* — but transparent disclosure is a floor, not the answer to Q-01. |
| DT-03 | The platform must defer to the notice because it cannot know who is right | The structural assumption of the entire notice-and-takedown regime, and it has been true for 25 years | ❌ REJECTED — **and this is the sharpest thing in domain 24.** The DMCA's design assumes the intermediary is blind: it hosts a file and knows nothing about its origin, so it must act on assertion. WeJammin is not blind. It holds the session record — who uploaded which stem, at what time, into whose project, with whose counter-attestation (D-18). When a label bot claims a producer's rough mix, the platform has a **contemporaneous, counter-attested record that the producer made it**, created long before anyone disputed anything. That does not resolve the legal question and it does not change the platform's obligations. But it means WeJammin is the first intermediary with a *positive competing record* rather than only a claim — and no competitor can manufacture that retroactively, because they were not in the room. This is the thesis, arriving in the least expected place. |
| DT-04 | Two distinct claimants against the same asset must mean two strikes | The naive reading of a rigid, per-notice counter | ❌ REJECTED as automatic — **the rigid counter and the anti-inflation constraint are in tension, and the resolution is a tuple, not a raw notice count.** A rigid counter (DT-01) says "increment on every valid notice, no discretion." But two *distinct* claimants on one asset means at least one of them is wrong, and counting both would let a competitor manufacture a second strike by piling a bogus-but-complete notice onto a genuine one. The resolution is to make the strike unit a `(claimant, asset, infringement-event)` tuple: the same claimant cannot re-file to inflate (dedup within claimant), and distinct claimants each register — but each strike is independently reversible when its counter-notice succeeds. This keeps the counter mechanical and auditable (no human judged which claimant is "real") while denying the inflation attack. The subtle residual — whether two genuinely-distinct-but-both-valid rights-holders on one shared work (a sample clearance dispute) should each cost a strike — is a legal-policy edge that counsel owns (Q-04), because getting it wrong in *either* direction (over-count = Cox-style unfairness; under-count = softening the ladder = the Cox liability itself) is expensive. |
