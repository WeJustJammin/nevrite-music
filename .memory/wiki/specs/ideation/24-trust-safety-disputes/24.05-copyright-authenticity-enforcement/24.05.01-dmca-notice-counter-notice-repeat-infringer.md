# Feature: DMCA Notice, Counter-Notice & Repeat Infringer Policy

> **Number**: 24.05.01
> **Parent**: [Copyright & Authenticity Enforcement](./24.05-copyright-authenticity-enforcement-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

The statutory notice-and-takedown cycle and the strike counter that safe harbour is conditioned on:
512(c) notices, expeditious removal, 512(g) counter-notices with the put-back window, and — the part that
actually decides whether the platform survives — a 512(i) repeat-infringer policy that is *reasonably
implemented* and actually executed. BMG v. Cox (~$1B) and UMG v. Grande ($46.7M) were not lost for lacking
a policy. They were lost for having one and exercising discretion around it.

For a platform hosting user-uploaded audio at the centre of its product (D-10), this is the single
highest-stakes feature in domain 24, and it is a Must from user one.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Both sides, and both are painful. As a **claimant**: files notices against someone using their recording — and discovers that independents get no priority lane (24.01.04 Q-01). As a **target**: receives a claim on their own work from a label bot, and must counter-notice — which means giving the claimant their home address and consenting to federal jurisdiction. To reclaim their own song. |
| Producer | Full | The highest-exposure party. Their working uploads *are* the masters labels control: a rough mix of a track they produced, uploaded to their own session, matches the label's master exactly. They are structurally the most likely to be claimed for possessing the thing they made. |
| Operator | Full | Low volume — a promo video with a soundtrack, a photo of the room with a poster in it. Their claims usually run the other way: someone using their venue's photos in a fake listing. |
| Fan | Full | Rare but **statutorily identical**. A fan whose gig video is claimed has exactly the same 512(g) right as a label. The platform cannot make it a professional-only feature, and negligible volume does not reduce the obligation. |

## Behavior

Notices require the 512(c)(3) elements including a sworn statement; incomplete notices are not notices and
must not trip the strike counter. Removal is expeditious on a complete notice. Counter-notice opens the
statutory put-back window (10–14 business days), during which the claimant may sue; if they do not,
material is restored.

**The strike counter is rigid, automatic and auditable** (24.05 D-02). It runs alongside the general
proportionate ladder (24.02.01), and it does not accept discretion — that is the entire lesson of Cox.
A reversed strike must decrement (24.02.02), or the ratchet punishes people for the platform's errors.

**The platform's own provenance record is weighed before a claim is actioned** (24.05 CX-01). This is the
capability no other intermediary has, and it is the direct application of D-18 to enforcement.

### Happy Path

1. A rights-holder files a notice with the 512(c)(3) elements (via 24.01.01, or 24.01.04 in bulk)
2. Completeness is checked; incomplete → rejected without a strike
3. The claim is weighed against the platform's provenance record for that asset
4. Complete and not contradicted → expeditious removal + notice to the uploader with counter-notice rights
5. Uploader may counter-notice; the put-back window opens
6. No suit filed → material restored. Suit filed → material stays down
7. The strike counter increments on the schedule the policy states, automatically

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Label bot claims a Producer's own rough mix of a record they produced | **The platform's provenance graph contradicts the claim** — it holds the session record showing the upload predates the release | Producer: the claim, with the platform's own record cited alongside it (24.05 CX-01) |
| Incomplete notice (no sworn statement, no specific URL) | Rejected. **No strike** — an invalid notice must not count | Claimant: the specific missing element |
| Uploader wants to counter-notice but won't publish their home address | This is the trap. 512(g) requires address + consent to jurisdiction — see DT-02 | `[PENDING]` — 24.05 Q-01 is a values decision |
| Strike counter would ban a user whose account holds co-owned masters | Access closes; the record does not (24.02.01 DT-01). The rigid ladder and the property constraint must coexist | Subject: account closed, ownership records preserved and exportable |
| Reversed strike (appeal upheld, or counter-notice unopposed) | Counter **must decrement** | Subject: strike count corrected |
| Same work claimed by two different claimants | Neither is adjudicated by the platform; both notices are notices | `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]` |
| Claimant is a trusted flagger with a bad accuracy record | Status review (24.01.04) — but the notice is still a notice today | Target: the claim proceeds |
| Non-US user, non-US claimant, non-US content | DMCA is US law; EU/UK regimes differ (Art 17 CDSM, UK CDPA). The platform likely needs more than one regime | `[PENDING]` — see Q-03 |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| No claim | Default | Nothing |
| Notice received | Complete 512(c)(3) notice | Uploader: removal notice + counter-notice rights + strike consequence |
| Removed | Expeditious action | Content down; the record of it is not |
| Counter-noticed | Uploader responds | Claimant notified; put-back clock runs |
| Restored | Window elapsed, no suit | Content back; strike decremented if the policy so provides |
| Stayed down | Suit filed | Content stays down pending the court |
| Strike N | Counter incremented | Subject: the count and what the next rung is — stated plainly, in advance |
| Terminated | Repeat-infringer threshold | Access closed; property preserved |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Safe harbour is automatic if you take things down when asked | The near-universal misunderstanding | ❌ REJECTED — 512(i) conditions safe harbour on a repeat-infringer policy that is **reasonably implemented**, and "implemented" means executed, not published. Cox had a policy and worked around it; the verdict was ~$1B. Grande had one and did not terminate; $46.7M. **A written policy you do not execute is worse than no policy** — it is affirmative evidence that you knew and chose not to act. The product consequence is concrete and unusual: the strike counter must be **automatic and auditable from launch**, precisely so that no human can be shown to have exercised discretion around it. That is a design constraint derived from a litigation posture, and it is the opposite of how every other enforcement surface in this domain is built. |
| DT-02 | The platform can be neutral machinery between claimant and uploader | The intermediary's comfortable self-image | ❌ REJECTED — **neutral machinery produces an asymmetric outcome, and on this platform it points the wrong way.** To reclaim their own work, a musician must file a 512(g) counter-notice, which requires giving the claimant their **physical address** and **consenting to federal court jurisdiction**. A label risks a filing fee. A session musician risks being sued in a foreign court and having their home address handed to a corporation's lawyers. The statute is symmetric; the consequences are not, and the asymmetry runs precisely against the persona `personas.md` calls the structural centre. WeJammin cannot rewrite 512(g) — but a platform whose thesis is that musicians should hold the proof of their own work (D-18) has to decide *deliberately* whether it stands neutrally beside that trap or does something about it (a designated agent, address shielding, counsel referral, an indemnity). Inheriting the default is itself a choice, and it is a choice against the product's own promise. |
| DT-03 | The platform must defer to the notice because it cannot know who is right | The structural assumption of the entire notice-and-takedown regime, and it has been true for 25 years | ❌ REJECTED — **and this is the sharpest thing in domain 24.** The DMCA's design assumes the intermediary is blind: it hosts a file and knows nothing about its origin, so it must act on assertion. WeJammin is not blind. It holds the session record — who uploaded which stem, at what time, into whose project, with whose counter-attestation (D-18). When a label bot claims a producer's rough mix, the platform has a **contemporaneous, counter-attested record that the producer made it**, created long before anyone disputed anything. That does not resolve the legal question and it does not change the platform's obligations. But it means WeJammin is the first intermediary with a *positive competing record* rather than only a claim — and no competitor can manufacture that retroactively, because they were not in the room. This is the thesis, arriving in the least expected place. |

| D-01 | The strike counter is automatic, rigid and auditable from launch | DT-01. Cox/Grande. Discretion is the liability here, not the virtue. |
