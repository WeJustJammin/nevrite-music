## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Fingerprinting ↔ DMCA

**Relationship**: The matcher produces `(asset, reference, confidence, offset)`. That is evidence, not a
verdict. Before any claim is actioned, it is weighed against the platform's own provenance graph — who
uploaded this stem, into which session, when, with whose counter-attestation. **This is the inversion the
product makes possible**: every other intermediary has only the claim; WeJammin has a competing record.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: a match against their own released master, on their own uploaded stem, must not become a
  claim against them. The provenance graph is what prevents it.
- **Producer**: the highest-risk party — their working uploads (stems, rough mixes, comps) match the
  masters they themselves made, and the label that controls the master may not know they exist.
- **Operator / Fan**: not affected, except a Fan's gig video matching a live performance.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: the matcher owns matches; DMCA owns claims. A match never mutates a claim's
   state directly — a human or a rule promotes it, and the promotion is logged.
2. **Trigger chain**: upload → match (async) → weigh against provenance → maybe claim → notice cycle. If
   matching is unavailable, nothing happens — no claim is generated. Failing open here is correct: the
   notice-driven path (24.05.01) is the statutory one and it does not depend on the matcher at all.
3. **Permission intersection**: none. A match confers no standing; only a rights-holder has that.
4. **Notification fan-out**: a match notifies nobody. A claim notifies the uploader.
5. **State transition conflict**: an asset can be matched and simultaneously be the subject of a
   counter-notice. The put-back window (512(g)) governs, not the match confidence.

### CX-02: Fingerprinting ↔ Leak Detection

**Relationship**: Same technique, cleaner question. Infringement matching asks "does this similarity mean
this is unlicensed?" — which requires knowing about licences, covers, interpolations and samples the
platform cannot see. Leak matching asks "has this specific unreleased asset, which we hold and know is
unreleased, appeared?" — which is a yes/no with almost no false-positive surface, because the reference
is the platform's own file and its unreleased status is the platform's own fact.

**Role scoping**:
- **Producer**: the primary beneficiary. They are the party who stores unreleased material and the party
  blamed when it escapes.
- **Musician**: same, as the artist whose record leaked.
- **Operator / Fan**: no visibility.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: none — leak detection reads the matcher, it does not write to it.
2. **Trigger chain**: leak report or external match → identify the asset → trace access (Audit Log
   cross-cut) → identify recipients → response. The tracing, not the matching, is the hard part.
3. **Permission intersection**: yes, and it is sensitive — the access log names collaborators. A leak
   investigation is an accusation machine pointed at the project's own members.
4. **Notification fan-out**: the asset's owner immediately. Everyone with access — carefully; see
   24.05.04 Q-02.
5. **State transition conflict**: a leak found *after* release is a non-event; the same match before
   release is a crisis. The asset's release state changes the meaning of an identical signal.

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 24.05.03 Authenticity | 24.05.02 Fingerprinting | Considered — "can we fingerprint a guitar?" Rejected: image matching on gear photos identifies *photos*, not instruments, and the dominant vintage fraud is a **genuine instrument misrepresented** (a real '59 Les Paul, refinished, sold as original — 24.05.03 DT-01). No matcher detects that, because there is nothing fake to match. The control is provenance and expert review, which is a different mechanism entirely. |
| R-02 | 24.05.04 Leak Detection | 24.05.03 Authenticity | No interaction. One is about unreleased audio escaping; the other is about physical goods being what they claim. They share the word "genuine" and nothing else. |
| R-03 | 24.05.01 DMCA | 24.03.06 Review Integrity | Considered because takedown-as-harassment exists (a claimant repeatedly claiming an artist's own work to suppress them). Rejected as a pair here: that abuse is **trusted-flagger misuse** and is handled by the accuracy ledger in 24.01.04, or by 24.06.01 if it is targeted at a person. Routing it through review integrity would put a statutory notice process under a reputation system. |
