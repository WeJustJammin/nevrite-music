# Feature: Authenticity, Counterfeit & Brand/IP Protection

> **Number**: 24.05.03
> **Parent**: [Copyright & Authenticity Enforcement](./24.05-copyright-authenticity-enforcement-index.md)
> **Status**: [PARTIAL]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-18

## Overview

Enforcement against goods that are not what they claim to be, and the channel through which manufacturers
and brands assert their marks. Music gear has two distinct problems that generic counterfeit tooling
conflates: **outright fakes** (counterfeit SM58s and SM7Bs are endemic; Shure publishes identification
guides because the fakes are that common), and — far more valuable and far harder — **genuine instruments
misrepresented**, where a real 1959 Les Paul has been refinished, re-topped, or assembled from parts and is
sold as original at ten times its actual worth.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Both sides. As a **buyer**: the thing that stops them spending £12k on a vintage instrument from a stranger is exactly this question. As a **seller**: they may be innocently wrong — most people selling a misrepresented vintage guitar bought it misrepresented themselves, sometimes decades ago. The feature must distinguish fraud from inheritance. |
| Producer | Read-only | Their exposure is digital: cracked plugins, leaked sample packs, resold non-transferable licences (D-06). Different goods, same "is this legitimate" question, and a much more tractable answer — a licence key is checkable in a way a nitrocellulose finish is not. |
| Operator | Full | Lists gear alongside room time and is more likely to be a **Trader** (the Tax cross-cut's classification), which raises the standard they are held to. Also a claimant: their room's photos in a fake listing is a brand/IP claim of a kind. |
| Fan | Read-only | Encounters it as counterfeit merch (domain 20) — the fan-facing half of this feature and the one with the highest volume and the lowest value per incident. |

## Behavior

Three claim paths converge here: a **buyer's** authenticity dispute after purchase, a **brand's** notice
against a listing, and the platform's own detection at listing time (which overlaps
[24.02.05](../24.02-enforcement-appeals-policy/24.02.05-prohibited-restricted-items-engine.md) — one gate,
two engines, and the seller must never get contradictory reasons; see
[24 CX-09](../trust-safety-disputes-cx.md)).

**The control for misrepresented vintage is provenance, not detection.** There is nothing counterfeit to
detect — the logo is real, the serial is real, the wood is real. What is false is the *history*, and
history is exactly what [domain 15 (Gear Registry)](../../15-gear-registry-ownership/) records: ownership
chain, service history, modifications, prior listings with photos. **The product's thesis, applied to
objects**: the fact captured at the moment it was true (this guitar was refinished in 1974) cannot be
manufactured later.

Authenticity failures also surface **years late**, which breaks the claim windows every other feature in
domain 24 uses.

### Happy Path

1. A seller lists gear; the engine evaluates brand, model, serial, declared originality and modifications
2. Registry history (domain 15) is attached where it exists — prior listings, prior photos, service records
3. A buyer purchases; the item is what the record says
4. If contested: an authenticity claim opens with the registry history already in evidence
5. Expert review where the value warrants it; enforcement (24.02.01) where fraud is established

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Counterfeit SM58 listed as genuine | Detectable-ish: price, seller history, photo details. Shure's own guides are the reference | Blocked or held; the specific tells cited |
| **Refinished '59 Les Paul sold as original** | Nothing is counterfeit. The logo, serial and wood are genuine; the *history* is false. **No detector finds this** | The registry history — or its conspicuous absence |
| Seller innocently believes it is original (bought it that way in 1985) | **Not fraud.** Most misrepresented vintage is inherited misrepresentation | Dispute, not enforcement — a distinction the feature must encode |
| "Frankenstein" — a genuine body, a replacement neck, sold as all-original | The commonest vintage fraud, and the hardest. No detector resolves it; the platform routes to expert examination and holds settlement until the determination lands. Who pays and who examines is `[PENDING — /create-prd]` (Q-01) | "All-original claim under expert review — funds held" |
| Brand files a notice against a legitimate used listing | Trademark exhaustion — reselling a genuine used item is lawful. **A brand register must not become a used-market veto**. Exhaustion is checked before any action; the notice is logged to the brand's accuracy ledger (24.01.04) | Notice rejected; the brand's accuracy record notes it |
| Discovered two years later | Every standard window (returns, protection, chargeback, claim) has closed. The item is registry-backed → a **long-tail authenticity flag** attaches to the registry record and the misrepresentation is recorded against the original seller's history even where no monetary remedy remains. Whether a **monetary** remedy exists past the window is `[PENDING — /create-prd]` (Q-02, DT-03) | "Reported outside the guarantee window — recorded on the item's registry history" |
| Cracked plugin sold as "full version" | Tractable — a licence key is checkable | Blocked; escalates to the seller's risk record |
| Counterfeit merch at a fan price point | High volume, low value; per-item expert review never pays for itself. Handled by **pattern/volume signals** (seller history, price, image reuse) and brand-notice batching, not per-listing examination. Refund via 24.04 protection, not authenticity expert review | Listing removed; refunded under buyer protection |
| Same listing trips both this engine and the prohibited-items gate (24.02.05) | One gate, two engines. The seller must never see contradictory reasons — a single reconciled reason is surfaced (24 CX-09) | One consolidated hold reason, not two |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Counterfeit enforcement means finding fake logos and fake brands | Every marketplace's anti-counterfeit programme | ❌ REJECTED — in vintage gear the dominant fraud is **misrepresented originality**, and it has nothing fake in it. A refinished 1959 Les Paul has a genuine logo, a genuine serial, genuine wood and a real Gibson history; what is false is that the finish is not from 1959 — and that single fact is the difference between roughly £30k and roughly £300k. A counterfeit detector looking for fake brands finds nothing here, because nothing is fake. **The control is provenance: ownership chain, service history, modification records, prior listing photos** — which is domain 15, and which is the product's own thesis applied to objects instead of recordings. The fact (this was refinished in 1974) was capturable at the moment it was true and is unobtainable now, which is `problem-statement.md`'s argument almost verbatim. |
| DT-02 | A brand-protection register is straightforwardly good — brands police their marks, everyone benefits | Amazon Brand Registry's framing | ❌ REJECTED as stated — **the used-instrument market runs on trademark exhaustion.** Reselling a genuine used Gibson is entirely lawful and it is the entire business of domain 13. Manufacturers have well-documented commercial interests in suppressing used and grey-market sales, and a naive brand register hands them a veto over a lawful market. The register can exist, but it must have exhaustion checked before anything is actioned, and it needs the accuracy ledger 24.01.04 already requires — **for exactly the same reason and against exactly the same asymmetry**: a rights-holder lane pointed at the platform's own users. Two features, one structural risk. |
| DT-03 | Authenticity claims fit the standard dispute window | Consistency with 24.04 | ❌ REJECTED — **authenticity fraud is discovered years late by nature.** A player buys a "1963" Strat, plays it for four years, takes it to a luthier for a refret, and learns the neck is from 1968. Every window in domain 24 — returns, protection caps, chargebacks, claims — has closed by an order of magnitude. This is not an edge case in vintage; it is the modal discovery path, because the tells are only visible to an expert with the instrument disassembled. The feature therefore needs either a **long-tail authenticity remedy** with no analogue elsewhere in the domain, or the honest admission that the platform's guarantee expires long before the truth surfaces. |
| DT-04 | The digital half (cracked plugins, resold non-transferable licences) belongs to the same feature as the vintage half | Both are "is this legitimate" questions on a listing | ✅ CONFIRMED — but only because they are **structurally opposite and share nothing but the question.** The digital case is machine-decidable at listing time (a licence key either validates against the vendor or it does not — domain 14), so it belongs to detection; the vintage case is undecidable by any detector and belongs to provenance (domain 15). Keeping them in one feature is correct precisely so the split is explicit: the tractable half sets the ceiling on what automation can do, and the vintage half is the honest reminder that most of the value here is *not* automatable. One feature, two enforcement mechanisms, chosen deliberately. |
| DT-05 | Counterfeit merch (the Fan-facing, domain-20 half) can reuse the same expert-review remedy as vintage gear | Symmetry with the gear path | ❌ REJECTED — the economics invert. Merch is high-volume, low-value; a £25 counterfeit t-shirt cannot carry the cost of expert examination that a £20k instrument can. The Fan half must be served by **pattern/volume detection and buyer-protection refunds** (24.04), not per-item review. Forcing one remedy across a 1000× value range would either bankrupt the review budget or leave gear under-served. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | The control for misrepresented vintage is **provenance**, not detection | DT-01. There is nothing fake to detect. This makes domain 15 an enforcement dependency. |
| D-02 | Trademark exhaustion is checked before any brand claim is actioned | DT-02. Otherwise the register vetoes a lawful used market — which is domain 13's entire business. |
| D-03 | Innocent misrepresentation is a dispute, not an offence | Most misrepresented vintage was bought misrepresented. Enforcing against the current seller punishes the last victim in the chain. |
