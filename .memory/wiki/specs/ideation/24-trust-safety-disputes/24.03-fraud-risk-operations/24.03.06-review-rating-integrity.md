# Feature: Review & Rating Integrity

> **Number**: 24.03.06
> **Parent**: [Fraud & Risk Operations](./24.03-fraud-risk-operations-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Detection of the three ways a reputation system gets corrupted: **collusion rings** (reciprocal reviews
between accounts that transacted only to review each other), **brigading** (a coordinated pile-on against
an artist), and **extortion** ("refund me or I'll one-star you"). Review *mechanics* — transaction-gating,
double-blind reciprocal release, right of reply — belong to the `Reviews, Ratings & Portable Reputation`
cross-cut (24.03 D-04), which calls them the primary integrity control and is correct. What is left here
is the abuse that survives those mechanics.

The stakes are unusually high because the cross-cut identifies portable reputation as **"the strongest
structural argument for WeJammin existing at all."** A corrupted reputation graph does not degrade a
feature; it invalidates the thesis.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | None | Invisible as machinery, existential as an outcome. Their reputation is per-role and portable (the cross-cut's model), and it is what wins the next job (D-18). They are the primary **extortion target** — a session player facing a one-star from a client who wants their money back has more to lose than the fee. |
| Producer | None | Invisible. Primary **collusion** population: two producers can buy each other's cheapest service and exchange genuine, transaction-gated five-stars. Nothing in the mechanics stops it. |
| Operator | None | Invisible. Primary **brigade** target on the venue side — a room that cancelled a show, or that a local scene turned against, can be buried overnight. Also the party most exposed to the cross-cut's "payment-reliability ledger", which it calls the most legally dangerous element it contains. |
| Fan | None | Invisible. The **brigade population** (D-13: orders of magnitude more numerous). A fan pile-on against an artist over a statement or a cancelled show is the specific music failure mode this feature exists for. |

## Behavior

The detectors are graph detectors, and they run on the linkage substrate from
[24.03.02](./24.03.02-ato-ban-evasion-ring-detection.md) — a review ring is a fraud ring with a different
edge type (24.03 CX-02). Signals: reciprocity within a closed set, transaction value inconsistent with
review weight, temporal clustering of negative reviews from accounts with no prior relationship to the
subject, and negative reviews that arrive immediately after a refund refusal.

**Removal is an enforcement action**, not a moderation convenience: a removed review needs a cited rule
(24.02.04), a statement of reasons to its author (24.02.03), and an appeal (24.02.02). A platform that
silently deletes reviews it dislikes has no reputation system worth the name.

Reputation is **per-role** (the cross-cut's rule: "a superb mastering engineer may be an unreliable gear
seller"), which means integrity is per-role too — a ring operating in services must not contaminate the
same humans' gear reputation.

### Happy Path

1. A review is left through the cross-cut's transaction-gated flow
2. Integrity detectors evaluate it against the graph and the transaction context
3. Clean → published on the cross-cut's schedule (double-blind release)
4. Ring/brigade/extortion signal → held for review, not silently dropped
5. A reviewer decides; removal carries a cited rule, an SoR, and an appeal

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Two producers buy each other's £5 service and leave 5-stars | Transaction-gating passes — the transactions are real. Caught only by reciprocity + value-vs-weight | Reviews held; ring case opened |
| 300 fans one-star an artist in an hour after a controversy | Temporal clustering + no prior relationship + no transaction. **Brigading is a music-native failure mode** | Reviews held; the artist is not left to watch it happen |
| Client threatens a one-star unless refunded, then leaves it | Extortion signal: negative review immediately post-refund-refusal, with the threat in the thread (24.01.05) | Case with the thread as evidence (24.09) |
| Genuine cluster of bad reviews — the venue really did have a terrible month | **Must not suppress.** Clustering is also what real failure looks like | Nothing; reviews stand |
| A scene-wide falling-out produces coordinated but *sincere* negative reviews | Coordination is present; the reviews are honest. The detector cannot tell these apart | `[PENDING — /create-prd, Q-01]` — the hardest case in the feature, and an owner policy line rather than a detector setting |
| A triangulation seller's five-stars are entirely genuine | Reputation cannot help detect them (24.03.04 DT-02); nor can this feature | Nothing |
| Removal of a review that was actually honest | Author appeals; reversal must restore the review **and** the aggregate it moved | 24.02 CX-03's restoration problem, applied to a score |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Published | Clean | The review, on the cross-cut's release schedule |
| Held | Integrity signal | Reviewer: "under review"; subject: nothing yet |
| Removed | Confirmed abuse | Author: SoR + appeal route. Subject: the review is gone; the aggregate recalculates |
| Reinstated | Appeal upheld | Review returns; aggregate restated |
| Flagged, retained | Suspicious but unproven | Review stands; the signal is retained for the graph |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Transaction-gating solves fake reviews | The standard claim, made by the Reviews cross-cut itself | ❌ REJECTED — gating stops *non-buyers*; it does nothing against **collusion**. Two producers each buy the other's cheapest listed service for £5 and leave genuine, gated, verified five-star reviews. Every mechanic in the cross-cut passes. The only counter is graph analysis — reciprocity within a closed set, and review weight inconsistent with transaction value — which is 24.03.02's machinery, not the review system's. This is precisely why review integrity sits in fraud ops and not with reviews. |
| DT-02 | Review abuse on a music platform looks like review abuse on a marketplace | Reasonable prior | ❌ REJECTED — the dominant pattern is **brigading, not fake positives**, and it is a consequence of D-11. Fans outnumber professionals by orders of magnitude and music has a documented pile-on culture; an artist who says something unpopular, cancels a show, or falls out with a scene can be buried in hours by people with no transaction and no stake. Amazon's problem is sellers buying stars. This platform's problem is **a public buying revenge** — and the victim is the persona the product exists to serve. Optimising for the wrong pattern means building the wrong detector. |
| DT-03 | Removing a bad review is a moderation convenience | It feels like content cleanup | ❌ REJECTED — reputation here is **portable and cross-domain** (the cross-cut's whole point), so removing a review edits a record that follows a person into every other domain and into their next job. That makes removal an enforcement action against the review's author *and* a material change to the subject's asset. It needs a cited rule, an SoR and an appeal, and its reversal has the restoration problem 24.02 CX-03 describes — with the extra difficulty that an aggregate score has already been read by people who made decisions on it. |
