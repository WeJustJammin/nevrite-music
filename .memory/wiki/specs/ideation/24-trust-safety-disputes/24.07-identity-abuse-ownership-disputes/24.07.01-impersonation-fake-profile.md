# Feature: Impersonation & Fake Profile Enforcement

> **Number**: 24.07.01
> **Parent**: [Identity Abuse & Ownership Disputes](./24.07-identity-abuse-ownership-disputes-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Adjudication of claims that a profile is pretending to be someone it is not: a fake artist, a fake booking
agent, a fake A&R, a cloned venue listing, a session player's identity used to take deposits. The obvious
answer — verification badges — solves it for the famous and does nothing for the person this platform is
built for. The non-obvious answer is that **an impersonator can copy a name, a photo and a bio, but cannot
produce a counter-attested credit graph** (24.07 D-03).

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | The modal victim, and not the famous kind. A working session drummer's name and credits are copied by someone who then takes £200 "deposits" from people who think they are booking them. The victim finds out when an angry stranger emails them. They report, and they want two things: it gone, and everyone who paid told. |
| Producer | Full | Impersonated to harvest stems ("send me the tracks, I'll mix them") and to solicit fees. Their name carries the trust the scam borrows — `personas.md` calls them a **trust broker**, which is precisely what makes them worth impersonating. |
| Operator | Full | Their venue's photos, specs and name cloned into a fake listing taking deposits for shows that will never happen. The damage is to the real room's reputation, and they hear about it from a furious band at the door. |
| Fan | Read-only | Within this sub-domain, a bystander (24.07 Role Matrix). They **report** impersonators — that action is [24.01.01](../24.01-reporting-moderation/24.01.01-report-intake-notice-and-action.md)'s — and they are the primary *victim of the scam*, though not of the impersonation. Nobody impersonates a fan. |

## Behavior

An impersonation claim is adjudicated against the identity and credit graph, not against a badge. The
decisive question is not "who is famous" but "who can produce evidence of being this person" — and on this
platform that evidence is unusual: counter-attested credits, session records, verified collaborators, a
history of being paid for the work in question.

**Impersonation is usually a wrapper around a payment scam** (24.01.05 DT-02), which changes the response.
Removing the profile is necessary and nowhere near sufficient — the money already left, off-platform, by
bank transfer. The victims who paid are not the impersonated party, and they may not be on the platform
at all.

Enforcement removes the fake and sanctions the operator (24.02.01). Parody, tribute acts and fan accounts
are **not** impersonation — music has a large, legitimate tradition of all three, and a rule that cannot
tell a tribute band from a fake is a rule that bans tribute bands.

### Happy Path

1. Someone reports a profile as impersonating a person or an entity
2. The case assembles: profile content, the claimed identity's credit graph, session history, collaborators
3. The impersonated party (if on-platform) is asked for evidence — which they already have, as credits
4. The impersonator cannot produce counter-attested credits, because they were not in any room
5. Profile removed, operator sanctioned; anyone who transacted with them is warned
6. If money left off-platform, the victims are told — even though they are not the reporter

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Fake booking agent using a real agency's name | The highest-value music scam (24.01.05 DT-02). Removal is necessary and late — the deposits are gone | Victims warned; the real agency notified |
| Impersonated musician has **no credits on the platform yet** | The mechanism (D-03) does not work at cold start. Falls back to ordinary identity evidence | `[PENDING]` — 24.07 Q-03 is a real gap |
| Tribute band ("The Bootleg Beatles") | **Not impersonation.** A legitimate, large, legal part of live music | Nothing |
| Parody or fan account | Not impersonation, if it is not passing itself off | Nothing |
| Two people with the same name, both real musicians | **Not impersonation at all.** Common names are common | Disambiguation, not enforcement — a Canonical Data problem |
| Impersonator is impersonating a *dead* artist | Nobody has standing unless succession established an estate (24.07 CX-03) | `[PENDING]` — routed to 24.07.03 |
| A cloned venue listing with the real venue's photos | Also a copyright claim on the photos (24.05.01). Two routes, one incident | Both; they must not contradict |
| Impersonation of the platform itself ("WeJammin Support") | A phishing attack on every user at once, and the platform is the only party that can act | `[PENDING — /ideate-discover Step 5 deepening]` |

### States

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| None | Default | Nothing |
| Reported | Claim filed | Reporter: case ID |
| Evidence requested | Impersonated party is on-platform | "Can you confirm your identity?" — usually answered by credits they already hold |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | Verification badges solve impersonation | Every platform's answer, for fifteen years | ❌ REJECTED — **badges scale to the famous, and the famous are not this platform's users.** The modal victim here is a working session drummer that no verification programme would ever prioritise, whose stolen identity is being used to take £200 deposits. Badge-based systems structurally protect the people who least need protecting. **What WeJammin has instead is better and unique to it**: an impersonator can copy a name, a photo, a bio and a credit *list* — but they cannot produce a **counter-attested** credit graph, because that requires other real humans to have confirmed, at the time, that this person was in the room (D-18; `personas.md`: a Producer's attestation is worth more than a self-claim). Identity here is proved by the work, and the work has witnesses. That is the thesis applied to identity, and no competitor can retroactively manufacture it. |
| DT-02 | Impersonation harms the person being impersonated | The name says so | ❌ REJECTED as incomplete — **the party who loses money is a third party**, usually one the platform has no relationship with. The fake booking agent's victim is a band that wired £200 to a stranger; the impersonated agency loses reputation, not cash. This inverts the response design: removing the profile protects the *impersonated* party and does nothing for the people who actually paid, because the money left off-platform (24.01.05 DT-02). The platform must therefore **notify people who transacted with a confirmed impersonator**, including ones who are not the reporter and may not be users — which is a fan-out nobody plans for and which will be the first thing the press asks about. |
| DT-03 | An impersonation rule can be simple: don't pretend to be someone else | It sounds simple | ❌ REJECTED — music has an unusually rich tradition of **legitimate identity play**: tribute bands (a large, legal, professional live-music sector that literally trades on the name), cover bands, parody acts, stage names, band names that pass between line-ups, and artists who are legally several people. `personas.md` describes the multi-hyphenate; the industry adds pseudonyms on top — a producer with three aliases across genres is normal, not deceptive. A naive "no pretending" rule bans The Bootleg Beatles and every producer's alt-name project. **The rule has to hinge on passing-off with intent to deceive for gain**, not on resemblance — which means it cannot be automated and needs the music-literate reviewer 24.01.03 DT-02 already flagged as scarce. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | Identity is proved by the counter-attested credit graph, not by a badge | DT-01. The platform's differentiator, doing enforcement work. |
| D-02 | Confirmed impersonation notifies **everyone who transacted with the impersonator**, not just the reporter | DT-02. The people who lost money are third parties and nobody else will tell them. |
| D-03 | Tribute acts, parody, stage names and aliases are not impersonation | DT-03. The rule hinges on passing-off for gain, not on resemblance. |
