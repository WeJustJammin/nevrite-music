## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Ownership Disputes ↔ Impersonation

**Relationship**: The two are indistinguishable at intake and divergent at adjudication. "The manager who
left is still posting as us" is reported as impersonation. It is not: the manager may hold a legitimate,
un-revoked mandate on domain 01's membership graph, in which case they are the account's authorised
operator and the band's complaint is a governance failure, not an impersonation. Or the mandate lapsed and
they are impersonating. **The same facts, and the answer lives in domain 01's graph, not in the report.**

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the band member whose account it is, and who assumed the manager's access ended when the
  relationship did. It did not, because nobody revoked it.
- **Producer**: the same problem at a studio — an engineer who left, an account that still works.
- **Operator**: a venue page created by an ex-promoter, which is the most common version of this in live
  music and the one nobody plans for.
- **Fan**: not affected — they own no entity (24.07 Role Matrix).

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: neither feature owns the entity. **Domain 01 does**, and both read it. This
   sub-domain writes only the case and, on decision, requests a mandate change — it must never mutate the
   membership graph directly, or the ontology and the adjudication diverge.
2. **Trigger chain**: report → triage (impersonation or governance?) → resolve against the mandate graph →
   decision → mandate change (in 01) → access change (via the Roles cross-cut). If the mandate graph is
   incomplete — which it will be, because bands do not file paperwork — the case has **no factual basis**
   and becomes a judgment call.
3. **Permission intersection**: acutely. The whole dispute *is* a permission question, and the answer
   changes who may act for an entity that owns rights, money and a fan list.
4. **Notification fan-out**: every member of the entity, not just the reporter — the outcome changes the
   band's governance and the members have a stake even if they did not file.
5. **State transition conflict**: both parties can act on the account while the case is open. **Freezing
   consequential actions (payouts, releases, fan-list export, deletions) is probably mandatory** —
   the Audit Log cross-cut already names "a manager exporting the fan list on their way out" as a real
   incident type, and this is exactly the window in which it happens.

### CX-02: Succession ↔ Ownership Disputes

**Relationship**: Succession asks the same question with better documents. Probate produces evidence an
ordinary band dispute never has, and yet it is harder — because the party whose intent matters most cannot
be asked, and because the asset (a catalogue) keeps earning throughout.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the surviving members of a band with a deceased member, facing a governance deadlock on
  decisions that require unanimity.
- **Producer**: the estate of a producer holding points on records still earning.
- **Operator**: succession of a venue business, which is ordinary commercial succession and the least
  interesting case here.
- **Fan**: bystander; sees a memorial.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: same graph, same rule — this sub-domain requests, domain 01 owns.
2. **Trigger chain**: death notified → verify → freeze consequential actions but **not** accruals →
   establish the estate's standing → transfer control. Royalties must keep accruing throughout; a frozen
   account that stops earning is a financial loss inflicted on a grieving family by a data model.
3. **Permission intersection**: an executor is a new kind of mandate holder that domain 01's model may not
   have — one who acts for a person rather than being one.
4. **Notification fan-out**: co-owners, collaborators, and anyone whose splits are tied to the deceased's
   works. A death changes other people's rights records.
5. **State transition conflict**: an estate claim can race an ordinary ownership dispute — surviving band
   members and an executor claiming the same account, both legitimately. This is the worst case in the
   sub-domain and it is not rare.
