# Feature: Mastering Workflow & DSP Loudness Targets

> **Number**: 07.07.03
> **Parent**: [Mix & Master Workflow](./07.07-mix-master-workflow-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

The mastering stage as a tracked piece of work: the mix goes out, the master comes back, it gets measured against
the loudness the destination actually applies, every alternate gets the same treatment, and the record of who
mastered it survives. WeJammin does not master (sub-domain D-02) — it holds the requirements, the measurements
and the record around the work.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Read-only | Sees that mastering happened, by whom, and how the master measures. Mostly they want one thing: reassurance that it will not sound quiet on Spotify next to the records they love. That fear is real, extremely common, and largely misplaced — see DT-01. |
| Producer | Full | Runs the stage. In practice this role is often a **mastering engineer**, who is a distinct professional the persona set does not name (sub-domain Q-03). Sends the mix, receives the master, checks it against targets and against the alternates. |
| Operator | None | Not their business. |
| Fan | None | Not visible. |

## Behavior

The mastering stage inherits the approved canonical mix (`07.04.02`) and produces a master version. The platform's
contribution is measurement and consistency, not craft:

**Measurement**: integrated loudness (LUFS), true peak, dynamic range — measured on ingest, shown against each
destination's normalisation target.

**Consistency**: every alternate is mastered in the same pass, in the same chain (parent CX-02). This is the
expensive failure the feature exists to prevent.

**Carry-forward**: the brief's reference targets (parent CX-01, `07.07.01` DT-03) arrive here automatically, so
the mastering engineer knows what the artist was aiming at without anyone re-explaining it.

The loudness question needs care, because it is where the platform could easily do harm by being confidently
wrong — see DT-01.

### Happy Path

1. The mix is approved (`07.05.04`) and canonical (`07.04.02`); the stage board advances to `master`
2. Handoff package built for the mastering engineer (`07.08.01`) — the mix, the brief's targets, the alternate matrix
3. Master comes back; ingested as a version with lineage to the mix
4. System measures: −9.2 LUFS integrated, −0.8 dBTP, and shows what each destination will do to it
5. All three alternates return from the same pass, measuring within 0.2 LU of each other — so the instrumental sounds like the record

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Master is louder than every destination's target | Informational, **not an error** — this is a legitimate artistic choice and the platform has no standing to call it wrong | "−6.1 LUFS. Spotify will turn this down ~2 dB." — a fact, not a verdict |
| True peak exceeds 0 dBTP | Genuinely worth flagging: lossy encoding at the destination will clip audibly, and this one is objective | "True peak +0.4 dBTP — this may distort after encoding" |
| Alternates measure differently from the primary | The standard failure (parent CX-02) | "The instrumental is 1.8 LU quieter than the primary" |
| Master is quieter than the mix | Perfectly normal (a mix with headroom, a mastering engineer adding dynamics) and not an error | No flag |
| Mastering done by an outside engineer who is not on the platform | Very common. The master arrives as a manual upload from an email; lineage is guessed (`07.04.01` D-05) | Version created as a lineage root or with an inferred parent |
| Destination targets change | Platform-specific normalisation values move over time (they have repeatedly) | Targets must be data, not hardcoded — see DT-03 |
| Artist asks "why is my record quieter than X?" | The most common real question in this feature | Comparison against measured references from the brief (`07.07.01` DT-03) |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | The platform should **enforce or recommend** a target loudness (e.g. "master to −14 LUFS for Spotify") | It is the single most common piece of advice in independent mastering, it is what artists ask for, and it looks like exactly the helpful guidance a platform should provide | ❌ **REJECTED — and it is the most dangerous plausible feature in this sub-domain.** "Master to −14 for Spotify" is folk wisdom that is *wrong in a specific and harmful way*: streaming normalisation turns loud masters **down**, it does not turn quiet ones up to match — so the loudness war is over on the loudness axis, but a master made deliberately quiet to "hit −14" simply arrives quiet, with its dynamics unexploited, and gains nothing. Meanwhile the platforms' targets differ, change, and are user-defeatable. A platform that stamps "❌ too loud" on an artist's master would be **wrong on the craft, wrong on the facts, and overriding a professional's deliberate decision** — precisely what `.claude/rules/decision-classification.md` says the agent must not do to the user. The correct behavior is to state facts and project consequences: "−6.1 LUFS; Spotify will turn this down about 2 dB." No verdict. This is the same principle as `07.05.03` D-01 (surface, do not adjudicate) and `07.02.02` D-01 (evidence, not arithmetic) — **the third instance of the domain's deepest pattern: measure and show, never judge.** |
| DT-02 | True peak overs are the one thing genuinely worth flagging | Distinguishing the objective from the aesthetic. Loudness is a choice; a true peak over 0 dBTP is a *fact* about what happens next — lossy encoding at the destination reconstructs a waveform that exceeds the original peaks, and it will clip audibly. The artist did not choose that; it is a consequence they cannot hear on their own system. | ✅ CONFIRMED — and it draws the line for the whole feature: **flag consequences the user cannot perceive; report facts about choices they can.** True peak is invisible until the master is on Spotify, at which point it is too late. Loudness is audible the moment you play it. That distinction is what separates a useful warning from a patronising one, and it generalises well beyond this feature. |
| DT-03 | Destination loudness targets can be encoded in the product | Necessary for the projection behavior to work at all | ⚠️ **CONFIRMED WITH A MAINTENANCE TRAP.** The targets are real and knowable — but they are **third-party values that change without notice**, they differ per destination, several are user-defeatable in client settings, and some are not officially published at all. Hardcoding them guarantees the platform is confidently wrong within a year, which is worse than silence because users will trust it. They must be **data, maintained deliberately, with a visible "as of" date** — and the projection must be framed as an estimate. This is a small feature with an ongoing operational cost that nobody notices at design time, and it is worth naming now: a projection nobody maintains becomes misinformation. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | The platform **measures and projects; it never prescribes a loudness target or judges a master** | Per DT-01 — the folk wisdom is wrong, the targets move, and overriding a professional's deliberate choice is out of bounds. |
| D-02 | **True peak overs are flagged**; loudness is reported | Per DT-02 — flag consequences the user cannot perceive; report facts about choices they can. |
| D-03 | Destination targets are **maintained data with a visible "as of" date**, and projections are framed as estimates | Per DT-03 — a projection nobody maintains becomes misinformation. |
| D-04 | Destination specs are **owned by domain 12**, referenced here | Two copies of third-party requirements would drift, and one would be wrong. |
| D-05 | A master quieter than the mix, or louder than every target, is **not an error** | Both are legitimate and common artistic choices. |
