## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Shadow Party ↔ Claim & Proof

**Relationship**: The sub-domain's spine, and the platform's growth loop. A shadow party accumulates credits from people who were in the room; the invitation says "you have three credits waiting"; the claim flow converts. Every other platform asks a new user to build a profile from nothing — this one hands them one built by their collaborators.

**Role scoping**:
- **Producer**: creates shadows as a byproduct of capture; is rarely the claimant.
- **Musician**: the archetypal claimant of a person shadow.
- **Operator**: claimant of a seeded/named venue — the highest-value conversion in the domain.
- **Fan**: absent from both.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The shadow party record is the same object before and after the claim; claiming attaches an owner rather than creating anything. Nothing merges, so nothing can be lost — which is why the accumulated history survives the conversion.
2. **Trigger chain**: Shadow created → invitation dispatched (best-effort, D-04: capture is not conditional on delivery) → claim initiated → proof → ownership. If the invitation never lands, the shadow persists and the credit stands. The chain is designed to survive its own failure at every link.
3. **Permission intersection**: A shadow party cannot act (01.05.01 D-05); claiming is the only thing that grants agency. That is the sub-domain's whole authority boundary.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Invitation to the subject. Should the *attesters* be told when their subject claims? Probably — it closes their loop and confirms their capture worked. `[PENDING — Step 5]`
5. **State transition conflict**: A shadow being credited by a second attester while a claim is in flight is safe — credits accrete to the party, and the claim attaches an owner. Orthogonal writes.

### CX-02: Claim & Proof ↔ Contested Claims

**Relationship**: One decision, two files. 01.05.02 optimises the funnel by ranking proof methods by speed rather than strength (D-02), which necessarily raises the error rate. 01.05.03 makes that affordable by making every ownership change reversible (D-03). Specify either alone and it is indefensible: fast proof without reversibility is a land-grab machine; slow proof without it kills the Operator conversion the domain overview names as core.

**Role scoping**:
- **Operator**: benefits from the fast path; exposed to the squatter case the reversibility covers.
- **Musician**: weakest proof, therefore most dependent on contest as a correction.
- **Producer**: witness in both — their attestation is proof at claim time and evidence at contest time.
- **Fan**: absent.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Ownership is the shared state. During a contest neither party may write it, and (01.05.03 D-04) neither loses existing access. The incumbent keeps operating while contested.
2. **Trigger chain**: Weak proof → provisional control → second claim → contest → decision → reversibility window → final. The window is a compensating action, not a rollback: the ownership change already happened and is undone forward, leaving both facts in the audit trail.
3. **Permission intersection**: Yes — provisional control is real control with a visible provisional marker. What it may *not* do (transfer onward, dissolve, spend) is `[PENDING — Step 5]` and is a live gap: a provisional owner who can immediately transfer the party defeats the reversibility window.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Contest notifies both claimants; a decision notifies both with reasons. A silent loss loses the user permanently (01.05.03 Happy Path step 5).
5. **State transition conflict**: A claim completing at the same moment a contest opens — the contest must win the race, or a provisional owner becomes final mid-dispute.

### CX-03: Shadow Party ↔ Contested Claims

**Relationship**: The uncomfortable one. 01.05.01 D-01 makes shadow creation unauthenticated because the thesis requires it, and 01.05.01 DT-02 accepts that this is an impersonation and spam surface made survivable by accountability rather than prevention. The bill for that decision is paid here: every duplicate shadow, every impersonated musician, every squatted venue becomes this feature's caseload. The sub-domain deliberately trades a contest workload for a capture rate.

**Role scoping**:
- **Producer**: the highest-volume shadow creator, therefore the highest-volume upstream cause.
- **Musician**: the impersonation target.
- **Operator**: the squatted-page target.
- **Fan**: cannot create shadows — which is precisely why the largest population contributes nothing to this caseload.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Two shadows for one human is not a contest — it is a merge (Canonical Data cross-cut), and the credits must redirect rather than be rewritten (domain CX-02). Distinguishing "two records, one person" from "two people, one claim" is a real classification problem at the front of this feature.
2. **Trigger chain**: Loose shadow creation → duplicate/impersonating parties → claims → contests. The volume here is a *function of* 01.05.01 Q-01's answer (what limits apply), which means Q-01 cannot be answered without costing this feature.
3. **Permission intersection**: Creator attribution (01.05.01 D-03) is the evidence trail a contest reads. Without it, contests would be unresolvable.
4. **Notification fan-out**: An impersonation contest should notify the attesters, not just the claimants — they are the witnesses.
5. **State transition conflict**: Two shadows merging while one is contested. Unmodelled. `[PENDING — Step 5]`

### CX-04: Shadow Party ↔ Claim & Proof (attestation as proof)

**Relationship**: The loop that makes the domain defensible. The people who created the shadow party by crediting someone are the same people who can confirm the claimant is that someone. The provenance graph — built for credits — turns out to be an identity-verification substrate. A competitor cannot copy it, because copying it would require having been in the room.

**Role scoping**:
- **Producer**: the trust broker (`personas.md`) whose attestation carries the most weight.
- **Musician**: both claimant (needs attestation) and attester (provides it for others).
- **Operator**: less applicable — their proof is business-shaped.
- **Fan**: absent, and must stay absent: fan attestation would make reputation purchasable at scale.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: None. Credits are 02's; this reads them.
2. **Trigger chain**: Claim with no strong proof → attesters asked → confirmations → claim resolved. If no attester responds, the claim stalls honestly (01.05.02 Edge Cases) rather than failing.
3. **Permission intersection**: An attester's confirmation grants an identity claim — meaningful authority, held by anyone who ever credited anyone. Collusion is the obvious attack: two accounts crediting each other and confirming each other's claims. Mitigation is 02's counter-attestation weighting, not this file's. Worth escalating.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Attesters are asked; asking is itself a re-engagement event.
5. **State transition conflict**: An attester's own party being contested while they act as a witness — their evidentiary weight during that period is unspecified. `[PENDING — Step 5]`
