# Feature: Estates, Deceased Users & Legacy Accounts

> **Number**: 01.10
> **Parent**: [Identity, Profiles & Organizations](./identity-profiles-organizations-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

What happens to a party when the person behind it dies. In most industries this is an account-closure problem; in music it is a **decades-long administration** problem, because a catalogue outlives its author by a lifetime plus seventy years, royalties keep arriving, licensing requests keep coming, and someone has to be able to answer them. This feature covers memorialisation of the public identity, nomination of a successor while the person is alive, and the estate's access to the parts of the account that keep generating money.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Nominates a successor while alive — the single cheapest action in this feature and the one that makes everything else possible. Their catalogue, credits and royalty streams are what the estate will administer. Their credits are permanent and untouchable in every scenario. |
| Producer | Full | Same nomination. Additionally exposed as a **counterparty**: a producer holding points on a deceased artist's catalogue needs someone able to answer a licensing request, or their own income stalls behind a party that cannot respond. |
| Operator | Full | Nominates for their own account, and their org needs a succession path independent of it — a venue whose sole owner dies still has shows booked next week, and its lifecycle (01.02.02) must not depend on a person's. |
| Fan | Read-only | Sees the memorialised profile. This is not cosmetic: the fan relationship to an artist frequently outlives the artist, and a page that silently keeps behaving as if the person is alive — or that vanishes — are both real failures with real grief attached. |

> **Access levels**: Full · Config · Read-only · Reports · None

## Behavior

Three distinct capabilities, deliberately kept simple at breadth:

1. **Successor nomination** — a living person names who should administer their party. Cheap, done in advance, and the only thing that makes the rest tractable.
2. **Memorialisation** — the public identity's state changes: the profile persists, is marked, and stops behaving as an active participant (no new bookings, no service offers, no listings). Credits, portfolio and catalogue remain.
3. **Estate access** — a verified successor gains a scoped ability to administer what continues: royalties, licensing requests, rights decisions. **Not** a login as the deceased. The estate acts as itself, on behalf of the party — which is a representation edge (01.03.02), not an identity handover.

The invariant, consistent with the whole domain: **death terminates authority, never the record.** Every credit, every membership edge, every attestation the person made stands exactly as it was.

Probate, legal succession and inheritance are out of scope. The platform records a nomination and verifies a successor as best it can; it does not adjudicate who inherits.

### Happy Path

1. Musician nominates a successor in settings. One field, done once.
2. Years later, they die. The successor (or a bandmate, or a family member) reports it.
3. Report is verified `[PENDING — Q-02, /create-prd-security: by what evidence]`. The party is memorialised: profile marked, active participation stopped.
4. Nominated successor is granted scoped estate access — royalties and licensing, not impersonation.
5. Catalogue keeps earning; royalties keep flowing; a licensing request gets an answer. The credits are permanent. The record of the career survives its author, which is the whole point of the platform stated at its limit.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| No successor nominated — the overwhelmingly common case | Memorialisation can proceed on a verified report; estate access cannot be granted to nobody. The catalogue keeps earning into a party nobody can administer | `[PENDING — Q-01 owner decision, /create-prd]` — see Q-01. Money accruing to an unadministrable party is a real, durable, bad state |
| False death report (malicious or mistaken) | Catastrophic and reversible-in-principle: the person's account is frozen and their page marked. Verification must be strong precisely because the harm is severe and the target cannot object | See Q-02 |
| Deceased was the only mandate holder for a band | The band cannot administer itself. Same rule as departure (01.03.01), but nobody can be blocked from dying | Band needs a mandate recovery path. See domain CX-09 |
| Deceased was a band member | Membership edge ends; their share passes to the estate per governance (01.04.01) or law. Their credits and lineup history are untouched | 01.04.01/01.04.04 |
| Estate wants the profile removed | Tension: the family's wishes against a public record other people's careers cite. A deleted profile would break every credit that references it — third parties' evidence, destroyed by a request from someone who is not them | See Q-03; the domain's hardest values question |
| Successor is nominated but disputed by the family | Platform is not a probate court (D-04). Nomination is evidence, not a verdict | Route to 24 / legal |
| Deceased Operator's venue | The org's lifecycle must not depend on a person's. Mandate recovery is an org problem, not an estate problem | 01.02.02 |
| Fan follows a memorialised artist | Follow persists. New releases from the catalogue still happen — posthumous releases are normal in music | Memorialised state on the page |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | This is account closure with extra steps | The generic-platform framing, and why the sweep placed it last | ❌ REJECTED — music inverts it. A catalogue earns for **life plus seventy years**; D-10 puts royalties, licensing and rights at the core of the product. So the deceased party is not a closed account, it is a *long-lived commercial entity that will never log in again*. Every other platform's memorialisation is about grief and photos; here it is grief, photos, **and decades of income administration**. Closing the account would stop the money. |
| DT-02 | Estate access means the successor logs in as the deceased | The path of least resistance, and what most platforms effectively allow via password sharing | ❌ REJECTED, and this is where the domain's own principles decide it. Impersonating the deceased would let an estate *attest credits in their name* — a dead person confirming a split they never saw. That is the platform's core artifact, forged, permanently, by someone with a financial interest. The estate must act **as itself, on behalf of the party** — which the domain already models as representation (01.03.02). No new mechanism needed; the answer falls out of the graph. |
| DT-03 | The cheap half is nomination, and it is separable from the expensive half | Reasoning about MoSCoW: what part of this is affordable early? | ✅ CONFIRMED, and it splits the feature's priority cleanly. Successor **nomination** is one field, done once, by a living user — trivial to build and worthless to retrofit, because the person it needed is gone. Memorialisation, verification and estate administration are a genuine flow with legal edges, and they are late-cohort. This is the same shape as 01.04.04 (the dissolution flow is a `could`; the data it consumes is not) and the same argument: **capture the fact early, build the flow late.** The pattern recurring twice in one domain is itself a signal about how the whole map should be sequenced. |
