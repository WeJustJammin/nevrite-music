## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-04: Service Mode ↔ Seller Capacity

**Relationship**: A remote engagement consumes **queue depth** (one of N concurrent slots, duration uncertain, pausable). An on-location engagement consumes a **calendar slot** (a specific date, perishable, held via the Availability cross-cut, and entangled with a room's date in domain 16). A seller who sells both — which is the normal case, not the exotic one, because `meta/personas.md` establishes the multi-hyphenate as the structural centre — has two scarcity models pointing at one body and one week.

The failure is concrete: a mix engineer with 3 remote mixes in flight accepts an on-location tracking date on Thursday. Neither model knows about the other. Thursday's calendar hold does not decrement the queue, and the queue does not know Thursday is gone. The seller is committed twice and one of the two commitments breaks — most likely the remote mix, silently, as a turnaround breach (CX-05).

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: fully exposed. The session player selling both remote overdubs and live tracking dates is the archetype, and they are the persona least able to absorb the cost of a double-commit.
- **Producer**: fully exposed, and worse, because their remote work has the longest durations — a Producer's queue slot may be occupied for three weeks, spanning many calendar dates.
- **Operator**: exposed indirectly and blindly. When the double-commit resolves as a no-show, the Operator loses the room's night — the costliest failure named in their persona — caused by a conflict in a domain they cannot see.
- **Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The contested entity is the seller's *time*, and today neither model owns it. Neither the queue nor the calendar is the authority. Resolution requires one authority over the human's committed time, with both models as views onto it — but that authority does not exist in the current cross-cut set (the Availability cross-cut is calendar-shaped by design, per 05.01.07 DT-01). **Unresolved — escalated to `/create-prd-architecture` (05.01.07 Q-01).**
2. **Trigger chain**: Accepting an on-location date should decrement remote capacity for the affected period; accepting a remote job should reduce on-location availability. Neither trigger exists. Direction of causation is symmetric, which is a hint that a shared underlying commitment model is the right answer rather than two features signalling each other.
3. **Permission intersection**: None. The same seller controls both.
4. **Notification fan-out**: A double-commit should warn the seller at accept time — this is the cheapest available mitigation and does not require the full model.
5. **State transition conflict**: Yes, and it races. Two orders (one remote, one on-location) accepted concurrently can both succeed against a seller who can only serve one. The Availability cross-cut's reservation half provides atomic decrement for the calendar side only; the queue side has no equivalent guard.

### CX-05: Turnaround ↔ Seller Capacity

**Relationship**: Turnaround is a promise; capacity is the seller's ability to keep it. The two are coupled through the Search & Discovery and Reviews cross-cuts in a loop the platform creates: good reviews → higher rank → more orders → ceiling exceeded → breached turnaround → bad reviews.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician / Producer**: the seller experiences this as the platform punishing them for succeeding on it. The rational escape is to take work off-platform — the exact behaviour `meta/personas.md` names as the Musician's "worst intentional" act, provoked here by the platform's own ranking.
- **Operator**: not affected (turnaround does not apply to their lens).
- **Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The seller's throughput. Capacity owns the ceiling; turnaround owns the per-job clock. No conflict of ownership, but a missing feedback path.
2. **Trigger chain**: Reaching the ceiling must make listings non-orderable *before* a turnaround is promised, not after. If capacity fails, turnaround breaches — capacity is upstream and the failure is not compensable, only visible.
3. **Permission intersection**: None.
4. **Notification fan-out**: An engagement that would push the seller over their ceiling should warn at accept time; a paused engagement squatting a slot should nudge the buyer (05.01.07 edge cases).
5. **State transition conflict**: A rush order and a standard order arriving together can both fit under the ceiling numerically while being jointly undeliverable in time — capacity as a *count* is a lossy proxy for capacity as *hours*. Recorded as 05.01.07 Q-03.

### CX-01: Taxonomy ↔ Pricing Models

**Relationship**: The craft axis constrains the legal pricing models. This is a hard constraint, not a suggestion — it keeps the seller's form short and prevents nonsense listings. It also has a downstream consequence neither feature owns alone: because `points` is legal only for creative crafts, the set of engagements that *cannot be escrowed* (05.01.03 DT-01) is determined by the taxonomy.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician / Producer**: see only the models relevant to the craft they picked.
- **Operator**: sees hourly/day only; points, per-song and per-minute are absent from this lens entirely.
- **Fan**: sees a rendered figure, never the model set.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The craft→model mapping is reference data. Owned here (this domain owns the craft axis, per 05.01.02 D-02); no merge conflict.
2. **Trigger chain**: Craft selection filters the model set synchronously at listing time. If a craft is later retired or remapped, existing listings may hold a now-illegal model — the migration gap recorded as 05.01.02 Q-03.
3. **Permission intersection**: The Operator lens restricts the craft set, which transitively restricts the model set. A permission in the taxonomy therefore changes what is priceable — a real intersection.
4. **Notification fan-out**: None.
5. **State transition conflict**: None — both are configuration-time, not runtime.

## Rejected Pairs

| R-01 | 05.01.04 Benchmarking | 05.01.07 Capacity | Considered: should a band reflect scarcity (busy sellers charge more)? Rejected — surge-priced human labour is a values failure in a domain where the buyer is often another working musician, and the data would be circular (busy → higher band → higher prices → busier). No shared state, no trigger. |
| R-02 | 05.01.02 Taxonomy | 05.01.06 Turnaround | Considered: should craft imply a default turnaround? Rejected — turnaround varies by seller and job by an order of magnitude within one craft (a 2-day demo mix and a 3-week album mix are both "mixing"). A craft-level default would be wrong more often than right and would be trusted because the platform said it. |
| R-03 | 05.01.04 Benchmarking | 05.01.05 Service Mode | Mode is a benchmark *facet*, not an interaction — it partitions cohorts and nothing flows between the features. The facet relationship is already carried by CX-08 via the taxonomy. |

The band renders in the pricing *step*, not on the public listing. **Scoped to listing surfaces** (2026-07-30, run-3 owner queue `05-services-marketplace#010`): this rejection is about a public, browse-scale shopfront and says nothing about a live quote, where 05.01.04 D-05 and 05.02.01 D-15 render the band to both parties once it is issued. |
