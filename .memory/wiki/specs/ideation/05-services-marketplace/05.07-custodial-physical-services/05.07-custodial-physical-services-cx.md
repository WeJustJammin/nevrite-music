## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01 / CX-02: Custody Chain ↔ Repair Job Flow

**Relationship**: These two are one thing viewed from two angles. The repair flow is what the seller *does*; the custody chain is what is *true* while they do it. Neither is coherent alone — a job flow with no custody model is a workshop with no paperwork, and a custody chain with no job is an object in a box.

CX-02 is the non-obvious half and the more important one. The naive model compares the intake condition record to the return condition record and calls the delta damage. That model flags **every completed job**, because the delta *is* the work: a refret changes the instrument permanently, a refinish changes it irreversibly, a re-cap changes what is inside the amp. So the comparison cannot be intake-vs-return. It must be **intake + approved estimate → expected return state**, measured against actual return.

That makes the approved estimate do double duty: it is the commercial agreement (05.07.01) *and* the specification of permitted change (05.07.03 DT-03). Which in turn means a revised estimate mid-job — the seized truss rod case — is not only a re-price, it is an **amendment to what the seller is permitted to do to someone's property**. That framing is stronger than "the price went up" and is the right one for a buyer whose instrument is currently in pieces on a bench 200 miles away.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the owner of the irreplaceable object. They experience the condition record as the first time anyone has written down what their guitar looked like before it went away.
- **Producer**: the same for capital equipment, where the loss is downtime as well as value.
- **Operator**: on both sides constantly — their PA goes to a tech, and bands leave backline in their room. The only persona routinely on both ends of a custody chain.
- **Fan**: none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The item's condition. The job flow proposes changes to it; the custody chain records them. The approved estimate is the arbiter between the two, which is why it must be immutable per version like a quote (05.02.01's machinery, applied to estimates).
2. **Trigger chain**: Intake → condition locked → estimate → approval (which *authorises* change) → work → return condition → comparison against intake+approved. If the estimate is revised mid-job, the authorisation is amended and the expected return state moves with it. A revision that is approved verbally and never recorded leaves the custody chain measuring against a stale specification — which would flag authorised work as damage.
3. **Permission intersection**: Yes, and it is unusually literal. The approved estimate is a **permission to alter someone else's property**. No other feature in domain 05 grants a permission over a physical object.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Every custody hand-off notifies both parties. Photo updates during the job are the trade's own existing habit and are the cheapest trust-building the platform gets.
5. **State transition conflict**: Damage discovered at return while the job is still marked in progress; or a revised estimate issued while the item is already being returned. Both need a defined precedence; `[PENDING]`.

### CX-03: Inspection ↔ Repair Job Flow — the conflict

**Relationship**: An inspection that finds work is a repair estimate waiting to happen, and the inspector is the obvious person to do it — they have seen it, they are qualified, they are local. That is a genuine convenience and a genuine structural incentive to find work.

It is exactly the MOT-centre problem: the party who diagnoses is the party who profits from the diagnosis. The trade has never solved it, and it would be arrogant to claim this drilling has. What can be said honestly:

- The conflict is **structural**, not a matter of the inspector's character.
- The platform can *see* it, because it holds both engagements and the collaboration graph (03).
- Separating the roles removes the conflict and removes the natural expert — in a thin market, possibly leaving nobody.

The unresolved question (05.07.02 Q-02) is whether the platform bars an inspector from quoting the work they recommended. Recorded, not decided.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the buyer who commissioned independence and may receive a sales pitch.
- **Operator**: same, at higher volume.
- **Producer**: read-only in this sub-domain; minimally exposed.
- **Fan**: none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The report. It is an inspection's deliverable and a repair's input, and the two engagements have different payers — the gear *buyer* commissions the inspection, and the *owner* commissions the repair, who may be a different person entirely if the sale did not complete.
2. **Trigger chain**: Report → (optionally) estimate. The transition crosses an engagement boundary and, often, a domain boundary into 13.
3. **Permission intersection**: The inspection's independence check (05.07.02 D-03) should probably also consider whether the inspector *became* the repairer on prior jobs — a pattern the platform can see and a single instance it cannot judge.
4. **Notification fan-out**: If the report goes to a gear buyer (13) and the repair would be commissioned by the gear seller, the report has two audiences with opposed interests. Who sees it is not obvious.
5. **State transition conflict**: None mechanically.

### CX-04: Inspection ↔ Custody Chain — the useful accident

**Relationship**: The most valuable finding in this sub-domain, and it was not visible from either feature alone. The custody chain's weakest field is **declared value** — required, load-bearing, unverifiable, and misstated by both parties for opposite reasons (05.07.03 DT-01). An inspection is a paid, independent, expert assessment of exactly that, and it can happen *before* handover.

So the two features solve each other: an inspection gives the custody chain a defensible value, and the custody chain gives an inspection a second reason to exist beyond gating a gear sale.

The better long-term answer points outside this domain entirely. Domain 15 (Gear Registry & Ownership) — one of the map's two whitespace domains — is a place where an instrument's identity, provenance, service history and value would already be established, before anyone needs it in a hurry, at a moment when nobody has an incentive to lie. That is a stronger argument for 15's existence than anything in 15's own rationale, and it arrived from a repair workflow, which is worth carrying to 15's drilling.

**Role scoping**: Musician, Producer and Operator all Full; Fan none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The item's value. Currently self-declared by the buyer; potentially established by an inspection; ideally held by 15. Three candidate owners and no decision — 05.07.03 Q-02.
2. **Trigger chain**: Inspection → value established → declared value pre-filled and evidenced. Optional, and its optionality is the point: nobody inspects a £300 guitar before a setup.
3. **Permission intersection**: None.
4. **Notification fan-out**: None.
5. **State transition conflict**: None.
