# Feature: Fixers & Ensemble Contracting

> **Number**: 05.05.02
> **Parent**: [Multi-Party Supply](./05.05-multi-party-supply-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

Buying an ensemble rather than a person. A producer needs a string quartet, a horn section, a twelve-piece for a film cue — they do not want to hire twelve individuals, negotiate twelve rates and chase twelve invoices. They call a **fixer**: one contact, one fee, an ensemble that turns up and can play. The fixer books the players, sets the desk, handles the parts, and takes a margin nobody discusses.

The fixer is a real and load-bearing profession in the session world, entirely invisible to every existing marketplace, and structurally dangerous to this platform's core asset.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | On both sides, and the sides are very different. As a **fixer** — a role many senior session players occupy — contracts an ensemble, assembles a section from their own book, and is paid one fee to deliver twelve humans. As a **contracted player**, gets a date from a fixer they trust, which is how a large share of session work is allocated; here their exposure is that the fixer's engagement is with the buyer, not with them, and today their credit depends on a fixer remembering to write their name down. |
| Producer | Full | The archetypal buyer. Needs a section, buys a section, does not want twelve conversations. Their `Success Criteria` is credits captured "as a byproduct of running the session — zero extra admin" — and the fixer is exactly the intermediary that today makes that impossible, because the Producer never met eight of the twelve people who played on their record. |
| Operator | None | Not visible under this lens. The room a twelve-piece needs is a domain 16 booking; the ensemble's commercial structure is not the Operator's business. |
| Fan | None | Not visible. |

> **Access levels**: Full · Config · Read-only · Reports · None

## Behavior

One buyer→fixer engagement, plus **N fixer→player engagements**. The buyer sees one price and one counterparty. The platform must model both layers, because the buyer's layer is where the money is and the players' layer is where the music is.

The fixer's own value is legitimate and substantial: knowing who can sight-read the part, who blends, who turns up, and being able to assemble that in an afternoon. They are paid for a book of contacts and a reputation for delivering — which is exactly the kind of thing this platform is otherwise trying to make legible.

### Happy Path

1. Producer requests a quote for a string quartet on a date
2. Fixer quotes one fee covering players, contracting, parts and their margin
3. Producer accepts; escrow captures against the fixer engagement
4. Fixer books four players, each becoming a fixer→player engagement with their own terms
5. Session happens (on-location — 05.01.05 — so a room is involved, from domain 16)
6. Producer accepts the fixer's delivery → escrow releases to the fixer
7. Fixer's engagements with the four players settle
8. **Four credits emit — one per player — plus the fixer's contracting credit.** Not one credit for the fixer. See DT-01.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| A contracted player is not on the platform | Constant. A fixer's book is thirty years old and mostly phone numbers. | Same gap as 05.05.01 Q-02 — **the feature's biggest practical obstacle** |
| Buyer accepts and pays the fixer; fixer does not pay the players | The classic fixer failure, and the one the platform can actually fix | Players' engagements should settle from the same escrow release, not from the fixer's goodwill — see DT-03 |
| One player deps out | A dep inside a fixer engagement — recursive substitution (05.05.01) | Fixer's problem, but the credit still follows the hands |
| Buyer wants to know who played | They usually do not, at booking. They very much do, at credits time — often years later. | The credit graph answers this by construction, which is the feature's real value |
| Fixer's margin | Historically undisclosed and normal | Sub-domain Q-02 — forcing disclosure may drive fixers off-platform, which loses their players too |
| Union session (05.02.03) | A fixer date is very often a union date | Scale floors apply per player, not to the fixer's aggregate fee |
| Fixer contracts themselves as a player | Extremely common — the fixer is usually in the section | Two roles, two credits, one human. Domain 01's multi-role identity handles it. |
| Session overruns | Overtime is owed to every player | Derives from session facts (05.02.03 DT-02), not from the fixer's arithmetic |
| Fixer never records who played | The current world's default | The engagement structure is what prevents it — the players' engagements *are* the record |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | **A fixer engagement, modelled naively, deletes the string section from the credit graph — and this is the single worst thing this domain could do to the platform's thesis** | Adversarial: trace one buyer→one seller through 02 and D-18. | ✅ CONFIRMED, and it is the reason this sub-domain exists as a unit (D-02). The naive model is seductive: the buyer paid the fixer, the fixer delivered, so the engagement is buyer→fixer and the credit goes to the seller of record. The result is **one credit, to a contractor, for a twelve-piece section they may not have played a note of** — and twelve working musicians who performed on a record are invisible in the system whose entire promise is that it captures who did what at the moment it was true. This is not a gap like Jaxsta's; it is a *fabrication*, generated automatically, at scale, on exactly the sessions that matter most (orchestral, film/TV, big-budget records — the work with the most players and the least individual visibility). It would also hit the persona least able to absorb it: the anonymous section player is precisely the musician whose career "exists in fragments" and who most needs proof of work to accumulate. The engagement structure must therefore be **N+1 engagements, N+1 credits** — the fixer credited for contracting, each player credited for playing. The two-layer model is not a nicety of completeness; it is what stops the feature from poisoning the well. |
| DT-02 | The fixer is just a reseller and this is the same as subcontracting (05.05.03) | Consolidation instinct | ❌ REJECTED — the difference is real and it is in the direction of the work. A subcontractor **delegates the same job** to someone else who does it instead of them: one job, one substitute, and the buyer arguably should not care as long as it is done. A fixer **assembles a group** to do a job that no individual could do: twelve jobs, twelve humans, done in parallel, none of them substitutable for the fixer. The credit consequences diverge accordingly — a subcontracted job produces one credit that should follow the hands (05.05.01 DT-01), while a fixer engagement produces N+1 credits with a distinct contracting role. The union consequences diverge too: scale applies per player, not to the fixer's aggregate fee. Merging them would produce a model that gets one of the two wrong, and the one it would get wrong is the twelve-person case. |
| DT-03 | **The players should be paid from the buyer's escrow release, not from the fixer's cashflow** | Adversarial: what is the failure mode a domain expert expects that nobody mentioned? | ✅ CONFIRMED as the right shape, though it needs the payments architecture to agree. The classic fixer failure — old as the profession — is that the buyer pays the fixer and the players wait, or do not get paid at all, because the fixer's cashflow has gone elsewhere. Nobody has leverage: the players' contract is with the fixer, not the buyer, and their remedy is to stop taking that fixer's calls. The platform can genuinely fix this, because it is simultaneously the payment rail and the holder of all N+1 engagements: the escrow release can settle the players' engagements **in the same transaction** rather than depositing a lump into the fixer's account and hoping. The Payments cross-cut already describes multi-party split payments where "a single payment routinely splits across a band, a producer, co-writers and an agent's commission" — this is that mechanism, with the fixer's margin as the residual rather than the whole. It is also a genuinely new thing in the world for session players, which makes it worth more than its complexity. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | A fixer engagement is **N+1 engagements producing N+1 credits** | The naive one-seller model fabricates a credit and deletes a section. See DT-01. |
| D-02 | Players settle from the buyer's escrow release, in the same transaction — not from the fixer's cashflow | Fixes the profession's oldest failure using the platform's unique position as both rail and record. See DT-03. |
| D-03 | The fixer's contracting work is itself a credited role | It is real work with real skill, and crediting it is what makes the two-layer model honest rather than a bypass. |
| D-04 | Union scale applies per player, not to the fixer's aggregate fee | Otherwise the aggregate hides a below-scale player. |
