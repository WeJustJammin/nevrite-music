## The Sub-Domain's Single Rule

Before the details: all four features resolve to one rule, discovered independently by each and stated here once.

> **The credit follows the hands.** The person who played, mixed or wrote is credited — never the fixer, the agency, the assembler, or the seller who delegated.

This is the reason these four are one sub-domain (D-02). Individually they look like four unrelated commercial conveniences; together they are four different ways to insert a party between the money and the labour, and every one of them is a place where the platform's core asset can silently acquire a false record. A model that got the payment chain right and the credit chain wrong would pass every test anyone would think to run — both parties got paid, nobody complains — while quietly fabricating the thing D-18 says the user cannot leave without.

## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Subcontracting ↔ Deps — the consent rule

**Relationship**: 05.05.01 DT-02 established an absolute rule: a seller cannot substitute without the buyer's consent, because the buyer bought a named person's taste. 05.05.03 DT-01 then found the exception: that is true only when the purchase was **identity-based**. A buyer who booked "Rockfield's in-house engineer" or hired through an agency's channel did not choose a human and cannot demand one.

So the rule is conditional, and the condition is knowable at listing time: **is the seller of record a person or an entity?** Which is 05.01 Q-02, arriving from a second direction — first as "who is the payee", now as "who must consent". A question that determines both the money and the consent model in two unrelated sub-domains is architectural, not cosmetic. It is escalated as sub-domain Q-03 and 05.01 Q-02, and they should be answered together.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: as a buyer, protected where they chose a person; as a seller, free to delegate where they sold a capability.
- **Producer**: the heaviest delegator (editing, tuning and comping routinely go to specialists) and the most identity-based seller. Both sides of the rule at once.
- **Operator**: this is their shape — a studio sells a facility's service and assigns whoever is free. The rule is what makes that legitimate rather than a silent substitution.
- **Fan**: none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The engagement's counterparty. Both features mutate it; only the consent precondition differs.
2. **Trigger chain**: Substitution proposed → consent required? (depends on listing type) → approved → counterparty changes → escrow payee changes → credit subject changes. If consent is refused, it falls to cancellation (05.03.05) with fault on the seller.
3. **Permission intersection**: Yes, and it is the interesting one — the *listing's* type (person vs entity) determines whether the buyer holds a veto. A property set at listing time governs a right exercised months later.
4. **Notification fan-out**: For on-location work, the Operator must be told who is now coming (access, load-in). They are notified of an identity change in an engagement they cannot otherwise see.
5. **State transition conflict**: A substitution proposed while a delivery is in flight — the original delivers as the dep is approved. Precedence undefined; `[PENDING]`.

### CX-05: Fixers ↔ Subcontracting — one escrow release, many payees

**Relationship**: Both features independently arrived at the same fix for the same ancient failure. The fixer takes the buyer's money and the players wait (05.05.02 DT-03). The agency takes the commission and the player waits (05.05.03 edge cases). In both cases the workers' contract is with the intermediary, not the buyer, so their only leverage is to stop taking that person's calls — which is not leverage, it is attrition.

The platform can fix it because it is simultaneously the payment rail and the holder of every engagement in the chain: acceptance releases escrow and settles **all** the downstream engagements in the same transaction, with the intermediary's margin as the residual rather than the whole. The Payments cross-cut already describes exactly this shape — "a single payment routinely splits across a band, a producer, co-writers and an agent's commission".

This is genuinely new for session players and is one of the domain's better arguments for existing.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the contracted player, who today is last in the queue and first to be forgotten. This changes their position structurally rather than reputationally.
- **Producer**: as a fixer or assembler, loses the float they currently enjoy — which is a real cost to them and worth naming rather than pretending it is costless.
- **Operator**: as a brokering studio, same.
- **Fan**: none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The escrow balance is claimed by N+1 engagements. One release, many settlements — the ledger must treat this as one transaction, not a lump deposit followed by hopeful transfers.
2. **Trigger chain**: Buyer accepts → escrow releases → intermediary's margin and every worker's fee settle atomically. A partial settlement here is the old failure with extra steps.
3. **Permission intersection**: The intermediary controls the composition of the downstream engagements (who is in the section, who is rostered) but must not control their settlement. Separating those two is the whole mechanism.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Every downstream worker learns they have been paid, from the platform, without asking the fixer — which is the part that changes the relationship.
5. **State transition conflict**: A downstream engagement disputed while the buyer's acceptance fires. The buyer's release should not be held hostage to a dispute between the fixer and one player — but the disputed player's tranche must be. Needs a per-payee freeze rather than a whole-escrow freeze; `[PENDING]`.

### CX-04 / CX-06: Fixers ↔ Subcontracting ↔ Bundles — assembly vs delegation

**Relationship**: Three features that look like one and are not. The discriminator is **cardinality and direction**:

| Feature | Shape | Credits produced |
|---|---|---|
| Subcontracting | One job, passed on | 1 — to the delegate |
| Fixing | Many parallel jobs, assembled | N+1 — each player, plus the contracting role |
| Bundling | Many sequential jobs, chained | N+1 — each seller, plus the assembling role |

A fixer and an assembler are arguably the same role at different cardinality (CX-06): both are a party who assembles humans, is paid one fee, and takes an undisclosed margin. That the margin-disclosure question is *identical* across 05.05.02 Q-01, 05.05.03 Q-01 and 05.05.04 Q-03 is a signal — three features asking one question means the answer belongs at domain level, not in three feature files.

**Role scoping**: Musician and Producer occupy every role in this table, often within one week. Operator occupies only the delegation column. Fan, none.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: None between them — an engagement is one shape or another.
2. **Trigger chain**: Distinct per shape; the only shared trigger is escrow release (CX-05).
3. **Permission intersection**: The intermediary's authority to compose comes from domain 01's mandate model in the agency case, and from nothing at all in the fixer case (a fixer just knows people). That asymmetry is worth noting: the fixer's book is informal by nature and 01's mandate model may not fit it.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Shared — every worker in every shape must learn of their engagement and their settlement directly.
5. **State transition conflict**: None.

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 05.05.02 Fixers | 05.05.04 Bundles (as one feature) | Considered merging: both assemble humans for one fee. Rejected — a fixer's jobs are **parallel and interchangeable in time** (twelve players, one date, one room); a bundle's are **sequential and causally dependent** (nothing to master until something is mixed). The failure modes diverge completely: a fixer's under-contracted section is a problem before the date and fine after; a bundle's stalled stage blocks everything downstream indefinitely. One model would be wrong for one of them. |
| R-02 | 05.05.03 Subcontracting | 05.05.04 Bundles | No interaction. A bundle's sellers are independent parties in a chain, not delegates of each other. If an assembler *delegated* their own stage that would be 05.05.03 acting inside a bundle — a composition, not a cross-cut. |
| R-03 | 05.05.01 Deps | 05.05.02 Fixers (fixer as dep-finder) | Considered whether a fixer is just a professional dep-finder. Rejected — a dep replaces **one** person on an engagement that already exists with terms and escrow; a fixer assembles **many** for an engagement that does not exist yet. The overlap is that both are people with a good phone book, which is a fact about the profession, not a shared mechanism. |

## Cross-Cut Mechanisms This Sub-Domain Consumes

| Mechanism | Used for | Note |
|---|---|---|

| **Roles, Permissions & Delegated Authority (Enforcement)** | An agency acting for a player at every call site | Domain 01 owns the mandate; this cross-cut enforces it. The seam matters — an agency listing someone who never agreed is blocked by 01, not by a check here. |

| **Availability, Scheduling & Reservation** | A dep must actually be free; twelve calendars plus a room on one date | Ensembles are the heaviest scheduling load in the domain. |

| **Canonical Data, Taxonomy & Entity Resolution** | Resolving off-platform players to real humans if they later claim an account | The off-platform gap (05.05.01 Q-02, 05.05.02 Q-03) touches this and domain 02's cold-start seeding. |
