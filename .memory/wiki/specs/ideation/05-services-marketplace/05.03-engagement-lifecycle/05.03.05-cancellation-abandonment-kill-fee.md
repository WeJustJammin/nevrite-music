# Feature: Cancellation, Abandonment & Kill Fee

> **Number**: 05.03.05
> **Parent**: [Engagement Lifecycle](./05.03-engagement-lifecycle-index.md)
> **Status**: [DEEP]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16
> **Origin**: Deep Think addition — **not in the sweep candidate list**

## Overview

What happens when an engagement does not finish: a buyer walks, a seller ghosts, a project is shelved, or a job simply dies at the requirements gate and nobody says so. The candidate list specified escrow, milestones, revisions and acceptance but never an exit — and an escrow with no cancellation rule is not an escrow, it is a pot of money with a support ticket attached.

Kill fees are standard practice in music and creative services. A producer who reserved three weeks for an album that got shelved in week one has lost three weeks either way.

The feature exists to make the exit **arithmetic instead of argument**. Everything it computes is computed from terms that were declared before the work started, because the one thing this feature knows for certain is that nothing agreed at the moment of cancellation will be agreed fairly — by then the relationship is already broken and the outcome goes to whoever has more leverage. That is the dynamic working musicians already live with, and it is the thing the platform exists to remove.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Either party can initiate. As a **seller**, sees a defensible number rather than a negotiation they will lose — the reason working musicians rarely get kill fees today is that asking for one, from a client who is already unhappy, costs the relationship and the next job. A rule that exists *before* the cancellation removes the ask. Specifically: sees the fee schedule on their own listing before publishing, the accruing fee figure on every live engagement's detail view, and the exact settlement arithmetic (not just a total) on any proposal. As a **buyer**, sees the current cost of walking away on the engagement view at all times — never revealed for the first time at the moment they click cancel (the same rule 05.06.03 D-05 applies to the rights transfer). As a **multi-hyphenate**, is routinely both: their buyer-side abandonment record and their seller-side reliability record are separate objects and never contaminate each other (D-15). |
| Producer | Full | The persona with the most exposure, because their engagements are the longest and reserve the most capacity. Also the persona whose own abandonment is the most damaging — a producer who ghosts mid-album leaves work that literally nobody else can finish (DT-03). And the persona most often caught **in the middle**: when a label shelves an album, the producer is the buyer of five session players whose engagements they must now cancel, owing downstream kill fees that nobody upstream is paying them (DT-05). They see the cascade explicitly — "cancelling this cancels 5 sub-engagements; your downstream exposure is £X against £Y recovered" — before they confirm, not after. |
| Operator | Read-only | Specific: the cancellation of an *on-location* engagement is visible against their room booking (domain 16), because their room has just been stranded. They see **the fact, the date, and which side cancelled** — never the fee, the scope, the deliverables or the settlement. Which side cancelled is the one settlement-adjacent fact they legitimately need, because it decides whether they are chasing the buyer or expecting the buyer to rebook. They are also shown, at booking time, that the seller's liability for their room is capped (D-13) — a warning before the loss, not a claim after it. |
| Fan | None | Not visible. A Fan never transacts in domain 05; services are sold to and by professionals. **Boundary note**: if a fan-commissioned service ever exists (a personalised song), it enters through domain 20 and would carry statutory consumer-protection obligations — cooling-off, non-waivable rights — that this feature's commercial kill-fee model does not have and must not be assumed to cover. See Q-05, which shows the same problem already reaches inside the professional set. |

## Behavior

Three distinct exits, and conflating them is the error:

| Exit | Who acts | Character | Fee? | Reputation fact? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cancellation** | A party affirmatively ends it | Deliberate. Has a settlement. | Per the declared schedule, by fault | Only if fault is assigned |
| **Abandonment** | Nobody acts | Silence. Needs a timeout, or it is eternal. | The schedule, applied automatically after notice | Yes — abandonment is a fact |
| **Mutual release** | Both agree | No fault. Usually a clean unwind. | None (D-14) | No |

The settlement question is **what has been consumed**, not what has been delivered. This is the fork from goods marketplaces, where an uncancelled order simply has not shipped. A producer who has held three weeks open has consumed three weeks whether or not a note of audio exists.

Milestones (05.03.02) make this tractable: accepted tranches are settled, the current stage is the only argument. Without milestones a long engagement's cancellation is a single unbounded negotiation, which is a strong argument for milestones being the default on longer work.

**Fault matters and must be assigned.** A cancellation caused by a buyer who never sent stems is not the same event as a seller who stopped answering — and 05.03.01 DT-01 established that the *common* case is buyer-caused, which is the opposite of the marketplace default assumption.

### The settlement has four legs, and they are independent

A settlement is not one number. Conflating the legs is how the arithmetic becomes an argument, so the proposal shows each one separately with its own basis:

| Leg | Basis | Take rate | Affected by fault? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Settled milestones** | Accepted tranches (05.03.02 D-01). Already released. | Already taken | No — settled is settled |
| **Kill fee** | The declared schedule × the unsettled engagement value | Normal | **Yes** — no fee where the seller is at fault (D-20) |
| **Expenses** | Money the seller has already spent (05.01.05 D-02) — travel, cartage, a hired amp | **Zero** (05.01.05 D-02) | **No** — an incurred expense is not compensation, it is a receipt |
| **Unconsumed escrow** | The remainder | n/a | No — always returns to the buyer |

Expenses reimbursing at 100% regardless of fault is deliberate and is the one leg that surprises people. A seller who cancels their own on-location date still spent £180 on a flight they cannot refund; making fault decide that leg would mean the platform confiscating a receipt as a penalty, which is a fine, and the platform does not fine people.

### The default kill-fee schedule is per scarcity model, not platform-wide

There is no single schedule, because there is no single scarcity. 05.01.07 D-01 established that remote work is scarce by **concurrency** (N engagements in flight) while on-location work is scarce by **calendar** (a Saturday). Those are not the same commodity and one table cannot price both — a Saturday that cancels on Friday cannot be re-sold at any price, whereas a concurrency slot released on day 2 cost the seller almost nothing.

**Remote engagements (concurrency-scarce)** — fee as % of the *unsettled* engagement value, buyer-fault:

| Cancelled at | Fee | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-gate, ≤ 48h since the engagement was created | **0%** | Nothing consumed. The slot was released before it displaced anything. A fee here is a fee on a mistake. |
| Pre-gate, 48h – 14 days | **10%** | The seller has held one of a small number of concurrency slots (05.01.07 D-02: per human, per craft) for up to two weeks against material that never arrived. |
| Pre-gate, > 14 days | **25%** | Beyond two weeks the slot is no longer incidental — it is a measurable fraction of the seller's quarter, spent on an engagement that never started. This is the abandonment case's automatic outcome. |
| Post-gate, no delivery yet in the current stage | **25%** | Work has started. The clock is running (05.01.06). |
| Post-gate, ≥ 1 delivery in the current stage | **50%** | The seller has produced the thing. Half of it is not a compromise — it is the honest split between work done and work not yet reviewed. |
| Current stage delivered and in revision | **100% of the current stage** | The deliverable exists and the buyer has it. Cancelling here is walking away from a finished stage. |

**On-location engagements (calendar-scarce)** — fee as % of the *date's* value, buyer-fault:

| Cancelled at | Fee |
|---|---|
| > 30 days before the date | **0%** |
| 30 – 14 days | **25%** |
| 14 – 7 days | **50%** |
| < 7 days | **100%** |
| No-show, or same day | **100% + expenses** |

This mirrors the cancellation terms studios and session players already write by hand, and the justification is the Operator persona's own words in `meta/personas.md`: an unsold Tuesday is gone forever. A booked Saturday cancelled on Friday cannot be back-filled; a slot cancelled a month out can.

**Seller-fault, both models: zero.** There is no reverse kill fee (D-20). The seller's obligation is to find cover (05.05.01), not to pay — that is the industry's actual remedy and the reason 05.05.01 exists. Where cover fails or is refused, 05.05.01 D-03 routes to cancellation with fault on the seller, and the outcome is: full refund of unsettled escrow, expenses still reimbursed, capacity slot released, and a reputation fact.

The schedule above is the platform's **default**, applied to any listing whose seller declares nothing. Whether a seller may override it is Q-03.

### Rights disposition is a term, not a negotiation

The hardest question in the domain (DT-03, 05.03.02 Q-02) is who keeps the partial work. The mechanism is now specified even though the platform's preferred default remains a user decision (Q-02).

At cancellation, **no rights transfer executes**. This is not a policy choice — it follows deterministically from 05.03.02 D-01 (milestone acceptance releases the tranche and does not transfer rights) and 05.06.03 D-03 (the transfer composes only from frozen data at final acceptance). Final acceptance never happened, so nothing vested. The buyer holds delivered files with no licence to use them; the seller holds the session.

That default is indefensible on its own — which is why the quote declares a **rights disposition** alongside the kill-fee schedule (DT-04), chosen from a fixed menu:

| Disposition | What happens | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| **Clean unwind** | Seller refunds the tranches for stages whose output the buyer will not use; buyer destroys or returns the files; seller keeps everything including the session. | Work that is worthless without its author — an unfinished mix, raw multitracks printed through one chain. |
| **Licence-back** | Buyer keeps the accepted-stage output under a licence to finish it elsewhere; settled tranches stay settled; the seller's credit and name are severed from the finished result unless they elect otherwise (05.02.02). | Work that genuinely transfers — a tracked drum take, an edited comp, an arrangement. |
| **Buyout of the partial** | Buyer pays an agreed figure for the unfinished current stage and takes assignment of everything to date. | A shelved project the buyer may revive, or a change of producer mid-album. |

Where the quote declared nothing and the parties cannot agree, the outcome is **no transfer** and the disposition is contested through 24. The platform states this consequence plainly rather than inventing a tiebreak it cannot justify.

The disposition term is also, unobviously, the domain's anti-extortion mechanism — see DT-07's second half.

### Credits survive a cancellation

A cancelled engagement emits credits for its **accepted milestones**, at the elected visibility (05.02.02), defaulting to **private**. This supersedes the surface pass's D-06, and the reasoning is load-bearing enough to be its own hypothesis — **DT-11**.

A credit is a statement that a person did work. It is not a rights claim, and coupling the two — which 05.06.03 D-01 does at final acceptance — is incidental to the happy path rather than necessary. Domain principle D-08, "the credit follows the hands", does not have an exception for deals that fell over. The hands did the work.

### Abandonment: the clock, the notice, and what resets it

Silence must terminate, or the engagement is eternal and the seller's capacity leaks to zero. Two clocks, deliberately asymmetric, because the two silences mean different things.

**Buyer-side (stalled at the requirements gate — the modal death, 05.03.01 DT-01):**

| Day | Event | Who is told |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Gate opens; engagement is `Awaiting Requirements` | Buyer (checklist), seller ("you're not late") |
| 7 | First nudge — informational | Buyer only |
| 14 | Second nudge | **Both.** The seller must know their slot is being squatted (CX-01 synthesis Q4) — they may want to nudge personally, or offer to release the buyer. |
| 30 | **Abandonment Notice** — formal. States the exact settlement figure, the exact date it applies, and what single act stops it. | Both, on every channel the party has |
| 45 | **Auto-Close.** The pre-stated settlement applies. | Both |

45 days is longer than any credible "I got busy" and shorter than the point at which one squatted slot out of a ceiling of ~4 becomes a structural drag on the seller's throughput (05.01.07). The 15-day gap between notice and close survives a two-week tour, which is the single most common legitimate reason a working buyer goes quiet.

**The clock is reset only by the act being waited on — never by activity (D-10).** Supplying requirements resets it. A message saying "sorry, next week!" does not. This is the anti-gaming rule and it is the difference between a timeout that works and one that is unreachable: a buyer who sends a reassuring message every 29 days is the classic ghost, and treating evidence of life as compliance makes the timeout decorative.

The one legitimate escape is an **extension**: once per engagement, +30 days, requested by the responsible party, requiring the counterparty's acceptance, recorded in the engagement history. Once. A second extension request is a conversation, not a mechanism.

**Seller-side (has the material, clock running, stopped answering):**

| Trigger | Event |
|---|---|
| Due date passes with no delivery | Seller nudged; buyer told the engagement is late |
| Due date + 14 days, no delivery **and** no response | **Abandonment Notice** to both; cover offered to the buyer (05.05.01) |
| Due date + 21 days | **Auto-Close.** Full refund of unsettled escrow, expenses still reimbursed, slot released, reputation fact recorded, cover flow surfaced. |

Faster than the buyer clock, and the asymmetry is justified rather than punitive: the seller committed to a date, the date passed, and they are not answering — three unambiguous facts. The buyer-side case has no equivalent certainty, because a buyer stalled at the gate may not even understand that they owe something. Ambiguity buys time; unambiguity does not.

### Happy Path (such as it is)

1. Party opens the engagement's **Cancel** action. The action requires the same mandate that accepted the quote (D-19) — a band member without signing authority on domain 01's mandate scope cannot exit a commitment they could not have made.
2. System asks for a **reason**, selected from a structured list (buyer: *shelved · budget gone · changed direction · no longer needed · unhappy with progress*; seller: *cannot deliver · unavailable · scope deadlock · buyer unresponsive*) plus optional free text. Structured because a free-text-only reason is unanalysable, and 24 needs the pattern.
3. System computes and displays the **proposed settlement**, with each of the four legs itemised and its basis named — never a bare total. The declared rights disposition is shown as part of the proposal, not as a footnote.
4. If cancelling cascades to sub-engagements (05.05), the system shows the **downstream exposure** — how many, whose, and what it costs the initiator — **before** the confirm, not after (DT-05).
5. Initiator confirms. Engagement enters `Cancellation Proposed`. Any open change order (05.03.03) is voided — you cannot amend a scope you are exiting.
6. Counterparty is notified and has **7 days** to accept, counter, or dispute. Silence for 7 days = acceptance, on the same reasoning as auto-accept (05.04.01 D-03): a settlement that depends on the counterparty's attention forever is not a settlement.
7. Counterparty accepts → the settlement's money legs commit **atomically**: settled tranches confirmed, kill fee released, expenses reimbursed, unconsumed escrow returned.
8. Engagement enters `Cancelled`. Capacity slot released (05.01.07). Credits for accepted milestones emit at the elected visibility, defaulting to private (DT-11).
9. Reputation facts write per fault (D-15). No fault (mutual release) → no fact.
10. Both parties receive a plain-language confirmation naming every outcome explicitly — what moved, what did not transfer, what each party now holds, and what the rights disposition means in practice.
11. Counterparty disputes instead → domain 24; escrow frozen; nothing commits.

### Edge Cases / Failure Modes

| Scenario | What Happens | User Sees |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Buyer never supplies requirements, then goes quiet | Abandonment. 7/14/30-day notices, auto-close at day 45 with the pre-gate >14d row = **25%**. | "No stems since {date}. This engagement closes on {date}. {Seller} receives 25% (£{x}); £{y} returns to you. Upload your stems to stop this." |
| Seller stops responding mid-engagement | Ghosting. Due date +14 notice, auto-close at +21. Full refund + cover (05.05.01) + reputation fact. No reverse kill fee (D-20). | "No response since {date}." → cover flow with named alternates |
| Buyer cancels before work starts, after the gate passed | Kill fee applies — capacity was reserved and the clock started | Post-gate, no delivery → **25%** |
| Buyer cancels before the gate passed, within 48h | **0%** — nothing consumed. Q-01 resolved: a slot alone is not worth a fee until it has displaced something. | "No charge. Your £{x} returns in 3–5 days." |
| Buyer cancels pre-gate at day 20 | **25%** — the slot has been held for three weeks out of a ceiling of a few | "£{x} to {seller} for 20 days of held capacity; £{y} returns to you." |
| Cancellation after milestone 3 of 5 | Cleanest case: 3 tranches settled, stage 4 priced per the schedule, stage 5 returns whole | Per-tranche arithmetic, each leg named |
| **Who keeps the partial work?** | Deterministically: nothing transfers (05.03.02 D-01 + 05.06.03 D-03). The quote's declared **rights disposition** (clean unwind / licence-back / buyout) is what makes that outcome survivable. Undeclared + no agreement → contested via 24. | The disposition shown on the proposal in plain language: "You will not receive rights to the tracked stems. {Seller} keeps the session." |
| Points-only engagement cancelled | No escrow exists, so there is nothing to draw a fee from. The settlement is a **rights disposition only** (D-17) — either nothing vests (default) or the quote's declared partial-vest schedule applies. The platform does not manufacture a cash obligation it cannot enforce. | "This engagement had no escrow. Nothing vests. {Seller} keeps the work." |
| Points-only cancelled where the quote declared a partial vest | The vest executes into 09/10 — and the buyer now holds a master permanently co-owned by someone who did not finish it and is not in the room (DT-09) | The instrument, plus an explicit warning naming the permanence |
| On-location cancellation strands the buyer's non-refundable room | The seller's liability is **capped at the engagement value** (D-13). A £400 drummer does not owe a £2,000 studio they never saw and never agreed to. The room's shape was already a required declaration (05.01.05 D-01); the platform's job is to warn at booking, not to reallocate the loss afterwards. | At booking: "{Seller}'s liability is capped at £400. Your room deposit is not covered." At cancellation: the cap, restated. |
| Quote declared a higher liability cap (a "date guarantee") | The declared cap governs. Real in artist contracts and priced accordingly. | Cap shown on the quote and on every settlement proposal |
| Cancellation to escape a change-order deadlock (05.03.03 Q-03) | A legitimate exit, and the schedule must not punish the party in the right. The refused change order is recorded as the reason; the seller cancels at **seller-initiated, buyer-fault** — no fee to the buyer, but a full refund is not automatic either: work already delivered in the current stage settles at the schedule. | "You refused a change order and {seller} exited. Stage 2 was delivered — £{x} settles to them." |
| Both parties initiate cancellation at once | Not a race. If the two proposed settlements are **identical**, the system converts to **mutual release** automatically. If they differ, both are shown side by side and the counterparty accepts one or disputes. | "You both want out. Your proposals differ by £{x}." |
| Both parties want out, no fault | Mutual release; clean unwind; **no fee** (D-14); no reputation fact | One click; "no charge to either party" |
| Repeated mutual releases between the same two accounts, followed by nothing | Leakage pattern — the deal moved off-platform (`meta/personas.md` names this as the Musician's worst intentional behaviour). **Not** met with a fee (DT-08). It is a 24 signal. | Nothing to the users. A pattern object in 24. |
| Retainer cancelled mid-period, buyer-initiated | Not an engagement cancellation — an **arrangement** cancellation, and its unit of settlement is the period. Current period runs to its boundary; allowance stays drawable; no proration (D-18). The declared expiry rule (05.03.04 D-02) already decided who bears a quiet month. | "Your retainer ends {boundary date}. You have 4 mixes and 11 days left — use them." |
| Retainer cancelled mid-period, seller-initiated | Fault inverts. Pro-rata refund of the **unconsumed** allowance, because the buyer's forfeiture rule was priced against the buyer's own inaction, not the seller's withdrawal (D-18). | "{Seller} has ended the retainer. 4 unused mixes refund at £{x}." |
| Retainer cancelled with an engagement in flight | The in-flight engagement drew its unit; it completes. The arrangement ends at the boundary regardless. | "This mix finishes. No new work after {date}." |
| Seller delivers stage 4 as the cancellation lands | **Delivery wins** (D-16). Same precedence principle as CX-01's ("requirements supply must win") — the party did the thing being waited on. Stage 4 enters its normal acceptance/auto-accept path; the cancellation applies from stage 5. | "{Seller} delivered stage 4 before your cancellation. Review it — your cancellation now covers stage 5 only." |
| Buyer supplies requirements at the moment the abandonment timeout fires | Requirements supply wins (CX-01 synthesis Q5, already established). Gate passes; auto-close aborts; clock starts. | "Received. Your engagement is live — due {date}." |
| Seller has rejected requirements 4 times, then claims an abandonment fee | **The attack** (DT-07). The gate is a weapon if abandonment settles in the seller's favour: demand the impossible, reject everything, wait out 45 days, collect 25%. Repeated `Requirements Rejected` (05.03.01 D-03) **caps the fee at 0% and routes the auto-close to 24 instead** (D-12). | Seller: "Your rejections are under review." Buyer: full return pending review. |
| Buyer cancels, forfeits 10%, then re-quotes the same job at 60% | Extortion via the exit, and real — the producer has already done the work and may take 60% over nothing. The **rights disposition** is what defuses it: under *clean unwind* the seller keeps everything and 60% is just an offer they can refuse. Under *licence-back* the buyer has leverage — which is precisely why the disposition is a declared term rather than a default (DT-04, DT-07). | Nothing special. The defence is structural, not a warning. |
| Cancellation lands while a dispute is already open in 24 | Cancellation is unavailable. 24 owns the outcome; escrow is already frozen. | "This engagement is in dispute. {24} decides the outcome." |
| Cancellation lands while a change order is pending | The change order is voided at `Cancellation Proposed`. You cannot amend a scope you are exiting. | "Change order CO-2 was withdrawn." |
| The escrow split partially commits (payment provider failure) | The money legs commit atomically or not at all — same requirement as 05.06.03 D-01, different transaction. On failure nothing partial is ever left standing and the settlement is **never reported as complete** until every leg has committed; the platform must resolve the stuck settlement itself, not wait for either party to notice. Never a silent partial. *(The recovery mechanism — retry policy, escalation, freeze — is architecture, deferred to `/create-prd-architecture`.)* | "Settlement agreed. Funds moving — we'll confirm once every leg has settled." Never a false confirmation. |
| Nobody notices the settlement never paid | The failure nobody reports — the seller was not expecting the money and the buyer is glad it did not leave. Same class as 05.06.03 D-02's credit-emission leg, and same requirement: the platform must **detect and reconcile the unpaid settlement on its own**; it does not wait for a complaint. | Nothing. That is the problem, and why the platform must surface it unprompted. |
| Cancelling a producer's engagement cascades to 5 session players | The producer is the buyer downstream and owes 5 kill fees that nobody upstream is paying (DT-05). The system computes and **shows the full cascade before the confirm**, including net exposure. Who ultimately bears it is Q-06. | "Cancelling this cancels 5 sub-engagements. You owe £{x} downstream; you recover £{y}. Net: −£{z}." |
| A sub-engagement's seller finds out their job died from a notification, not their contractor | Correct and deliberate. CX-03 already requires the fan-out to reach downstream players. Cancellation reaching them **late** is the current-world failure. | "{Producer} has cancelled the project this session was part of. Your settlement: £{x}." |
| Buyer is a hobbyist, not a business | **Possibly a consumer** — and a contractual kill fee may be unenforceable against a consumer regardless of what the quote says. The personas file states the professional/hobbyist boundary is porous. Not resolvable by design. **Needs counsel** — Q-05, same shape and same routing as 05.04.01 Q-01. | The schedule renders and applies exactly as written — the behavior does not fork on the buyer's status, because the platform cannot detect it. Enforceability is downstream of counsel, not of design. |
| Buyer is a band entity (domain 01); one member cancels | Requires the mandate that accepted the quote (D-19). A member without it sees the action disabled with the reason. | "Only {names} can cancel this — they accepted the quote." |
| Engagement has an A&R third approver (sub-domain Q-03) | Out of scope until Q-03 resolves. If a third approver exists, whether they can cancel is undecided and must not be assumed. | n/a — flagged, not designed |
| Cancellation reason is abusive or defamatory free text | It is user content, shown to the counterparty and to 24, and goes through the platform's ordinary moderation surface (24). The **structured** reason is what the platform analyses; the free text is evidence, not data. | Standard report/moderate affordances |
| Buyer cancels 4 of 7 engagements across different sellers | Buyer-side reliability is a first-class, per-role reputation object (D-15, DT-06). Sellers see it **before accepting an order**, which is the single most useful fact a seller could have and which no music services marketplace shows today. | On the incoming order: "This buyer has abandoned 3 of 7 engagements." |
| That same person is a 5-star mixer | Their buyer record and their seller record never touch (D-15). Per-role reputation is already mandatory in this domain (domain CX, Reviews cross-cut) for exactly this reason. | Two records, two contexts, no bleed |
| Engagement cancelled after the platform's own outage caused the missed date | Not the seller's fault and not the buyer's. Fault has a third value — **none** — and it must be assignable, or every infrastructure failure becomes a reputation fact against a user. Routes to mutual-release arithmetic with no reputation consequence. | "This was our fault. No fee, no record against either of you." |

### States — the engagement's exit lifecycle

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| At Risk | Inactivity threshold crossed (day 7 buyer / due date passed seller) | Warning to the responsible party, before any consequence |
| Abandonment Notice | Day 30 (buyer) / due date +14 (seller) | Escalating notice with the exact settlement, the exact date, and the single act that stops it |
| Cancellation Proposed | Party initiates and confirms | Proposed settlement with its four legs itemised — arithmetic, not a number. 7-day counterparty clock. |
| Settlement Agreed | Counterparty accepts, or 7-day silence | Money legs commit atomically. Until every leg confirms, the user sees "funds moving" — never a false confirmation, and a failed commit is reconciled by the platform rather than left partial or waiting on a complaint. *(Recovery mechanics → `/create-prd-architecture`.)* |
| Auto-Closed | Timeout expired after notice | The pre-stated settlement applied. Distinct from `Cancelled` permanently — the difference between a decision and an expiry, the same distinction 05.04.01 D-04 makes for auto-accept, and for the same reason. |
| Cancelled | Settlement committed | Read-only; evidence retained; slot released; credits emitted at elected visibility |
| Mutually Released | Both parties agree, no fault | Clean unwind; no fee; no reputation fact |
| Disputed | Settlement contested, or D-12's rejection cap triggered | 24 owns it; escrow frozen; nothing commits |

### States — the settlement view

| State | Trigger | What User Sees |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Loading | Settlement proposal opened | Skeleton of the four-leg table. The **legs**, not a spinner — the shape of the answer is known before the numbers are. |
| Empty | Engagement is pre-gate, ≤48h, nothing consumed | "Nothing has been consumed. Cancelling costs you nothing and returns £{x}." Not an error state — a good outcome. |
| Populated | Settlement computed | Four legs itemised, each with its basis named; rights disposition in plain language; cascade exposure if any |
| Error | Settlement cannot be computed — expenses unreconciled, a milestone's state ambiguous, the quote's schedule unreadable | **Never a guessed number.** "We can't compute this settlement yet: {specific reason}. Nothing has been cancelled." The engagement does not move. A wrong number here is worse than no number, because a proposed settlement is an offer and the counterparty may accept it. |
| Partial | Multi-party engagement (05.05) where a downstream sub-engagement's settlement has not returned | The legs that are known, shown as known; the cascade shown as pending with a count. Never summed into a total that will change. |

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | **Escrow cannot be operated without a defined cancellation outcome — this is a precondition, not a feature** | The reasoning that created this node. The candidate list had escrow (candidate 13), milestones, revisions and acceptance, but no exit. Trace any stalled engagement forward: money is held, no release trigger will ever fire, and the only resolution is a human deciding. | ✅ CONFIRMED — and it is why this is a `must`, despite being an addition rather than a swept candidate. Without a cancellation rule every stalled engagement is a support ticket, resolved by whoever picks it up, inconsistently, with no precedent — which is both unscalable and *unfair*, because outcomes then depend on which party complains harder. That is the exact dynamic musicians already experience with labels and is the thing the platform is supposed to fix. A published, pre-agreed settlement rule is the product. |
| DT-02 | **Seller-side abandonment and buyer-side walk-away need different remedies, and the common case is buyer-caused** | Adversarial: what is the failure mode, and who actually causes it? Cross-referenced with 05.03.01 DT-01. | ✅ CONFIRMED — and the direction matters because it inverts the default. Marketplace design instinct treats the seller as the risk (they might not deliver) and builds buyer protection. Here, the modal dead engagement died because the **buyer** never sent their stems, having paid and then got busy. A settlement rule imported from a goods marketplace would refund that buyer in full and hand the seller nothing for three weeks of reserved capacity, systematically punishing sellers for buyers' inaction. Fault assignment is therefore not a nicety — it is what stops the platform being structurally unfair to the persona it exists for. |
| DT-03 | **Partial creative work is not portable, which makes "who keeps it" unanswerable by analogy** | Adversarial: what would a domain expert expect that nobody mentioned? Compare against generic freelance precedent. | ✅ CONFIRMED as a real problem — and **partially resolved at Step 5 by DT-04**. A half-built website transfers to another developer and keeps working. A half-produced album does not: the multitracks are printed through this producer's chain, comped to their taste, and the next producer will start again. So the standard freelance settlement ("you keep the deliverables you paid for") produces an outcome where the buyer holds assets they cannot use and the seller has lost control of unfinished work carrying their name and sound. Neither party wins and both feel robbed. What Step 5 establishes is that the question is unanswerable *as a platform default* but entirely answerable *as a per-deal term* — see DT-04. The residue is which default the platform proposes, which is a values decision (Q-02). |
| DT-04 | **The rights disposition on cancellation must be a declared term of the quote, for the identical reason the kill fee is (D-05)** | Adversarial, Step 5: why is D-05 true, and does its reasoning generalise? | ✅ CONFIRMED — and it converts the domain's hardest question from unanswerable to answerable. D-05 exists because a fee negotiated at the moment of cancellation is a fee the seller loses: the relationship is already broken, the buyer is already unhappy, and the outcome goes to whoever has more leverage rather than whoever is right. **Every word of that applies to the partial work**, and more forcefully, because the partial work is worth more than the fee and its disposition is permanent. The insight is that DT-03's question was being asked in the wrong place. There is no *platform* answer to "who keeps the partial work" — the honest answer differs per craft (raw multitracks are worthless without their author; an edited comp genuinely transfers) and per deal. But there is a *quote* answer, and the parties can give it cheaply on a day when they like each other. So the platform's job is not to adjudicate the question; it is to supply a small fixed menu (clean unwind / licence-back / buyout), force the choice before the work starts, and make the consequence visible throughout (05.06.03 D-05's principle, applied to the exit). The menu must be fixed and small: a free-text disposition is an unparseable clause that nobody reads and 24 cannot adjudicate. |
| DT-05 | **Cancellation cascades down the supply chain, and the party in the middle eats the difference** | Adversarial, Step 5: what breaks at the intersection with 05.05 (multi-party supply)? Trace a shelved album, which is the modal way music work dies. | ✅ CONFIRMED — and it is a genuine gap, not a restatement. The domain's CX file (CX-03) already requires that a cancellation's notification reach "any sub-contracted or fixer-contracted players downstream whose own engagements are now orphaned" — and then stops. Nobody specified **the money**. Trace it: a label shelves an album. The producer's engagement (where they are the seller) is cancelled by the label, and they collect the pre-gate or post-gate fee — say 25%. But the producer is simultaneously the *buyer* of five session players, and cancelling those five engagements makes the producer liable for five kill fees under this very schedule, at 25–100% depending on how far each had progressed. The producer can easily owe more downstream than they recovered upstream. They did nothing wrong, and the platform's own fairness rule bankrupts them. Three shapes are available and none is free: (a) fees are **composable** — an upstream cancellation is a no-fault trigger downstream, which protects the middle and hands the loss to the session players, who are the least powerful party and the exact persona the platform exists for; (b) **pass-through** — the upstream fee is apportioned across the downstream chain, which is fair in principle and produces a settlement that cannot be computed until every sub-engagement returns (hence the `Partial` settlement state); (c) the middle **bears it**, which is the status quo and is why producers refuse to sub-contract on shelved-risk projects. The choice is a product decision (Q-06). What is not optional is that the initiator **sees the cascade before they confirm** — that behaviour is specified now regardless of who ultimately pays, because a producer discovering their net exposure after clicking cancel is indefensible under any of the three. |
| DT-06 | **Buyer reliability is a first-class reputation object, and no music services marketplace has one** | Adversarial, Step 5: DT-02 says the buyer is the modal cause of failure. What is the consequence of taking that seriously? | ✅ CONFIRMED, and it is the cheapest high-value thing in this feature. Every incumbent — SoundBetter, Fiverr, Upwork — shows seller ratings, because they inherited the goods-marketplace assumption DT-02 refutes. So a seller decides whether to accept an order, hold a concurrency slot, and turn down other work **with no information about the person who will or will not send the stems**. This platform will know, as a byproduct of the abandonment mechanism it needs anyway: abandonment is a *fact*, not an opinion, which is exactly what the Reviews cross-cut says it wants ("fact-derived signals"). Two constraints follow. First, it must be **per-role** — the domain CX already makes per-role reputation mandatory here because "the multi-hyphenate's mixing rating must not contaminate their luthiery", and buyer-abandonment contaminating seller-reliability is the same error with higher stakes: one bad month as a client should not cost someone their session work. Second, it must be a **rate, not a count** — "3 abandonments" is meaningless without "of 40 engagements", and a raw count punishes volume, which punishes exactly the professional buyers the platform wants. |
| DT-07 | **The requirements gate becomes a weapon if abandonment auto-settles in the seller's favour** | Adversarial, Step 5: assume the seller is hostile. What does the abandonment mechanism hand them? | ✅ CONFIRMED as a live attack; the naive design ❌ REJECTED. The tempting rule is "the buyer abandoned, it's their fault, release the escrow to the seller." It is wrong, and the reason is that **fault here is asserted by the platform, not adjudicated**. The gate is controlled by the seller: they wrote the requirement template (05.01.01), and 05.03.01 D-03 gives them a first-class `Requirements Rejected` action that stops the clock and returns the ball. A hostile seller therefore has a complete recipe: demand an impossible template, reject whatever arrives, say nothing, wait 45 days, collect. 05.03.01 DT-02 already noticed that "a seller who rejects everything is a different problem (24)" — this is what that problem *pays*. Two consequences, both specified: the automatic outcome is the **schedule, never the full escrow** (D-11), so the ceiling on the attack is 25% rather than 100%; and **repeated rejections cap the fee at zero and route the auto-close to 24** (D-12) rather than executing it. The second half of this hypothesis is the mirror-image attack from the buyer: cancel cheaply pre-gate, then re-quote the same job at 60% knowing the seller has already done the work and would rather take 60% than nothing. That one is defused structurally rather than by rule — under a *clean unwind* disposition the seller keeps everything and the 60% offer is just an offer. Which is a second, independent argument for DT-04: the disposition term is not only a fairness device, it is the domain's anti-extortion mechanism, and neither party gets to pick it after the leverage exists. |
| DT-08 | Mutual release should carry a fee, to stop it being used to escape the platform's take rate | Step 5: the obvious defence against the leakage risk `meta/personas.md` names as the Musician's worst intentional behaviour. | ❌ REJECTED — the remedy is detection, not a fee, and the fee is actively harmful. The concern is real: two parties who "mutually release", get 100% back, and then do the same job over WhatsApp have used the platform as a free introduction service. But a fee on a no-fault unwind is a **fee on honesty**. The parties who mutually release are the ones behaving well — they talked, they agreed, they closed it cleanly, and they generated zero support cost. Charging them teaches them to do it silently instead, which converts a clean terminal state into an abandonment the platform must then chase for 45 days. The fee makes the platform's data worse and its costs higher in exchange for revenue it will not collect, because the leakers will simply stop using the exit. And it cannot even discriminate: the honest mutual release (a genuinely shelved project) and the dishonest one are identical at the moment they happen. They are only distinguishable **afterwards**, by the pattern — the same two accounts, repeatedly, with no subsequent engagements between them. That is a 24 pattern object, computed from facts the platform already has, costing the honest majority nothing. Detect, don't tax. |
| DT-09 | **A points-only cancellation cannot have a cash kill fee, so its settlement is necessarily a rights event — and a partial points vest is a permanent encumbrance the buyer can never clear** | Adversarial, Step 5: trace this feature through 05.01.03 DT-01's escrow-less deal shape. | ✅ CONFIRMED, and it is worse than it first looks. A points-only engagement holds **zero cash** (05.01.03 D-07: cash and rights are two ledgers and are never summed). So there is nothing to draw a fee from, and a kill fee here would be an unsecured debt the platform cannot collect — which is why D-17 refuses to manufacture one. That leaves the settlement as a rights event, and the only available instrument is a **partial vest**: "you cancelled after I produced 3 of 10 tracks, so 30% of my points vest." Follow it. Cash is proportionate because it is finite and it ends. A vested points share is **permanent, cannot be bought out without the holder's agreement, and travels with the master forever**. The buyer's price for walking away from a producer after three tracks is a co-owner on a record they will finish with someone else, for the life of copyright. That is not a fee — it is a title defect. And it is a title defect *this platform created*, which lands squarely on the thesis: the product exists so that chain of title is clean at source, and here is the cancellation mechanism minting exactly the encumbrance that domain 09 will meet at registration and domain 12 will meet at release, years later, when the record needs clearing and the minority holder is unreachable. Two consequences: the default must be that **nothing vests** on a points-only cancellation (the harsh but clean outcome, and the honest description of what spec work already is), and any declared partial-vest schedule must warn about permanence in plain language at the moment it is declared. This is also the strongest evidence yet for 05.01.03 Q-01's "require a minimum cash component" option — a points-only deal is not merely unprotected, its *exit* is structurally incapable of being fair, and no amount of design fixes that. |
| DT-10 | Activity should reset the abandonment clock — a buyer who responds is not abandoning | Step 5: the humane-looking rule. | ❌ REJECTED — it makes the timeout unreachable and it protects precisely the wrong behaviour. The abandonment mechanism exists because silence must terminate. But "silence" is the wrong variable: the buyer who is genuinely ghosting is almost never *silent* — they are apologetic. "So sorry, mad week, stems Friday I promise." That message every 29 days, forever, is the actual failure mode, and it is indistinguishable from good faith at any single point. A clock reset by activity is reset by that message, so the engagement never closes, the seller's slot never frees, and the mechanism does nothing. The rule must be that **the clock is reset only by the act being waited on** (D-10): supplying requirements resets it; talking about supplying requirements does not. This sounds harsh and is not — it is the same principle the domain has already reached twice from other directions (D-09: "no clock runs against a party until the counterparty has done their bit"), just pointed the other way, and the buyer keeps a genuine escape in the one-time 30-day extension, which requires the counterparty's agreement and is recorded. The difference between an extension and a reassuring message is that the extension is a *decision by both parties*, which is what the whole feature is trying to preserve. |
| DT-11 | **A cancelled engagement is where the platform's thesis and its mechanism come apart — and the shelved album is the single most common way music work disappears from a musician's record** | Adversarial, Step 5: trace the thesis (`meta/problem-statement.md` — provenance captured at the moment of creation) through this feature rather than through the happy path. | ✅ CONFIRMED, and it is the most important thing in this file. The thesis is that the facts of the work evaporate because no system is in the room when the work happens. Here, the system **was** in the room. It knows who tracked the drums, on what date, for which project, and the buyer accepted and paid for that milestone. And then the album got shelved — which is not an edge case, it is the single most common ending for recorded music work, and every working producer and session player has a list of records they played on that never came out. Under the mechanism as inherited, that engagement emits **no credit at all**, because 05.06.03 D-01 fires credit emission atomically with escrow release and rights execution at final acceptance, and final acceptance never happens. So the platform is present at exactly the moment its thesis says is unrepeatable, holds the fact, and discards it — reproducing the reconstruction problem it exists to solve, in its own database, on its own watch. The resolution is to see that the atomic coupling is **incidental, not necessary**. Those three legs co-occur at final acceptance because that is when all three become true at once; nothing makes credit *depend* on the rights transfer. A credit is a statement that a person did work. A rights transfer is a statement about who owns the result. The first is true whether or not the second ever happens, and domain principle D-08 — "the credit follows the hands" — has no exception for deals that fell over. So: a cancelled engagement emits credits for its **accepted milestones** as facts, decoupled from the transfer. Two guardrails keep this honest. It must emit at the **elected visibility** (05.02.02) and default to **private**, because a buyer may have real reasons not to publicise a shelved project, and a public credit on an unreleased record is a claim about something nobody can hear — the private fact is still verifiable on request and still counter-attestable (domain 02), which is the mechanism that makes it worth anything. And it must cover **accepted** milestones only: work the buyer confirmed and paid for is a fact, whereas work in flight when the deal died is a claim, and this platform does not record claims as facts (domain principle D-07). This supersedes the surface pass's D-06, which reached for the right outcome via a mechanism that does not exist — it protected credits from withdrawal, when the actual risk was that they were never emitted. |
| DT-12 | The seller's kill fee should cover the buyer's stranded non-refundable room (05.01.05 Q-02) | Step 5: the intuitive fairness answer to the on-location cross-domain question. | ❌ REJECTED as written, and the reasoning generalises into D-13. The intuition is sound — the seller cancelled, the room is wasted, someone should pay — but it makes the seller liable for a third party's cancellation policy they never saw and never agreed to. Run the numbers: a session drummer's £400 date, at a studio the buyer booked for £2,000 non-refundable. Unbounded consequential liability means a £400 engagement carries £2,000 of risk, and the rational response for every session player on the platform is to **stop accepting on-location work** — which deletes an entire category of the domain to solve a problem that has a cheaper answer. The cheaper answer is already in the domain: 05.01.05 D-01 makes the room's shape a **required declaration**, so the platform knows at booking time who booked the room and therefore who is exposed. Its job is to **warn before the loss**, not to reallocate it after: "this seller's liability is capped at £400; your room deposit is not covered" shown at the moment the buyer books the room, which is the only moment they can act on it. The general rule is D-13: liability is capped at the engagement value unless the quote declares otherwise. That is ordinary commercial practice (limitation of liability = contract value), and the escape hatch is real rather than theoretical — a declared higher cap is exactly what a "date guarantee" is in an artist's contract, and it gets priced. What survives as an open question is narrow and worth asking: should the platform **block** an on-location booking where the buyer's declared non-refundable exposure exceeds the seller's cap, or merely warn? (Q-04). |
| DT-13 | **A hobbyist buyer may be a consumer, in which case the kill-fee schedule may be unenforceable against them regardless of what the quote says** | Adversarial, Step 5: who is actually on the buyer side, and does the contract bind them? | ✅ CONFIRMED as a real exposure; **not resolvable by design — needs counsel (Q-05)**. The feature's entire architecture rests on a pre-agreed contractual term (D-05, DT-04), and that rests on both parties being able to agree it. `meta/personas.md` states the professional/hobbyist boundary is porous, and a hobbyist commissioning a mix of their bedroom demo is plausibly not acting for business purposes — which in the UK/EU (and the canonical domain is `wejamm.in`) engages consumer-protection law, where statutory rights are typically **non-waivable by contract**. If that applies, a signed kill-fee schedule may bind a label and not bind a bedroom artist, on identical listings, and the platform cannot tell which is which from the account. The honest position is that this is a legal question with a product consequence, not a product question — and the domain already has this exact shape and routing precedent in 05.04.01 Q-01 ("is a rights assignment executed by auto-accept enforceable? Needs counsel"), which is not a coincidence: both are cases where the domain's core mechanism depends on a contractual effect the platform asserts and a court might not grant. Recording it here without a fabricated answer. What is specified regardless: the schedule renders, displays and applies as written; enforceability is downstream of counsel; and `meta/constraints.md` already carries "Consumer protection / distance selling" as an unresolved compliance item, which this escalates from a goods concern to a services one. |

| D-12 | Repeated `Requirements Rejected` by the seller **caps their abandonment fee at zero** and routes the auto-close to 24 for review rather than executing it | See DT-07. 05.03.01 DT-02 already flagged the serial rejecter as a 24 problem; without this rule the abandonment mechanism *pays* them. |
| D-13 | A party's liability on cancellation is **capped at the engagement value** unless the quote declares a higher cap | See DT-12. Unbounded consequential liability on a £400 engagement makes sellers refuse on-location work entirely. A declared higher cap is a real instrument — that is what a date guarantee is. |
| D-14 | Mutual release is **free**. The off-platform leakage pattern is a 24 signal, not a fee | See DT-08. A fee on a no-fault unwind is a fee on honesty; it converts clean terminal states into abandonments the platform must chase for 45 days. Detect, don't tax. |
| D-15 | Buyer-side reliability is a **first-class, per-role reputation object**, expressed as a rate, and never contaminates the same human's seller reputation | See DT-06. Per-role is already mandatory in this domain; one bad month as a client must not cost someone their session work. A count punishes volume, which punishes professionals. |
| D-16 | **Delivery wins** a race with cancellation | Same precedence principle as CX-01's "requirements supply must win": the party did the thing being waited on. Resolves the precedence gap left open in CX-03's synthesis Q5. |
| D-17 | A points-only engagement's settlement is a **rights disposition, not a fee**. The platform does not manufacture a cash obligation it cannot enforce | See DT-09. There is no escrow to draw from; a kill fee here is an unsecured debt. Default: nothing vests. Any declared partial vest must warn about permanence at declaration. |
| D-18 | A mid-period retainer exit settles at the **period boundary** per the declared expiry rule; a seller-initiated exit refunds the unconsumed allowance pro-rata | Resolves CX-07. The unit of settlement is the period, not the work — 05.03.04 D-03: the retainer sold access and the seller held capacity for the whole period. Fault inverts it: the buyer's forfeiture rule was priced against the buyer's inaction, not the seller's withdrawal. |
| D-19 | The cancel action requires the **same mandate that accepted the quote** | Domain 01 owns mandate scope. You cannot exit a commitment you lacked the authority to make, and you cannot exit someone else's unless your mandate covers it. |
| D-20 | There is **no reverse kill fee**. A seller's fault produces refund + cover + a reputation fact, never a payment obligation | The seller's money is not held, so the obligation is uncollectable; and the industry's actual remedy for a seller who cannot make it is a dep (05.05.01), not a payment. A fee that cannot be collected teaches sellers to go off-platform. |
| D-21 | The settlement is presented as **four itemised legs with their bases named**, never as a total | A bare number is an argument. The arithmetic is the product — it is what a musician has never been shown before at the moment a job dies. |
| D-22 | Expenses reimburse at **100%, zero take rate, regardless of fault** | An incurred expense is a receipt, not compensation. Making fault decide it would mean the platform confiscating a receipt as a penalty, which is a fine. The platform does not fine people. |
