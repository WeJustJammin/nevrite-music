# Feature: Cancellation, Abandonment & Kill Fee

> **Number**: 05.03.05
> **Parent**: [Engagement Lifecycle](./05.03-engagement-lifecycle-index.md)
> **Status**: [SURFACE]
> **Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Overview

What happens when an engagement does not finish: a buyer walks, a seller ghosts, a project is shelved, or a job simply dies at the requirements gate and nobody says so. The candidate list specified escrow, milestones, revisions and acceptance but never an exit — and an escrow with no cancellation rule is not an escrow, it is a pot of money with a support ticket attached.

Kill fees are standard practice in music and creative services. A producer who reserved three weeks for an album that got shelved in week one has lost three weeks either way.

## Role Lens

| Role | Access | What They See/Do |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Musician | Full | Either party can initiate. As a seller, sees a defensible number rather than a negotiation they will lose — the reason working musicians rarely get kill fees today is that asking for one, from a client who is already unhappy, costs the relationship and the next job. A rule that exists *before* the cancellation removes the ask. As a buyer, sees what walking away costs before they do it. |
| Producer | Full | The persona with the most exposure, because their engagements are the longest and reserve the most capacity. Also the persona whose own abandonment is the most damaging — a producer who ghosts mid-album leaves work that literally nobody else can finish (see DT-03). |
| Operator | Read-only | Specific: the cancellation of an *on-location* engagement is visible against their room booking (domain 16), because their room has just been stranded. They see the fact and the date, not the settlement. Whether the engagement's kill fee covers their non-refundable room is a live cross-domain question (05.01.05 Q-02). |
| Fan | None | Not visible. |

## Behavior

Three distinct exits, and conflating them is the error:

| Exit | Who acts | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Cancellation** | A party affirmatively ends it | Deliberate. Has a settlement. |
| **Abandonment** | Nobody acts | Silence. Needs a timeout, or it is eternal. |
| **Mutual release** | Both agree | No fault. Usually a clean unwind. |

The settlement question is **what has been consumed**, not what has been delivered. This is the fork from goods marketplaces, where an uncancelled order simply has not shipped. A producer who has held three weeks open has consumed three weeks whether or not a note of audio exists.

Milestones (05.03.02) make this tractable: accepted tranches are settled, the current stage is the only argument. Without milestones a long engagement's cancellation is a single unbounded negotiation, which is a strong argument for milestones being the default on longer work.

**Fault matters and must be assigned.** A cancellation caused by a buyer who never sent stems is not the same event as a seller who stopped answering — and 05.03.01 DT-01 established that the *common* case is buyer-caused, which is the opposite of the marketplace default assumption.

### Happy Path (such as it is)

1. Party initiates cancellation with a reason
2. System computes a proposed settlement from the engagement's state: accepted milestones settled, kill fee per the quote's declared schedule, unconsumed escrow returned, expenses already incurred (05.01.05) reimbursed
3. Counterparty accepts the settlement → escrow splits accordingly; engagement enters `Cancelled`
4. Counterparty disputes → domain 24; escrow frozen
5. Capacity slot released (05.01.07); credits already earned on accepted milestones are **not** withdrawn

## Deep Think Annotations

| # | Hypothesis | Source | Outcome |
|---|-----------|--------|---------|
| DT-01 | **Escrow cannot be operated without a defined cancellation outcome — this is a precondition, not a feature** | The reasoning that created this node. The candidate list had escrow (candidate 13), milestones, revisions and acceptance, but no exit. Trace any stalled engagement forward: money is held, no release trigger will ever fire, and the only resolution is a human deciding. | ✅ CONFIRMED — and it is why this is a `must`, despite being an addition rather than a swept candidate. Without a cancellation rule every stalled engagement is a support ticket, resolved by whoever picks it up, inconsistently, with no precedent — which is both unscalable and *unfair*, because outcomes then depend on which party complains harder. That is the exact dynamic musicians already experience with labels and is the thing the platform is supposed to fix. A published, pre-agreed settlement rule is the product. |
| DT-02 | **Seller-side abandonment and buyer-side walk-away need different remedies, and the common case is buyer-caused** | Adversarial: what is the failure mode, and who actually causes it? Cross-referenced with 05.03.01 DT-01. | ✅ CONFIRMED — and the direction matters because it inverts the default. Marketplace design instinct treats the seller as the risk (they might not deliver) and builds buyer protection. Here, the modal dead engagement died because the **buyer** never sent their stems, having paid and then got busy. A settlement rule imported from a goods marketplace would refund that buyer in full and hand the seller nothing for three weeks of reserved capacity, systematically punishing sellers for buyers' inaction. Fault assignment is therefore not a nicety — it is what stops the platform being structurally unfair to the persona it exists for. |
| DT-03 | **Partial creative work is not portable, which makes "who keeps it" unanswerable by analogy** | Adversarial: what would a domain expert expect that nobody mentioned? Compare against generic freelance precedent. | ✅ CONFIRMED as a real, unresolved problem — flagged, not invented. A half-built website transfers to another developer and keeps working. A half-produced album does not: the multitracks are printed through this producer's chain, comped to their taste, and the next producer will start again. So the standard freelance settlement ("you keep the deliverables you paid for") produces an outcome where the buyer holds assets they cannot use and the seller has lost control of unfinished work carrying their name and sound. Neither party wins and both feel robbed. There may be no clean answer — plausible shapes include the buyer receiving the work only under a licence-back to finish elsewhere, or the seller retaining everything and refunding, or a negotiated buyout of the partial. This is a **product and values decision** (Q-02) and the strongest reason cancellation cannot be settled by importing another marketplace's rules. |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Context |
|---|----------|---------|
| D-01 | Cancellation, abandonment and mutual release are three distinct exits | Conflating them produces one blunt rule that is wrong for two of the three. |
| D-02 | The settlement is computed from what was **consumed**, not what was delivered | A producer who held three weeks consumed three weeks. This is the fork from goods marketplaces. |
| D-03 | Fault is assigned, and the common fault is the buyer's | See DT-02. A fault-blind rule imported from goods marketplaces is structurally unfair to sellers here. |
| D-04 | Abandonment has a timeout with escalating notice and a pre-stated automatic outcome | Silence must terminate. The notice is what makes the automatic outcome legitimate. |
| D-05 | The kill-fee schedule is declared on the quote, before the work starts | A fee negotiated at the moment of cancellation is a fee the seller loses. |
| D-06 | Credits earned on accepted milestones are not withdrawn on cancellation | A credit is a fact about work that happened. Cancellation is a commercial event, not a retraction of history. |
