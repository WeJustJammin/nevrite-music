## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Requirements Gate ↔ Cancellation

**Relationship**: These two features were specified independently and arrived at the same fact from opposite ends. The gate exists to stop the clock until the buyer delivers their side; cancellation exists to terminate engagements that never finish. The intersection is that **the gate is where engagements die**, so the gate's failure mode is not a stalled state — it is an abandonment, and it is the buyer's.

That fault direction inverts the default marketplace assumption. Goods and freelance marketplaces are built on the premise that the seller is the risk: they might not ship, might not deliver, might not be real. Buyer protection follows. Here the modal dead engagement is a buyer who paid, got busy, and never sent the stems — while the seller has a capacity slot locked (05.01.07) and money sitting in escrow they cannot touch.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: the seller side of this is where the unfairness lands. A rule imported from a goods marketplace refunds the buyer in full and hands the seller nothing for three weeks of reserved capacity.
- **Producer**: same exposure, larger, because their engagements reserve the most time.
- **Operator**: read-only visibility on the on-location case — their room is stranded by a cancellation from a domain they cannot see.
- **Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The engagement's terminal state. The gate cannot terminate anything (it only holds), and cancellation needs a fault input the gate is the only place to observe. Neither owns the outcome alone — the gate observes, cancellation decides.
2. **Trigger chain**: Inactivity at the gate → escalating notice → automatic settlement (05.03.05 D-04). The notice is a hard requirement, not a courtesy: an automatic outcome without prior warning is indistinguishable from the platform confiscating money.
3. **Permission intersection**: None; both parties can act. But the *right* to cancel without penalty depends on whose inaction caused the stall, which is a fault question, not a permission one.
4. **Notification fan-out**: The seller must be told their engagement is at risk even though it is the buyer's fault — they may want to nudge, or to offer to release the buyer.
5. **State transition conflict**: A buyer supplying requirements at the moment the abandonment timeout fires. The gate would pass and the engagement would auto-close simultaneously. Requirements supply must win — the buyer did the thing they were being chased for.

### CX-03: Milestones ↔ Cancellation

**Relationship**: Milestones are the only reason a mid-engagement exit has a defensible number. Without them, cancelling a £12,000 album production two-thirds through is a single unbounded negotiation over a subjective quantity of creative progress. With them, three tranches are settled facts and only the current stage is contested — the argument shrinks from the whole engagement to one stage.

This is a strong argument, discovered here rather than in either feature, for **milestones being the default on long engagements** rather than an option. The feature that looks like a cash-flow convenience is actually the domain's main dispute-reduction mechanism.

But it collides with 05.03.02 DT-01. Milestone acceptance releases the tranche and deliberately does **not** transfer rights. So a cancellation after milestone 3 leaves the buyer having paid for three stages and owning nothing, while the producer holds partial work carrying their sound that the buyer cannot use (05.03.05 DT-03). The arithmetic is tractable; the *asset* question is not.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician / Producer**: both sides feel robbed in the partial-work case, which is the signature of an unresolved design question rather than an unfair rule.
- **Operator / Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The partial work itself. Neither feature claims ownership of it, and the rights model (05.06) says the transfer has not happened. **Unresolved — 05.03.05 Q-02, escalated to the owner.**
2. **Trigger chain**: Cancellation → per-tranche settlement → escrow split. Deterministic for accepted tranches; contested for the in-flight stage.
3. **Permission intersection**: None.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Cancellation must reach every party with a stake, including any sub-contracted or fixer-contracted players downstream (05.05) whose own engagements are now orphaned.
5. **State transition conflict**: Cancelling while a milestone delivery is in flight — the seller delivers stage 4 as the buyer cancels. Whoever's event lands first determines whether stage 4 is settled. Needs a defined precedence rule; `[PENDING]`.

### CX-04: Revisions ↔ Recall

**Relationship**: The same buyer request — "can you change this?" — is two different objects depending on which side of acceptance it lands on, because acceptance is atomic and irreversible (05.06.03). Before: a revision, inside a bounded allowance, against unreleased escrow and unvested rights. After: a recall, in a bounded window, against work the buyer now owns and money the seller has been paid.

The boundary is sharp and non-negotiable, which is unusual in this domain — most of its boundaries are judgement calls. This one is a state fact.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician / Producer**: sellers experience the difference as obligation shape. A revision is owed. A recall is a bounded courtesy with a defined end, which is precisely what they lack today.
- **Operator / Fan**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The delivered artifact. A recall replaces it while preserving version history; the version history is what proves what was accepted, which matters because the accepted version is what the rights transfer attached to.
2. **Trigger chain**: Acceptance closes revisions and opens the window. One event, two consequences, atomic.
3. **Permission intersection**: After acceptance, on a work-for-hire posture, the buyer *owns* the artifact — so a recall is the seller working on the buyer's property. The rights and the labour have separated, which no state in the engagement model currently expresses.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Window expiry must warn both parties. Sellers forget windows are open; buyers forget they are closing.
5. **State transition conflict**: A revision request arriving at the instant auto-accept fires. 05.04.01 D-04 owns the deterministic boundary: a request at or before deadline plus 120 seconds wins; a later request is a recall. 05.03.03 D-10 applies that outcome to revision lifecycle state.

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 05.03.04 Retainers | 05.03.02 Milestones | Considered whether a retainer's periods are milestones. Rejected — they are opposites. Milestones stage **one** job's deliverables causally (nothing to mix until something is tracked); retainer periods are independent time boxes with no causal chain and no per-period deliverable. Modelling one as the other would produce a milestone chain where stage 4 can run without stage 3, which breaks the sequencing that gives milestones their meaning. |
| R-02 | 05.03.06 Recall | 05.03.02 Milestones | Considered a per-milestone recall window. Rejected — a recall exists because acceptance transferred rights and released money, and milestone acceptance deliberately does neither (05.03.02 DT-01). There is nothing to recall *from* at a milestone boundary; an unhappy buyer at stage 3 is simply in revision. |
| R-03 | 05.03.04 Retainers | 05.03.06 Recall | No interaction beyond the ordinary. A retained engagement's recall behaves like any other; the retainer changes who paid and when, not what a recall is. |
| R-04 | 05.03.01 Requirements Gate | 05.03.06 Recall | Considered whether a recall re-gates requirements. Rejected — the seller already has everything; a recall is work on material in hand. The only failure is the seller no longer having the *session* (05.03.06 DT-02), which is a retention problem, not a gating one. |
