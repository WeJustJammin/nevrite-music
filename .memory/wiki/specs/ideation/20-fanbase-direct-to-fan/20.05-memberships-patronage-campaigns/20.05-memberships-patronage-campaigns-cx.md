## Cross-Cut Details

### CX-01: Membership Tiers ↔ Exclusive Content Vault

**Relationship**: The tier is a promise; the vault is where the promise is kept. Every vault item declares the
minimum tier that opens it, and every tier is only as valuable as what is behind it. The failure mode is not
technical — it is that the artist stops filling the vault in month four and the membership silently becomes a
direct debit for nothing, which is how fans learn not to trust memberships.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: sets tiers, puts things in the vault, marks the minimum tier per item. Needs to be told, kindly and repeatedly, when the vault is going stale — because that is the actual churn driver, not price.
- **Fan**: sees everything, locked or open. **Locked items must be visible** — the value of a tier is legible only if you can see what you are not getting.
- **Producer / Operator**: no visibility.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Tier definitions are owned by `20.05.01`; item→tier mapping by `20.05.02`. Deleting a tier that vault items reference must not orphan them — it must force a decision about what happens to those items and to the members who hold that tier.
2. **Trigger chain**: Join → entitlement (CX-M24) → vault access. Lapse → access revoked. **What happens to items released while they were a member is the real question** — see CX-M24 and `20.05.02` DT-02. Revoking a track a fan legitimately had for a year feels like theft to them and like enforcement to us.
3. **Permission intersection**: This *is* the permission intersection. Tier ≥ item minimum.
4. **Notification fan-out**: New vault item → notify entitled members (CX-M07). This is the drumbeat that makes a membership feel alive; without it the vault is a folder nobody opens.
5. **State transition conflict**: A tier downgrade mid-cycle. Access until period end, then narrow — not immediately, because they paid for the period.

### CX-02: Crowdfunding ↔ Backer Funds & Fulfilment

**Relationship**: The campaign is the promise; the funds node is the guarantee. A campaign takes £30,000 from
1,200 fans against "we will make this record and send you one". The money must not be spendable at will
(index D-02) — but the artist genuinely needs it to *make the thing*, which is the whole point of raising it.
**That tension is the actual design problem**, and it is why this is two nodes rather than one: the campaign
optimises for raising, the funds node optimises for keeping the promise, and they must be allowed to disagree.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: raises, then draws against milestones rather than helping themselves. Will find this frustrating — and it is the frustration that protects them from becoming the next cautionary tale.
- **Fan**: backs with a credible expectation of either the record or their money.
- **Producer**: a payee if the campaign funds a work they are on ([20.04.04](../20.04-direct-to-fan-storefront/20.04.04-d2f-revenue-split-payout.md)).
- **Operator**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The campaign owns goals, rewards and backers; `20.05.05` owns the money and is authoritative over it. **The campaign cannot move money.** That separation is enforced structurally, not by permission.
2. **Trigger chain**: Back → funds held → milestone verified → tranche released → fulfilment → close. Failure at any milestone → refund path. Milestone **verification** is the unsolved part (index Q-02): self-attested is gameable, carrier-confirmed only works for physical, and nothing verifies "I made the record".
3. **Permission intersection**: The artist has no release permission at all. Nobody at the platform should either — an ops override is how policies die.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Backers are told at each milestone. Silence is what killed trust in music crowdfunding; the update cadence is a feature, not a courtesy.
5. **State transition conflict**: Campaign succeeds, artist vanishes. Funds held; refund after a timeout. **Design for artist disappearance** — it is common, it is often not malicious (illness, band collapse, life), and a policy that assumes good-faith completion fails exactly when it is needed.

### CX-03: Membership Tiers ↔ Virtual Fan Events

**Relationship**: An event gated by tier gives a membership a heartbeat. A vault is asynchronous and easy to
ignore; an event is an appointment, and appointments are what make a recurring payment feel like a
relationship rather than a subscription. It is also the cleanest tier benefit to produce: unlike the vault, it
does not require the artist to keep manufacturing exclusive content — they just have to turn up and play.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: "members get in free, everyone else pays £5." One scheduling act, one performance.
- **Fan**: the reason the tier is worth it.
- **Operator**: index Q-04 — a venue could run this, and a grassroots room streaming its own shows to a supporter scheme is a real and underserved product.
- **Producer**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: The tier is authoritative on access; the event holds its own access rule (members free, non-members ticketed via 19).
2. **Trigger chain**: Schedule → entitled members notified → access at showtime. If a member joins *during* the stream, they should get in immediately — that is a conversion moment, and making them wait wastes the only urgency this domain ever generates.
3. **Permission intersection**: Tier ≥ event minimum, **or** a ticket from 19. Two paths to one door, and they must not diverge — a ticket-holder and a member must have identical access.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Announce, remind, and go-live. The go-live push is the one that matters, and it is the channel [20.03.02](../20.03-broadcast-fan-messaging/20.03.02-channel-routing-bulk-delivery.md) DT-02 says the declared web surface cannot reliably deliver on iOS.
5. **State transition conflict**: Membership lapses mid-stream. Do not eject someone from a gig they are watching. Grace to the end of the event — the humane answer is also the obviously correct one.

### CX-04: Crowdfunding ↔ Membership Tiers

**Relationship**: Reward tiers and membership tiers look identical (a ladder of price → benefit) and are
fundamentally different objects: a reward is a **one-off promise with a delivery obligation**; a membership is
a **recurring entitlement with no delivery date**. Building one from the other means either memberships
inherit fulfilment obligations they do not have, or rewards inherit an entitlement model that never completes.

Beyond the modelling, there is a strategy question the sweep never asked: **they compete for the same fan's
money** (index Q-03). A fan paying £10/month may not also back a £50 campaign, and an artist running both may
simply be moving money between their own pockets while feeling twice as busy.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: may not realise they are cannibalising. The platform can see it — it holds both sides.
- **Fan**: finite budget, infinite affection.
- **Producer / Operator**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**:
1. **Shared state conflict**: Distinct objects. Shared *shape* (a tier ladder), not shared state.
2. **Trigger chain**: A campaign reward may **grant** a membership ("back at £50, get a year of membership") — legitimate, and it means a campaign can create recurring entitlements with a funding obligation attached. Deliberate, not accidental.
3. **Permission intersection**: None.
4. **Notification fan-out**: Separate streams. A backer is not a member unless the reward made them one.
5. **State transition conflict**: None technically. The conflict is economic and it belongs to the owner (index Q-03).

### CX-05: Tipping ↔ Virtual Fan Events

**Relationship**: Tipping during a live stream converts far better than a tip jar on a profile, for the same
reason the merch table outsells the online store: the artist is *right there* and just did something. It is
the digital equivalent of throwing money at a stage.

**Role scoping**:
- **Musician**: sees tips arrive live. Also has to decide whether to acknowledge them mid-song, which is a real performance problem the tool creates.
- **Fan**: a way to say thank you at the moment of feeling it.
- **Operator / Producer**: not affected.

**Synthesis questions answered**: Deferred — Medium confidence. The mechanic is heavily precedented on
Twitch/TikTok and unproven for this audience. It also imports a culture (tip-spam, name-on-screen status
games) that may be actively wrong for a listening party. See [20.05.03](./20.05.03-tipping-micro-patronage.md) DT-03.

## Rejected Pairs

| # | Source | Target | Reason for Rejection |
|---|--------|--------|---------------------|
| R-01 | 20.05.03 Tipping | 20.05.01 Membership Tiers | Tempting — "a tip is a one-off membership" — and wrong. A tip buys nothing and entitles nothing; that is precisely its appeal to a fan who does not want a relationship, just to say thanks. Giving tips entitlements makes them micro-memberships and destroys the thing that makes them work: zero commitment on both sides. No shared state, no trigger. |
| R-02 | 20.05.02 Exclusive Content Vault | 20.05.04 Crowdfunding | Considered: campaign backers often get exclusive content as a reward, so surely vault ↔ crowdfunding? But the reward is *delivered as an entitlement*, and entitlements come from tiers (CX-04 → CX-01). The path exists and is already modelled; a direct pair would double-count it. |
| R-03 | 20.05.05 Backer Funds | 20.05.06 Virtual Fan Events | Independent lifecycles. An event is delivered the moment it happens — no supply chain, no milestone, nothing to escrow. The whole reason backer funds exist is the gap between payment and delivery, and a livestream has no gap. |
